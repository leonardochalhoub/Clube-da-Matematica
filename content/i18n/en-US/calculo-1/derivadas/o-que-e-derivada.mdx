<Porta nivel="formal" titulo="Formal definition of the derivative">

<Definicao titulo="Derivative at a point">

Let $f: I \subseteq \R \to \R$ and $a \in I$ be an interior point. The **derivative** of $f$ at $a$ is, if it exists:

<Equation numero="1" explicacao={<>It is a <strong>limit</strong> — not a simple division. <Eq>{`h`}</Eq> approaches zero, and the ratio between the change in <Eq>{`f`}</Eq> and the change in <Eq>{`x`}</Eq> tends toward a number. That number is the derivative.</>}>
{`f'(a) = \\lim_{h \\to 0} \\frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h}`}
</Equation>

</Definicao>

The derivative as a **function**: $f': x \mapsto f'(x)$. For the function viewed as an object, Leibniz's notation $\diff{f}{x}$ or $\diff{y}{x}$ applies.

### Fundamental interpretations

1. **Geometric**: $f'(a)$ is the slope of the tangent line to the graph of $f$ at $(a, f(a))$.
2. **Physical**: if $s(t)$ is position, $s'(t)$ is instantaneous velocity.
3. **Marginal**: if $C(q)$ is total cost, $C'(q)$ is the cost to produce one additional unit.

### Essential rules

<Equation numero="2" explicacao="Product rule. Not trivial — two functions vary simultaneously, but each one's contribution separates.">
{`(f \\cdot g)'(x) = f'(x)\\, g(x) + f(x)\\, g'(x)`}
</Equation>

<Equation numero="3" explicacao="Quotient rule — derivative of division.">
{`\\left(\\frac{f}{g}\\right)'(x) = \\frac{f'(x)\\,g(x) - f(x)\\,g'(x)}{g(x)^2}`}
</Equation>

<Equation numero="4" explicacao="Chain rule. In statistics + ML it is literally the heart of backpropagation: the derivative of a composite function is the product of the derivatives.">
{`(f \\circ g)'(x) = f'(g(x)) \\cdot g'(x)`}
</Equation>

### Notable derivatives

<Equation numero="5" explicacao={<>The derivative of <Eq>{`\\e^x`}</Eq> is itself. <strong>It is the only function (up to a constant) with this property</strong>. That is why <Eq>{`\\e`}</Eq> is the most special number in calculus.</>}>
{`\\diff{\\e^x}{x} = \\e^x`}
</Equation>

<Equation numero="6" explicacao={<>The derivative of the natural logarithm is <Eq>{`1/x`}</Eq>. That is why <Eq>{`\\ln`}</Eq> appears in almost every integral involving division.</>}>
{`\\diff{\\ln x}{x} = \\frac{1}{x}`}
</Equation>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

You are in a toy car sliding down a ramp.

**Speed** is how fast you are going right now. If you are going slowly, your speed is small. If you are going super fast, your speed is big.

The **derivative** is just a fancy name for that: how fast things are changing right now, at any moment, with any thing.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

You are on a bicycle going down a street. You look at the speedometer: it reads **20 km/h**.

That 20 is your **speed at this instant**. It is not the average speed of the whole trip — it is just right now.

How does the speedometer know? It measures how much the wheel rotates in a **tiny, tiny bit of time** and calculates.

The **derivative** is exactly that: it takes something that changes (the position of your bicycle), looks at a very small piece of time, and calculates the speed *at that instant*.

Everything that changes over time has a derivative:

- The height of a growing plant → its derivative is the growth rate
- The temperature of cooling coffee → its derivative is the cooling rate
- The money from your allowance being spent → its derivative is how much you spend per day

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old teen">

You throw a ball upward. Its height over time is roughly:

$$ h(t) = 20t - 5t^2 $$

(in meters, with $t$ in seconds)

**Question**: how fast is the ball rising at the instant $t = 1$ second?

If you use "average speed between $t=0$ and $t=1$": $\frac{h(1) - h(0)}{1 - 0} = 15$ m/s.

But that is the **average**! At the start it is faster, at the end it is slower. What is the *exact* speed at $t=1$?

The **derivative** answers that. It is defined as:

$$ h'(t) = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{h(t + \Delta t) - h(t)}{\Delta t} $$

For $h(t) = 20t - 5t^2$, after doing the calculation (derivative rules you will learn):

$$ h'(t) = 20 - 10t $$

At $t = 1$: $h'(1) = 20 - 10 = 10$ m/s.

The ball is rising at 10 m/s at that exact instant.

<Insight>

**The derivative is the Sherlock Holmes of calculus.** You give it a function, it tells you how it is changing *at that exact point*. Not the average of the trip. The instant.

In Physics 1, you will see that every law of motion uses the derivative:

- Velocity = derivative of position → $v(t) = s'(t)$
- Acceleration = derivative of velocity → $a(t) = v'(t)$
- Force = mass × acceleration → $F = m \cdot a$ → everything connected by derivatives

And in financial derivatives (which you will see in the Black-Scholes from Clube), the "**volatility**" and the "**delta**" of an option are derivatives.

Differentiating is feeling the pulse of anything that changes.

</Insight>

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

The derivative is the most fundamental concept of Calculus 1 — you use it in **EVERY course in engineering**.

### Where it appears concretely

| Discipline | Application of the derivative |
|---|---|
| Physics 1 | $v = ds/dt$, $a = dv/dt$, $F = m\cdot a$ |
| Physics 2 | $\omega = d\theta/dt$, $i = dq/dt$ |
| Physics 3 | Maxwell's equations (all have derivatives) |
| Thermodynamics | $C_p = (\partial H / \partial T)_p$ |
| Fluid Mechanics | $\rho \frac{Du}{Dt} = -\nabla p + \mu \nabla^2 u$ |
| Statistics | Probability density $f(x) = F'(x)$ |
| Optimization | $\nabla f(x^*) = 0$ at the critical point |
| Control | Transfer function $G(s) = \mathcal{L}\{g(t)\}$ uses derivatives |
| Solid Mechanics | $\eps = du/dx$ (strain = derivative of displacement) |

### Connection with integral

The derivative and the integral are **inverses** of each other. This is the **Fundamental Theorem of Calculus**:

$$ \int_a^b f'(x) \, \d x = f(b) - f(a) $$

In prose: integrating the derivative of $f$ from $a$ to $b$ recovers $f$.

### Automatic differentiation (autodiff)

On a GPU running neural network training, **every derivative computed is symbolic**. PyTorch, JAX, TensorFlow have auto-differentiation engines — they implement the chain rule at runtime over computational graphs. Learning the chain rule properly is a prerequisite for understanding deep learning.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

The derivative is not just a concept — it is a **computational architecture**.

### Forms of differentiation in production

| Form | Where it appears | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| **Symbolic** | Computer algebra (SymPy, Mathematica) | Expensive, exact |
| **Numerical** (finite difference) | Simulation libraries, debugging | $O(n)$, error $O(h)$ |
| **Forward-mode AD** | Sensitivity analysis, few inputs problem | $O(n_{\text{inputs}})$ |
| **Reverse-mode AD** | ML, optimization (few outputs, many inputs) | $O(n_{\text{outputs}})$ |

For a neural network with $10^9$ parameters and 1 scalar loss: **reverse-mode AD** costs the same as one forward pass. That is why backprop is viable.

### Production pitfalls

- **Numerical derivative in float**: truncation error + catastrophic cancellation. Use central difference with $h \approx \eps_{\text{mach}}^{1/3}$, not overly small $h$.
- **AD in code with if/else**: the path taken defines the graph. Changing branches during training can cause subtle bugs. JAX `lax.cond` handles this explicitly.
- **Higher-order derivatives**: Hessians cost much more. In deep learning, use **Hessian-vector products** via double backprop.

### Real application: vega of an options portfolio

Vega = derivative of portfolio price with respect to volatility. The derivatives desk computes vega for **the entire curve of strikes and maturities** — hence the vol surface, exposures by bucket, vol hedge via other options.

Without the derivative there is no modern risk management. Anyone trading derivatives without truly understanding the derivative is a sophisticated gambler, not a professional.
