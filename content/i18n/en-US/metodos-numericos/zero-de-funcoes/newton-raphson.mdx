<Porta nivel="formal" titulo="Rigorous definition">

Given $f \in C^2([a,b])$ and an initial guess $x_0$ close to the root $\xi$ with $f'(\xi) \neq 0$, the Newton-Raphson iteration is:

<Equation numero="1" explicacao={<>Each iteration takes the tangent to the curve at the current point and follows it until it crosses the <Eq>{`x`}</Eq>-axis. That crossing is the next guess.</>}>
{`x_{n+1} = x_n - \\frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)}`}
</Equation>

<Teorema titulo="Quadratic convergence">

Under the hypotheses above, the sequence $(x_n)$ converges to $\xi$ and there exists $C > 0$ such that:

<Equation numero="2" explicacao={<>The error is <strong>squared</strong> at each iteration — the number of correct decimal places <strong>doubles</strong>. 4 places → 8 places → 16 places. Reasonable and impressive.</>}>
{`|x_{n+1} - \\xi| \\leq C \\cdot |x_n - \\xi|^2`}
</Equation>

</Teorema>

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

Imagine a long playground slide.

You are at the top of the slide and want to know **where it touches the ground**.

Instead of sliding down slowly, you **look at the slide's tip very straight** and guess where it's going to stop — where its line hits the ground.

Then you walk to that spot, climb up a little on the slide that keeps going from there, and look again. But now you're much closer to the end.

Newton works like that: looks at the slope, draws a line to the ground, goes to that spot. Very fast.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

Bisection (from the other topic) looked for roots by **dividing the interval in two**. Slow, but guaranteed.

Newton-Raphson has a different strategy: **uses the slope of the function** to take a big jump toward the root.

Imagine the function drawn on a graph. You're at a point. You draw **the tangent** (the line that touches the curve at that point without crossing). Where does that line cross the horizontal axis? That's your next guess.

Each jump gets you much closer than bisection. Like a rocket versus a bicycle. But if you aim badly, the rocket can throw you **the wrong way**.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old teen">

You want to find the root of:

$$ f(x) = x^3 - 2x - 5 $$

Starting with $x_0 = 2$:

- $f(2) = 8 - 4 - 5 = -1$
- $f'(x) = 3x^2 - 2$, so $f'(2) = 10$
- $x_1 = 2 - (-1)/10 = 2.1$

Iteration 2:
- $f(2.1) = 9.261 - 4.2 - 5 = 0.061$
- $f'(2.1) = 13.23$
- $x_2 = 2.1 - 0.061/13.23 = 2.0954$

Iteration 3:
- $f(2.0954) \approx 0.00018$
- $x_3 \approx 2.09455148$

In **3 iterations**, you have 8 correct decimal places. Bisection would need more than 25 iterations for the same precision.

<Insight>

**Why is Newton so much faster?** Because it uses **more information**: besides the value of $f$, it uses the derivative $f'$. The derivative says "where is the function going". Bisection only knows "there's still a root in here". Whoever knows more walks faster — as long as they don't trust too much.

</Insight>

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

Newton-Raphson is the most used root-finding method in engineering, **within its envelope of applicability**. It has three classic pitfalls:

### Pitfall 1: zero derivative

If $f'(x_n) = 0$, the iteration breaks (division by zero). This happens at minima/maxima of the function. Practical solution: use **protection rule** — if $|f'(x_n)| < \delta$, fall back to bisection.

### Pitfall 2: cycle

Consider $f(x) = x^3 - 2x + 2$. With $x_0 = 0$, you get $x_1 = 1$, $x_2 = 0$, $x_3 = 1$, ... — eternal cycle between 0 and 1.

### Pitfall 3: divergence

For $f(x) = \arctan(x)$ with $x_0$ far from the root $\xi = 0$, Newton diverges. Each step pushes further away.

### Brent's method (in the real world)

That's why production code (`scipy.optimize.brentq`, MATLAB `fzero`) uses **hybrid**: Newton or inverse interpolation when the step falls within bracketing; bisection when it doesn't. Guarantee plus speed.

### Real application: implied volatility

The implied volatility of the options market is solved by Newton-Raphson over Black-Scholes. Since $\partial C/\partial \sigma > 0$ always (positive vega), the function is monotonic and Newton converges in ~3-5 iterations with tolerance $10^{-8}$.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

On a derivatives desk, Newton-Raphson over $\sigma \mapsto \text{BS}(S, K, r, \sigma, T) - C_{\text{mkt}}$ is the engine that extracts implied vol from the bid-ask book in **microseconds**. Every quote update recalculates vol, updates the surface.

But the pure version of Newton is not what's in production. What is:

- **Halley method** (order 3) — even faster if you have the second derivative (and BS does)
- **Brent** — when you need robustness (e.g., option almost deep-OTM, vega tending to zero)
- **Bisection** — fallback when everything else fails

In quant teams, "which root finder to use" is a decision by **asset class**:

- Listed equity options: Brent (bid-ask spread creates noise)
- Complex OTC swaps: Newton with warm-start from previous iteration
- Exotics with Monte Carlo pricing: bisection (pricing is not differentiable)

The wrong choice doesn't crash the system but degrades latency — and in high-frequency, latency is P&L.
