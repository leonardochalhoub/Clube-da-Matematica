<Porta nivel="formal" titulo="Black-Scholes Model (1973)">

### Hypotheses

1. The price $S_t$ of the underlying asset follows a **geometric Brownian motion** under the risk-neutral measure $\Q$:

<Equation numero="1" audioTexto="Equation one. d S sub t, equals, r times S sub t, d t, plus sigma times S sub t, d W sub t. In words: the infinitesimal change in asset price equals the risk-free rate r times the price, times d t, plus volatility sigma times the price, times the increment of the standard Brownian motion W." explicacao={<>The asset price grows with expected rate <Eq>{`r`}</Eq> (the risk-free rate) and fluctuates with volatility <Eq>{`\\sigma`}</Eq>. <Eq>{`W_t`}</Eq> is a standard Brownian motion — infinitesimal random steps that accumulate into a continuous walk.</>}>
{`dS_t = r S_t \\, dt + \\sigma S_t \\, dW_t`}
</Equation>

2. **Complete market, frictionless**: no transaction costs, unlimited short positions, infinite divisibility.
3. **$r$ and $\sigma$ constant** along $[0, T]$.
4. **No dividends** (relaxable: replace $r$ with $r - q$).
5. **Absence of arbitrage** — fundamental principle.

### The Black-Scholes PDE

By Itô's lemma applied to $V(S, t)$ and construction of the replicating portfolio (long $\Delta$ shares, short 1 option), risk is eliminated and the portfolio must earn $r$. Thus:

<Equation numero="2" audioTexto="Equation two. Partial derivative of V with respect to time, plus one half sigma squared times S squared times the second partial derivative of V with respect to S, plus r times S times the partial derivative of V with respect to S, minus r times V, equals zero. It is the Black-Scholes partial differential equation — a parabolic equation analogous to the heat equation of physics." explicacao={<>It is a <strong>parabolic PDE</strong> — analogous to the heat equation in physics! The term <Eq>{`\\frac{1}{2}\\sigma^2 S^2 V_{SS}`}</Eq> is the "heat" diffusing, <Eq>{`r S V_S`}</Eq> is a drift, and <Eq>{`-rV`}</Eq> is discounting. All intuition from the physics of heat applies here.</>}>
{`\\pdiff{V}{t} + \\frac{1}{2}\\sigma^2 S^2 \\pdiff{^2 V}{S^2} + r S \\pdiff{V}{S} - r V = 0`}
</Equation>

with final condition $V(S, T) = \max(S - K, 0)$ for a European call.

### Closed-form solution

<Equation numero="3" audioTexto="Equation three. C of S and t, equals, S times N of d one, minus K times, e raised to minus r times T minus t, times N of d two. C is the price of the European call, S is the current price of the asset, K is the strike, r is the risk-free rate, T minus t is the time to expiration, and N is the cumulative distribution function of the standard normal." explicacao={<>The call price is the <strong>risk-neutral expected value of the payoff</strong>. <Eq>{`N(d_2)`}</Eq> is the probability the call finishes in-the-money. <Eq>{`S \\cdot N(d_1)`}</Eq> is the expected value of <Eq>{`S_T`}</Eq> conditional on exercise.</>}>
{`C(S, t) = S \\cdot N(d_1) - K\\,\\e^{-r(T-t)} N(d_2)`}
</Equation>

where

<Equation numero="4" audioTexto="Equation four. d one, equals, in the numerator, natural logarithm of S over K, plus r plus one half sigma squared, times T minus t, divided by the denominator sigma times square root of T minus t. And d two, equals, d one minus sigma times square root of T minus t." explicacao={<>The <Eq>{`d_1`}</Eq> measures how many standard deviations the log-price is above the log-strike, adjusted for drift. <Eq>{`d_2 = d_1 - \\sigma\\sqrt{T-t}`}</Eq>.</>}>
{`d_1 = \\frac{\\ln(S/K) + (r + \\frac{1}{2}\\sigma^2)(T-t)}{\\sigma\\sqrt{T-t}}, \\quad d_2 = d_1 - \\sigma\\sqrt{T-t}`}
</Equation>

and $N(\cdot)$ is the cumulative distribution function of the standard normal:

<Equation numero="5" audioTexto="Equation five. N of x, equals, one over square root of two pi, times the integral from minus infinity to x, of e raised to minus u squared over two, d u. It is the cumulative distribution function of the standard normal — the area under Gauss's bell curve up to point x." explicacao={<>The famous <strong>bell curve</strong> of Gauss, integrated from minus infinity to <Eq>{`x`}</Eq>. It has no closed form — computed via rational approximations (Abramowitz & Stegun) or the complementary error function.</>}>
{`N(x) = \\frac{1}{\\sqrt{2\\pi}} \\int_{-\\infty}^{x} \\e^{-u^2/2} \\, du`}
</Equation>

### Put-call parity

<Equation numero="6" audioTexto="Equation six, put-call parity. C minus P, equals, S minus K times e raised to minus r times T minus t. C is the price of the call, P is the price of the put, same strike and same expiration." explicacao={<>Exact relationship between European calls and puts of the same strike and expiration. If this identity breaks in the market, there is pure arbitrage — in practice, violated by fractions of a penny for seconds.</>}>
{`C - P = S - K \\e^{-r(T-t)}`}
</Equation>

### The Greeks

Partial sensitivities of the option price. Every derivatives desk in the world monitors these numbers.

<Equation numero="7" audioTexto="Equation seven, the delta of the call. Delta sub C, equals, partial derivative of C with respect to S, equals, N of d one. It is the sensitivity of the option price with respect to the asset price." explicacao={<>Delta — <strong>how much the option price changes for each dollar move in the stock</strong>. For a deep-ITM call, <Eq>{`\\Delta \\to 1`}</Eq>; deep-OTM, <Eq>{`\\Delta \\to 0`}</Eq>.</>}>
{`\\Delta_C = \\pdiff{C}{S} = N(d_1)`}
</Equation>

<Equation numero="8" audioTexto="Equation eight, gamma. Gamma, equals, second partial derivative of C with respect to S, equals, N prime of d one, divided by S times sigma times square root of T minus t. It is convexity — how delta changes when the asset price changes." explicacao={<>Gamma — <strong>convexity</strong>: how delta changes when price changes. Same for call and put. Largest near the strike and near expiration.</>}>
{`\\Gamma = \\pdiff{^2 C}{S^2} = \\frac{N'(d_1)}{S \\sigma \\sqrt{T-t}}`}
</Equation>

<Equation numero="9" audioTexto="Equation nine, vega. Nu, equals, partial derivative of C with respect to sigma, equals, S times square root of T minus t, times N prime of d one. It measures the sensitivity of the option price to volatility." explicacao={<>Vega — sensitivity to volatility. <strong>Same for call and put</strong>. On the trading floor, implied vol is what you operate, not price directly.</>}>
{`\\nu = \\pdiff{C}{\\sigma} = S \\sqrt{T-t} \\cdot N'(d_1)`}
</Equation>

<Equation numero="10" audioTexto="Equation ten, theta. Theta sub C, equals, minus S times sigma times N prime of d one, divided by two times square root of T minus t, minus r times K times e raised to minus r times T minus t, times N of d two. It measures time decay — how much the option loses per day." explicacao={<>Theta — <strong>time decay</strong>: how much the option loses value per day, holding everything else constant. <Eq>{`\\Theta < 0`}</Eq> for long options — option is an asset that "melts".</>}>
{`\\Theta_C = -\\frac{S \\sigma N'(d_1)}{2\\sqrt{T-t}} - r K \\e^{-r(T-t)} N(d_2)`}
</Equation>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

You have **a friend** who has a super rare trading card from their collection.

Today, they promise you:

> *"Tomorrow, if you want, I'll give you my rare card. You just give me 10 of your cards in exchange. But you decide tomorrow. If you don't want to, that's okay."*

This **right to choose tomorrow** is worth something **today**, right?

- If their rare card is **really special**, it's worth more.
- If you have **plenty of time** to decide, it's worth more — it could get even cooler.
- If their cards tend to **become rarer quickly**, it's worth even more.

Black-Scholes is the **right formula** to know how much that right is worth.

It has a formula with Greek letters and everything. But the heart of it is just this: **the right to choose later is worth money today**.

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

You play a game on your phone. There's a legendary skin that costs **100 coins**. You only have 80.

A friend offers you a **magic coupon**:

> *"I'll sell you a coupon that gives you the right to buy the skin for 100 coins anytime in the next 7 days — even if the price goes up to 200 or 500."*

How much is that coupon worth?

It depends on **5 things**:

1. **Current price** of the skin (closer to the target, the more the coupon is worth).
2. **Target price** (100 coins).
3. **How many days** the coupon lasts (more days, more it's worth).
4. **Interest rate** (because saved money earns interest — small effect here).
5. **How much the skin price bounces around** — this is called "**volatility**". If the skin is unstable (goes up and down a lot), the coupon is worth **much more** — because there's a bigger chance it explodes upward and you make a huge profit.

The Black-Scholes formula takes these 5 ingredients and calculates the **fair price of the coupon**.

It's not a guess. It's math. And it was so important for the economy that it won [**the Nobel Prize** in 1997](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/1997/summary/).

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old teenager">

Let's imagine something concrete.

**Petrobras (PETR4)** stock is worth **R\$ 46.60** today (April 24, 2026, source B3). You think it will go up over the next 30 days.

You have two choices:

**Choice A**: Buy the stock. Costs R\$46.60 per share. If it rises to R\$55, you make R\$8.40. If it falls to R\$40, you lose R\$6.60.

**Choice B**: Buy a **call option** — the right (but not the obligation) to buy the stock at R\$47 in 30 days. This option costs about **R\$1.94**. If the stock rises to R\$55, you exercise: pay R\$47, sell at R\$55, profit R\$8 minus the R\$1.94 paid = **R\$6.06 profit on a R\$1.94 investment** (profit of ~312%). If the stock falls to R\$40, you don't exercise — you just lose the R\$1.94.

Why is an option so powerful? **Leverage** — you control R\$47 of stock with R\$1.94.

Why doesn't everyone use options? Because it **expires**. If in 30 days the stock is at R\$46.99 (1 cent below the strike), you don't exercise and lose **100% of what you paid**.

Black-Scholes's question is: **how much should this option cost, in fair theory?**

The formula combines:

$$ C = S \cdot N(d_1) - K \cdot \e^{-rT} \cdot N(d_2) $$

Don't be scared. The letters are:

- $S$: current stock price (R\$46.60)
- $K$: target exercise price, "strike" (R\$47.00)
- $r$: interest rate of the CDI (14.65% a.a. in April 2026)
- $T$: time to expiration, in years (30 days = 30/365 ≈ 0.082)
- $\sigma$: how much the stock normally bounces, "volatility" (~35% a.a. for PETR4)
- $N(\cdot)$: the famous **normal distribution** — the bell curve you'll see in statistics

Plugging in the numbers: the fair option costs about **R\$1.94**. That's exactly what the market prices it at.

<Insight>

**Why is this magic?** Black-Scholes says: given price, interest, vol, and time, the **fair price of the option is mathematically determined**. It's not opinion. It's not a guess. If the market sells it at R\$2.80, there's higher "implied vol" — the market is forecasting the stock will bounce more than 35%. If it sells at R\$1.30, the market forecasts less bouncing.

The formula extracts from the price what the market thinks. **That's really powerful.**

</Insight>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering / finance student">

You're in a **Master's in Finance** program, or **Financial Engineering** at Poli, or **Quant** interview prep. Black-Scholes is bread and butter.

### Origin: dynamic replication

The central intuition is **not the formula** — it's the no-arbitrage argument via **replicating portfolio**:

- Long $\Delta$ shares, short 1 call.
- By Itô's lemma, the $dV$ of the portfolio is risk-free if $\Delta = \partial C/\partial S$.
- Risk-free portfolio → earns $r$. Thus the PDE.

This argument is **fundamentally different** from "compute expected payoff and discount". Black-Scholes is, at heart, **an argument for continuous dynamic hedging**.

### Implied vol — what you actually trade

Derivatives desk doesn't trade "call price". It trades **implied volatility** — the $\sigma$ that, plugged into Black-Scholes, returns the observed price:

$$ \sigma_{\text{impl}}(C_{\text{mkt}}) = \text{BS}^{-1}(C_{\text{mkt}}, S, K, r, T) $$

Solved by **Newton-Raphson** (vega as derivative) or Brent. Use bisection as fallback if Newton diverges. It's one of the first real applications you see of "find the zero of a function" — exactly the topic of the bisection method on this site.

### Cross-domain: the heat equation

The Black-Scholes PDE (Eq. 2 in the formal door) is, **literally**, the heat equation in disguise. Change of variables $u(x, \tau) = \e^{\alpha x + \beta \tau} V(S, t)$ with $x = \ln S, \tau = T - t$ leads to:

$$ \pdiff{u}{\tau} = \pdiff{^2 u}{x^2} $$

All the intuition you have about **heat diffusion** applies to options — including solutions via Green's functions, Fourier transform, separation of variables. When a quant talks about "Black-Scholes kernel", it's the Green's function of the heat equation.

### Connections to other fields

- **Probability**: $N(d_2)$ is the risk-neutral probability the call finishes ITM.
- **Stochastic calculus**: Itô, martingales, change of measure (Girsanov).
- **Numerical methods**: solving via finite differences is an alternative to the closed form — useful for American and exotic options where there's no formula.
- **Software engineering**: implementation requires care with $T \to 0$ (singularity in $d_1$) and $\sigma \to 0$ (same). Classic pitfall.

### Complete numerical example (see Practice door for more)

PETR4 on 24/04/2026: $S = 46{,}60$, $K = 47{,}00$, $r = 14{,}65\%$, $\sigma = 35\%$, $T = 30/365$.

$$ d_1 = \frac{\ln(46{,}60/47) + (0{,}1465 + 0{,}35^2/2)(0{,}0822)}{0{,}35\sqrt{0{,}0822}} = \frac{0{,}00854}{0{,}1003} \approx 0{,}085 $$

$$ d_2 = 0{,}085 - 0{,}1003 \approx -0{,}015 $$

$$ N(d_1) \approx 0{,}534, \quad N(d_2) \approx 0{,}494 $$

$$ C = 46{,}60 \cdot 0{,}534 - 47 \cdot \e^{-0{,}012} \cdot 0{,}494 \approx 1{,}94 $$

⟹ **R\$ 1.94**

Greeks of the option:

- $\Delta = N(d_1) \approx 0{,}534$ → each R\$1 PETR4 rises, the option rises R\$0.53
- $\Gamma \approx 0{,}085$ → the $\Delta$ accelerates with price
- $\nu \approx 0{,}053$ per 1pp of vol → each 1pp of implied vol = R\$0.053
- $\Theta \approx -0{,}040$ R\$/day → ATM 30d option "melts" R\$0.04 per day

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

Black-Scholes is not the truth. It's the **lingua franca** that makes conversation possible between traders in Tokyo, London, New York, and São Paulo.

### Every hypothesis breaks under stress

| BS Hypothesis | Reality | How the market handles it |
|---|---|---|
| Log-normality | **Fat tails**. Real distributions have kurtosis >> 3. Crashes happen. | Smile/skew of implied vol; jump models (Merton, Kou). |
| $\sigma$ constant | Vol is **stochastic**. Smile + term + skew form a 3D surface. | Heston, SABR, rough Bergomi. |
| $r$ constant | Rates are stochastic. Important in long options. | Hull-White, HJM, multi-curve. |
| Complete market | Friction, bid-ask, regulatory capital, funding. | Funding-adjusted pricing (FVA), capital VA (KVA). |
| Continuous hedge | Hedge is discrete. Each rebalance costs. | Discrete delta-hedging + replica error analysis. |

### Cargo-cult versus proper use

LTCM fell in 1998 trusting models. Bear Stearns fell in 2008 too. Operational lesson:

1. **Calibration matters more than the model**. Who calibrates well with a bad model beats who calibrates poorly with the best model.
2. **Stress testing outside log-normality is mandatory**. Historical vol underestimates tail risk. Use scenarios with 3x vol, 30% jump, gap on open.
3. **Monitor Greeks on all aggregated positions**, not just per trade. Net portfolio $\Delta$, $\Gamma$, $\nu$, jump exposure.

### Implied volatility as a thermometer

The implied vol surface is the **thermometer of fear**. In calm periods, smile is gentle (~25% at the money, ~35% at extremes). In panic, vol jumps to 80%, 100% (2008 case, COVID 2020, COVID 2021 energy lockdown).

The VIX (S&P 500), VVIX (vol of vol), MOVE (vol of Treasuries) indices are all derived from this idea: extract market expectation from option prices.

### Real case: PETR4 smile April 2026

Estimated snapshot from B3 (see Practice door for detailed calculations):

| Strike (% Spot) | Implied vol |
|---|---|
| 80% | 47% |
| 90% | 41% |
| 100% (ATM) | 35% |
| 110% | 32% |
| 120% | 33% |

**Classic negative skew** for stocks: OTM puts (protection) cost more vol than OTM calls. The market pays a premium for downside protection — makes sense, because stock downside typically comes with vol exploding.

### Real cost of "hedging"

A position short 1,000 PETR4 calls strike R\$47 at 30 days needs continuous delta hedge. Daily costs:

- **Funding cost** of shares bought for delta-hedge: ~CDI × position value.
- **Execution cost** (bid-ask + impact): ~0.15% per rebalance.
- **Slippage** on high-vol days: can double execution cost.

Over 30 days, with daily rebalancing, hedge costs eat **2-4% of the premium received**. That's why MM of derivatives charges a spread over BS — it's not greed, it's **real hedge is expensive**.

### Modern alternatives

- **Heston** (1993) — stochastic vol with price-vol correlation. Captures skew better.
- **Local Vol (Dupire 1994)** — vol = deterministic function of $S$ and $t$. Calibrates exactly to smile.
- **SABR** (Hagan 2002) — benchmark on FX and rate desks.
- **Rough Bergomi** (Bayer-Friz-Gatheral 2016) — vol of vol roughly fractional Brownian. State-of-the-art in research.

But all of these **reduce to BS** when $\sigma$ is constant. BS is the **null model** everything is built on. Knowing BS correctly is prerequisite for knowing anything serious in quant finance.

</Porta>

### Scenario 3 — Long Put 90d · portfolio protection

**Thesis:** You hold a PETR4 long position (say 1,000 shares = R\$46,600) and want to **protect against sharp downside** in the next 90 days while keeping upside.

**Inputs:** $S=46{,}60$, $K=47{,}00$, $r=14{,}65\%$, $\sigma=35\%$, $T=90/365=0{,}2466$

**Calculation (put via parity):**

$$ d_1 = \frac{\ln(46{,}60/47) + 0{,}20775 \cdot 0{,}2466}{0{,}35\sqrt{0{,}2466}} = 0{,}246 $$

$$ d_2 = 0{,}246 - 0{,}174 = 0{,}072 $$

Call: $C = 46{,}60 \cdot N(0{,}246) - 47 \cdot \e^{-0{,}036} \cdot N(0{,}072) \approx 3{,}83$

Put (parity): $P = C - S + K \e^{-rT} = 3{,}83 - 46{,}60 + 47 \cdot 0{,}9645 \approx 2{,}57$ → **R\$ 2.57**

**Real operation (10 contracts = protection of 1,000 shares):**

| Item | Value |
|---|---|
| Premium paid | 1,000 × R\$2.57 = **R\$ 2,570.00** |
| Costs | ~R\$ 13.00 |
| **Total protection cost** | **R\$ 2,583.00** (~5.5% of portfolio value) |
| Guaranteed floor | $S_T = 47 \Rightarrow $ receive at least R\$47,000 + share gains |
| Protection cost annualized | ~22% a.a. |

**Comment:** **Cost of protection is high** because implied vol reflects downside fear (skew). Comparison:

- **No put**: falls to R\$35 → -R\$11,600
- **With put strike 47**: falls to R\$35 → -R\$11,600 + (47-35)·1,000 - 2,583 = +R\$ -2,183
- **Cost of "insurance"** if PETR4 goes sideways at R\$46.60: **R\$2,583 wasted**

Professional alternatives: **zero-cost collar** (buy put + sell OTM call, premiums cancel), **put spread** (buy put + sell further OTM put), **pure stop-loss** (no cost but no guarantee on gap).

### Scenario 4 — Covered Call · generate yield on long portfolio

**Thesis:** You hold 1,000 PETR4 shares (not selling) and want to **generate monthly income** by selling OTM calls.

**Inputs:** $S=46{,}60$, $K=49{,}00$ (~5% OTM), $r=14{,}65\%$, $\sigma=35\%$, $T=30/365=0{,}0822$

**Calculation:**

$$ d_1 = \frac{\ln(46{,}60/49) + 0{,}20775 \cdot 0{,}0822}{0{,}1003} = -0{,}33 $$

$$ d_2 = -0{,}33 - 0{,}1003 = -0{,}43 $$

$$ C = 46{,}60 \cdot N(-0{,}33) - 49 \cdot \e^{-0{,}012} \cdot N(-0{,}43) \approx 1{,}12 $$

⟹ **R\$ 1.12 per share**

**Real operation (sell 10 calls = "cap" 1,000 shares):**

| Item | Value |
|---|---|
| Base position | 1,000 shares × R\$46.60 = R\$ 46,600.00 |
| Premium received | +1,000 × R\$1.12 = **+R\$ 1,120.00** |
| Costs | ~R\$ 13.00 |
| **Monthly yield** (if shares go sideways) | R\$ 1,107 / R\$ 46,600 = **2.38% p.m. = ~32.7% a.a.** |
| Cap (max forced sale price) | **R\$ 49.00** (if exercised) |
| Maximum profit | (49.00 - 46.60)·1,000 + 1,107 = **R\$ 3,507** (=7.5% in 30d) |

**Comment:** Popular strategy in **dividend portfolios**. You trade **unlimited upside for deterministic income**:

- **Sideways scenario** ($S_T \in [45{,}48, 49{,}00]$): you keep shares + R\$1,107 clean. Excellent yield.
- **Strong upside scenario** ($S_T > 49$): shares are "called away" at R\$49. Profit capped at R\$3,507.
- **Downside scenario** ($S_T < 45{,}48$): you lose money on shares, but R\$1.12/share buffer helps.

Hidden risk: **if PETR4 explodes to R\$60, you left R\$11,000 on the table**. Who runs covered call **systematically** accepts that trade-off. Who does it once and the stock explodes gets frustrated and quits — behavioral.

### Operational lessons

1. **Scenario 1** is the "default beginner trader" — cheap, but requires directional AND timing correctness. Theta eats 100% of premium in sideways market.
2. **Scenario 2** is extreme leverage — only makes sense with real informational edge.
3. **Scenario 3** is expensive because negative skew prices in downside fear. Compare with zero-cost collar first.
4. **Scenario 4** is classic overlay on long-term portfolio (dividends). Sustains extra 1-3% p.m. yield.

**Single principle:** option is **bet on movement + time + vol**, not just direction. Who trades only direction loses to who understands all 3 dimensions. Black-Scholes is the ruler that decomposes observed price into "how much is baked into direction, vol, time".
