### Table of Elementary Derivatives

<Definicao titulo="Derivatives of Elementary Functions">

Let $f$ be differentiable at $x$. The derivatives below are direct consequences of the limit definition and are considered fundamental results:

| Function $f(x)$ | Derivative $f'(x)$ | Condition |
|---|---|---|
| $c$ (constant) | $0$ | $c \in \mathbb{R}$ |
| $x^n$ | $n x^{n-1}$ | $n \in \mathbb{R}$ |
| $\sin x$ | $\cos x$ | — |
| $\cos x$ | $-\sin x$ | — |
| $\tan x$ | $\sec^2 x$ | $x \neq \frac{\pi}{2} + k\pi$ |
| $e^x$ | $e^x$ | — |
| $\ln x$ | $\dfrac{1}{x}$ | $x > 0$ |
| $a^x$ | $a^x \ln a$ | $a > 0, a \neq 1$ |
| $\log_a x$ | $\dfrac{1}{x \ln a}$ | $x > 0, a > 0, a \neq 1$ |

</Definicao>

### Operational Rules

<Teorema titulo="Differentiation Rules (Leibniz and Cauchy)">

Let $f$ and $g$ be differentiable functions at $x$, and $c \in \mathbb{R}$ be a constant. Then:

<Equation numero="R1" explicacao="Constant: the derivative of any constant number is zero, as there is no variation." latex="(c)' = 0" />

<Equation numero="R2" explicacao="Power Rule: the exponent comes down as a coefficient and decreases by 1. Valid for any real exponent." latex="(x^n)' = n x^{n-1}" />

<Equation numero="R3" explicacao="Constant Multiple: constants pass through the derivative unchanged." latex="(c f)' = c f'" />

<Equation numero="R4" explicacao="Sum and Difference: the derivative distributes over sums and differences." latex="(f \pm g)' = f' \pm g'" />

<Equation numero="R5" explicacao="Product Rule (Leibniz): the product generates two terms. Each differentiates one of the factors, keeping the other intact." latex="(fg)' = f'g + fg'" />

<Equation numero="R6" explicacao="Quotient Rule: the numerator is f prime g minus f g prime; the denominator is g squared. The minus sign in the numerator is critical — do not reverse the order." latex="\left(\frac{f}{g}\right)' = \frac{f'g - fg'}{g^2}, \quad g \neq 0" />

</Teorema>

> "If $f$ and $g$ are differentiable functions, then the derivative of the product $(fg)'$ exists and is given by $f'(x)g(x) + f(x)g'(x)$." — [Active Calculus §2.3](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-2-3-prod-quot.html)

### Proof of the Product Rule

<Teorema titulo="Proof of the Product Rule from the Definition">

Let $h(x) = f(x) g(x)$. By definition:

$$h'(x) = \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \frac{f(x + \Delta x)\,g(x + \Delta x) - f(x)\,g(x)}{\Delta x}$$

Add and subtract $f(x+\Delta x)\,g(x)$ in the numerator:

$$= \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \frac{[f(x+\Delta x) - f(x)]\,g(x+\Delta x) + f(x)\,[g(x+\Delta x) - g(x)]}{\Delta x}$$

Separating the limits and using the continuity of $g$ (every differentiable function is continuous, so $g(x + \Delta x) \to g(x)$):

$$= \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \frac{f(x+\Delta x)-f(x)}{\Delta x} \cdot g(x) + f(x) \cdot \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \frac{g(x+\Delta x)-g(x)}{\Delta x} = f'(x)g(x) + f(x)g'(x)$$

$\blacksquare$

</Teorema>

The tangent line to the graph of $f$ at the point $(a, f(a))$ has the equation:

<Equation numero="T1" explicacao="Point-slope form: the slope (angular coefficient) is the derivative f'(a) calculated at the point of tangency." latex="y - f(a) = f'(a)(x - a)" />

</Definicao>

<Insight>

**Why isn't the product simply $f' \cdot g'$?** Imagine a rectangle with sides $f$ and $g$. When $f$ grows by $\Delta f$ and $g$ grows by $\Delta g$, the area grows by $\Delta f \cdot g + f \cdot \Delta g + \Delta f \cdot \Delta g$. The last term $\Delta f \cdot \Delta g$ is a product of two increments — an infinitesimal of higher order — and vanishes in the limit. The two terms of the product rule remain.

</Insight>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

Imagine you're walking on a road full of ups and downs.

The **derivative** is just **how steep the road is** at a point. Is it going up fast? Big slope. Flat road? Zero slope.

If road $A$ and road $B$ join to make a combined road, there's a special rule to find the steepness of the combined road. You can't just multiply the steepnesses. The **product rule** says: the steepness of the junction has **two parts** — one part accounts for the change in $A$, and the other accounts for the change in $B$.

It's like a box that grows in width and height at the same time: the area grows both from the width side and the height side.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

The **derivative** tells you the **speed** at which a function changes. If $s(t)$ is where you are at each moment, $s'(t)$ is your speed.

**Rules to calculate derivatives quickly:**

- **Power**: $(x^3)' = 3x^2$. The exponent comes down and becomes the coefficient, and decreases by 1. $(x^5)' = 5x^4$. $(x^{10})' = 10x^9$.
- **Constant disappears**: $(5x^2)' = 5 \cdot 2x = 10x$. The 5 passes through.
- **Sum of derivatives**: $(x^3 + x^2)' = 3x^2 + 2x$. Differentiate each piece separately.
- **Product**: this is different! $(x^2 \cdot x^3)' \neq 2x \cdot 3x^2$. The correct rule is $(fg)' = f'g + fg'$: you differentiate the first and leave the second, plus leave the first and differentiate the second.

Why complicate with the product rule? Because sometimes the two factors are very different functions, like $x^2 \cdot \sin x$. Then there's no way to simplify before differentiating.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old">

Lesson 51 gave you the definition of the derivative via limits — theoretically correct, but slow. This lesson gives you the **algebraic shortcuts** that professionals use. With them, you can differentiate any combination of polynomials, trigonometric, and exponential functions without ever touching a limit.

**The 3 rules you'll use in 90% of problems:**

**1. Power Rule:** $(x^n)' = nx^{n-1}$ for any real $n$.
- $(x^4)' = 4x^3$
- $(x^{-2})' = -2x^{-3} = -2/x^3$
- $(\sqrt{x})' = (x^{1/2})' = \frac{1}{2}x^{-1/2} = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}$

**2. Product Rule:** $(fg)' = f'g + fg'$

Example: $(x^2 \sin x)' = 2x \sin x + x^2 \cos x$. Note the two terms.

**3. Quotient Rule:** $\left(\dfrac{f}{g}\right)' = \dfrac{f'g - fg'}{g^2}$

Example: $\left(\dfrac{x^2}{x+1}\right)' = \dfrac{2x(x+1) - x^2 \cdot 1}{(x+1)^2} = \dfrac{x^2 + 2x}{(x+1)^2}$

**Table you should memorize:** $(\sin x)' = \cos x$; $(\cos x)' = -\sin x$; $(e^x)' = e^x$; $(\ln x)' = 1/x$.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an Engineering Student">

In engineering, differentiation is a **routine operation**: state equations, frequency response, design optimization. This lesson formalizes the algebra that makes this feasible.

### Hierarchy of Rules

Rules R1–R6 are theorems, all derived from the limit definition. The order of practical importance:

1. **R2 + R3 + R4** (power, multiple, sum): cover all polynomials. Cost $O(n)$ terms.
2. **R5** (product): necessary when factors do not simplify. Cost: doubles the terms.
3. **R6** (quotient): equivalent to product by $1/g$ + chain (Lesson 53). Cost: square in the denominator.

### Proof of the Quotient Rule via Product Rule

Instead of deriving by definition, apply the product rule to $f = (f/g) \cdot g$:

$$f' = \left(\frac{f}{g}\right)' g + \frac{f}{g} \cdot g' \implies \left(\frac{f}{g}\right)' = \frac{f' - (f/g) \cdot g'}{g} = \frac{f'g - fg'}{g^2}$$

### Linearity of the Differentiation Operator

The map $D: C^1(\mathbb{R}) \to C^0(\mathbb{R})$, $D(f) = f'$, is **linear**:
- $D(f + g) = D(f) + D(g)$
- $D(cf) = c \cdot D(f)$

Leibniz's property $D(fg) = D(f) \cdot g + f \cdot D(g)$ is the extra condition that transforms it into a **derivation operator** (derivation in algebra). Any linear map satisfying Leibniz in a commutative ring is called a **derivation** in the algebraic sense — this appears in differential geometry and Lie algebra.

### Cross-Domain Connection

- **Mechanics**: position $s(t)$, velocity $v = s'$, acceleration $a = v' = s''$. Force $F = ma = m s''$.
- **Electromagnetism**: Faraday's law $\mathcal{E} = -d\Phi_B/dt$. Derivative of flux.
- **Engineering optimization**: optimal design requires $f'(x^*) = 0$ — and recognizing this as a minimum via the sign of $f''(x^*)$.
- **Autodiff**: libraries (JAX, PyTorch) implement R5 via reverse chain rule on the computational graph. Every neural network trains by backpropagation = Leibniz at scale.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a Senior Professional">

Differentiation rules are instances of abstract algebraic structures with applications in differential geometry, non-commutative algebra, and symbolic computation.

### Differential Algebra

Let $A$ be a commutative ring with unity. A **derivation** on $A$ is a $\mathbb{k}$-linear map $D: A \to A$ satisfying Leibniz's rule:

$$D(ab) = D(a)b + aD(b), \quad \forall a, b \in A$$

The set $\text{Der}(A)$ of all derivations on $A$ forms a Lie $A$-module with bracket $[D_1, D_2] = D_1 D_2 - D_2 D_1$ — the structure of vector fields in the context of differentiable manifolds (Cartan, 1930s).

### Non-commutative Generalization

In non-commutative algebras (matrices $M_n(\mathbb{R})$, linear operators), Leibniz takes the form:

$$D(AB) = D(A)B + AD(B)$$

The order of factors is essential. Direct application: differentiation of matrix exponential $\frac{d}{dt}e^{At}$ is not simply $Ae^{At}$ when $A$ depends on $t$ in a non-commutative way.

### Jacobi's Formula for Determinants

The identity $\frac{d}{dt}\det A(t) = \det A(t) \cdot \text{tr}(A(t)^{-1} \dot A(t))$ follows from Leibniz applied to cofactor expansion. It appears in Riccati equations (optimal control) and in volume estimates in differential geometry.

### Symbolic Computation and Autodiff

Automatic differentiation (AD) systems implement R5 in reverse mode (backpropagation) by traversing the computational graph in a topologically ordered manner. The cost of reverse AD is $O(\text{cost of the forward pass})$, independent of dimension — which is why gradients of networks with $10^9$ parameters are computable in inference time.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practical Application — Engineering and Sciences">

### Case 1: Velocity and Acceleration from Position

A projectile has vertical position $s(t) = -4.9t^2 + 20t + 1.5$ meters, where $t$ is in seconds.

- Velocity: $v(t) = s'(t) = -9.8t + 20$ m/s.
- Acceleration: $a(t) = v'(t) = s''(t) = -9.8$ m/s² (gravity, constant).
- Initial velocity ($t=0$): $v(0) = 20$ m/s.
- Highest point: $v(t^*) = 0 \Rightarrow t^* = 20/9.8 \approx 2.04$ s.

### Case 2: Marginal Cost in Economics

Cost function: $C(q) = 500 + 40q + 0.2q^2$ (reais), where $q$ is quantity produced.

Marginal cost: $C'(q) = 40 + 0.4q$.

At $q = 100$: $C'(100) = 40 + 40 = 80$ reais per unit. This means: producing the 101st unit costs approximately R$ 80 more.

### Case 3: Tangent Line as Local Linearization

Near $x = 1$, the function $f(x) = x^3 - 2x + 3$ behaves like its tangent at $x=1$:

- $f(1) = 1 - 2 + 3 = 2$
- $f'(x) = 3x^2 - 2$, so $f'(1) = 1$
- Tangent: $y = 2 + 1 \cdot (x - 1) = x + 1$

For $x = 1.02$: tangent gives $2.02$; real value $f(1.02) \approx 2.0208$. Error of $0.04\%$.

This is the basis of **Newton-Raphson's method** and all linearization of non-linear systems in control engineering.

### Differentiation Workflow

1. **Identify the structure**: polynomial, product, quotient, trigonometric?
2. **Apply rules R1–R6** in the correct order.
3. **Substitute using the table** of elementary functions.
4. **Simplify algebraically**.
5. **Check with SymPy**:

```python
import sympy as sp
x = sp.symbols('x')
f = (x**2 + 1) * sp.sin(x) / (x + 2)
print(sp.diff(f, x))
```

Use symbolic tools to **check**, not to replace understanding.

## Solved Examples

<Exemplo titulo="Derivative of a polynomial (direct application of rules R2–R4)" numero="1">

**Problem:** Calculate $f'(x)$ for $f(x) = 4x^5 - 3x^3 + 7x - 2$.

**Strategy:** Polynomial = sum of monomials. Apply R4 (linearity), R2 (power), and R1 (constant) term by term.

**Solution:**

$$f'(x) = (4x^5)' - (3x^3)' + (7x)' - (2)'$$

Applying R3 (constant multiple) and R2 (power):

$$= 4 \cdot 5x^4 - 3 \cdot 3x^2 + 7 \cdot 1 - 0$$

$$= 20x^4 - 9x^2 + 7$$

**Verification:** Each exponent reduced by 1; each coefficient multiplied by the original exponent. Constant $-2$ disappeared (R1). Correct.

**Source.** [Active Calculus §2.1 Exercise 2.1.3](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-2-1-elem-rules.html) — Boelkins · 2024 · CC-BY-NC-SA.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Product Rule — polynomial times trigonometric" numero="2">

**Problem:** Calculate $h'(x)$ for $h(x) = x^3 \sin x$.

**Strategy:** $h = f \cdot g$ with $f(x) = x^3$ and $g(x) = \sin x$. Apply R5: $(fg)' = f'g + fg'$.

**Solution:**

- $f(x) = x^3 \Rightarrow f'(x) = 3x^2$
- $g(x) = \sin x \Rightarrow g'(x) = \cos x$

Applying R5:

$$h'(x) = f'(x)g(x) + f(x)g'(x) = 3x^2 \sin x + x^3 \cos x$$

**Factored form (optional):** $h'(x) = x^2(3 \sin x + x \cos x)$

**Verification:** Two terms (correct for product). First: differentiated $x^3$, kept $\sin x$. Second: kept $x^3$, differentiated $\sin x$. Both elementary rules applied correctly.

**Source.** [Active Calculus §2.3 Exercise 2.3.2](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-2-3-prod-quot.html) — Boelkins · 2024 · CC-BY-NC-SA.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Quotient Rule — rational function" numero="3">

**Problem:** Calculate $k'(x)$ for $k(x) = \dfrac{x^2 + 1}{x - 3}$, $x \neq 3$.

**Strategy:** $k = f/g$ with $f(x) = x^2 + 1$ and $g(x) = x - 3$. Apply R6.

**Solution:**

- $f'(x) = 2x$
- $g'(x) = 1$

Applying R6:

$$k'(x) = \frac{f'(x)g(x) - f(x)g'(x)}{[g(x)]^2} = \frac{2x(x-3) - (x^2+1) \cdot 1}{(x-3)^2}$$

$$= \frac{2x^2 - 6x - x^2 - 1}{(x-3)^2} = \frac{x^2 - 6x - 1}{(x-3)^2}$$

**Attention:** The numerator is $f'g - fg'$, not $fg' - f'g$. The minus sign is not commutative — reversing it creates the most frequent error in this rule.

**Source.** [OpenStax Calculus Vol. 1 §3.3 Example 3.28](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/3-3-differentiation-rules) — OpenStax · 2016 · CC-BY-NC-SA.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Product of exponential with trigonometric" numero="4">

**Problem:** Calculate $p'(x)$ for $p(x) = e^x \cos x$.

**Strategy:** Product of $f(x) = e^x$ and $g(x) = \cos x$. Apply R5 with the derivatives from the table.

**Solution:**

- $f'(x) = e^x$ (notable: $e^x$ differentiates to itself)
- $g'(x) = -\sin x$

Applying R5:

$$p'(x) = e^x \cos x + e^x \cdot (-\sin x) = e^x(\cos x - \sin x)$$

**Verification:** At $x = 0$: $p(0) = e^0 \cos 0 = 1$ and $p'(0) = e^0(\cos 0 - \sin 0) = 1 - 0 = 1$. The tangent at $(0, 1)$ has a slope of 1, which makes geometric sense (the function grows with a slope of 1 at the origin).

**Source.** [Active Calculus §2.2 Exercise 2.2.3](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-2-2-sin-cos.html) — Boelkins · 2024 · CC-BY-NC-SA.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Equation of the Tangent Line with Critical Point" numero="5">

**Problem:** Find the equation of the tangent line to the curve $y = \dfrac{x}{x^2 + 1}$ at the point where $x = 1$.

**Strategy:** (a) Calculate $y(1)$ to get the point of tangency. (b) Calculate $y'(x)$ using the quotient rule. (c) Evaluate $y'(1)$. (d) Write in point-slope form.

**Solution:**

**(a) Point of tangency:**

$$y(1) = \frac{1}{1^2 + 1} = \frac{1}{2}$$

Point: $\left(1, \dfrac{1}{2}\right)$.

**(b) Derivative by quotient rule** with $f = x$ and $g = x^2 + 1$:

$$y'(x) = \frac{f'g - fg'}{g^2} = \frac{1 \cdot (x^2+1) - x \cdot 2x}{(x^2+1)^2} = \frac{x^2 + 1 - 2x^2}{(x^2+1)^2} = \frac{1 - x^2}{(x^2+1)^2}$$

**(c) Slope at $x = 1$:**

$$y'(1) = \frac{1 - 1}{(1+1)^2} = \frac{0}{4} = 0$$

**(d) Equation of the tangent:**

$$y - \frac{1}{2} = 0 \cdot (x - 1) \implies y = \frac{1}{2}$$

The tangent at $x = 1$ is horizontal — this makes sense, as $y = x/(x^2+1)$ has a local maximum at this point (the function increases from $x=0$ to $x=1$ and then decreases).

**Source.** [OpenStax Calculus Vol. 1 §3.3 Exercise 176](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/3-3-differentiation-rules) — OpenStax · 2016 · CC-BY-NC-SA.
</Exemplo>
