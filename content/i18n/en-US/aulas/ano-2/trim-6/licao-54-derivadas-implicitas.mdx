### Motivation

A plane curve can be given by $F(x, y) = 0$ without it being possible, or convenient, to isolate $y$ explicitly. The circle $x^2 + y^2 = r^2$ and the Folium of Descartes $x^3 + y^3 = 3axy$ are canonical examples. Implicit differentiation bypasses the obstacle.

### Formal Recipe

Let $F(x, y) = 0$ be an equation defining $y$ as a function of $x$ in a neighborhood of a point $(a, b)$.

<Definicao titulo="Implicit Differentiation Algorithm">

1. Write the equation in the form $F(x, y) = 0$.
2. Differentiate both sides with respect to $x$. Whenever $y$ appears, apply the chain rule:

$$\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[G(y)\bigr] = G'(y)\,\frac{dy}{dx}$$

3. Gather all terms with $\tfrac{dy}{dx}$ on the left side.
4. Factor out $\tfrac{dy}{dx}$ and divide.

</Definicao>

### Canonical Example: Circle

$$x^2 + y^2 = r^2$$

Differentiating: $2x + 2y\,y' = 0$, whence $y' = -\dfrac{x}{y}$ (valid for $y \neq 0$).

### Table of Classic Curves

| Curve | Equation $F(x,y)=0$ | $dy/dx$ |
|---|---|---|
| Circle | $x^2 + y^2 - r^2 = 0$ | $-x/y$ |
| Ellipse | $x^2/a^2 + y^2/b^2 - 1 = 0$ | $-(b^2 x)/(a^2 y)$ |
| Hyperbola | $x^2/a^2 - y^2/b^2 - 1 = 0$ | $(b^2 x)/(a^2 y)$ |
| Folium of Descartes | $x^3 + y^3 - 3axy = 0$ | $(ay - x^2)/(y^2 - ax)$ |

> "If the equation relating $x$ and $y$ cannot be solved for $y$ explicitly, we can still find $y'$ by differentiating the equation implicitly." — [OpenStax Calculus Volume 1, §3.8](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/3-8-implicit-differentiation)

### Implicit Function Theorem (1D version)

<Teorema titulo="Implicit Function Theorem (Scalar Case)">

Let $F: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}$ be of class $C^1$ in an open neighborhood of $(a, b)$, with $F(a, b) = 0$ and $F_y(a, b) \neq 0$. Then there exist an open interval $I$ containing $a$ and a unique function $y = g(x)$, of class $C^1$ on $I$, such that $g(a) = b$ and $F(x, g(x)) = 0$ for all $x \in I$. Furthermore:

<Equation numero="IFT" explicacao="The implicit differentiation formula is a direct consequence of the chain rule applied to the composition F(x, g(x)) = 0." latex="g'(x) = -\frac{F_x(x,\,g(x))}{F_y(x,\,g(x))}" />

</Teorema>

<strong>When it fails.</strong> If $F_y(a, b) = 0$, the curve may have a vertical tangent at that point, or it may not define a function locally. Example: the circle at points $(\pm r, 0)$ — $F_y = 2y = 0$ there.

<Insight>

The condition $F_y \neq 0$ is the crucial hypothesis. Geometrically, $\nabla F = (F_x, F_y)$ is the gradient of $F$, perpendicular to the curve. The tangent vector is $T = (-F_y, F_x)$. Thus the slope of the tangent is $T_y/T_x = F_x/(-F_y) = -F_x/F_y$ — exactly the implicit formula. When $F_y = 0$, the tangent vector is vertical and the slope does not exist as a real number.

</Insight>

### Implicit Second Derivative

Apply $\tfrac{d}{dx}$ again to $y' = -F_x/F_y$, using the quotient rule and remembering that $y$ depends on $x$.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

Imagine you drew a **circle** on a piece of paper. The circle isn't a simple function — for every horizontal position, there are two points on the circle: one above and one below.

But we can ask: if I move a little bit to the right, does the top point go up or down, and by how much? That's the **slope** of the circle.

The trick of implicit differentiation is: instead of trying to separate the "top" from the "bottom," we **differentiate the whole circle's equation** at once, treating $y$ as if it were a function of $x$.

It's like trying to figure out how many apples are inside a closed box, without opening the box — just by weighing it and using what you know.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

Sometimes you have an equation that mixes $x$ and $y$ in a way that makes it impossible (or very hard) to isolate $y$ by itself. Example:

$$x^3 + y^3 = 6xy$$

This is the **Folium of Descartes** — a beautiful curve that you can't write as "$y = $ some simple thing."

The trick is: **differentiate everything with respect to $x$**, remembering that $y$ changes when $x$ changes. Every time you differentiate a term with $y$, put a $y'$ next to it (because of the chain rule).

**Example with the circle.** $x^2 + y^2 = 25$. Differentiating both sides with respect to $x$:

$$2x + 2y \cdot y' = 0$$

The $2y \cdot y'$ appears because $y^2$ depends on $x$ through $y$. Isolating:

$$y' = -\frac{x}{y}$$

At the point $(3, 4)$ on the circle: $y' = -3/4$. This means the tangent line to the circle there has a slope of $-3/4$.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old">

**Why use implicit differentiation?** Because many important curves — circles, ellipses, leaves — are not graphs of functions. And even when it's possible to isolate $y$, the result might be so messy that working with the implicit equation is cleaner.

### The Step-by-Step Recipe

1. **Treat $y$ as a function of $x$** — call it $y(x)$.
2. **Differentiate each term** with respect to $x$.
3. **Apply the chain rule** whenever $y$ appears: $\dfrac{d}{dx}[y^n] = n\,y^{n-1}\,y'$.
4. **Group the terms with $y'$** on one side of the equation.
5. **Isolate $y'$** by dividing.

### Complete Example: $x^2 + xy + y^2 = 7$

Differentiating each term with respect to $x$:

$$\frac{d}{dx}[x^2] + \frac{d}{dx}[xy] + \frac{d}{dx}[y^2] = 0$$

$$2x + \bigl(y + x\,y'\bigr) + 2y\,y' = 0$$

(The $xy$ term required the product rule: $(x)' y + x(y)' = y + x y'$.)

Grouping $y'$ terms:

$$x\,y' + 2y\,y' = -2x - y$$

$$y'(x + 2y) = -2x - y$$

$$y' = \frac{-2x - y}{x + 2y}$$

### Tangent at a Point

To find the tangent at $(a, b)$: evaluate $y'$ at the point and write $y - b = y'(a, b)\cdot(x - a)$.

### Where is the Tangent Horizontal or Vertical?

- **Horizontal**: $y' = 0 \Rightarrow$ numerator $= 0$.
- **Vertical**: $y'$ does not exist $\Rightarrow$ denominator $= 0$ (and numerator $\neq 0$).

<Insight>

Implicit differentiation connects directly to the **chain rule**: differentiating $F(x, y(x)) = 0$ gives $F_x + F_y \cdot y' = 0$, so $y' = -F_x/F_y$. Everything you learned in Lesson 53 is here — implicit differentiation isn't a new trick, it's the chain rule in disguise.

</Insight>

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

### Geometric and Analytic Interpretation

The curve $\{(x,y) : F(x,y) = 0\}$ has gradient vector $\nabla F = (F_x, F_y)$ perpendicular to the curve. The tangent vector is $T = (-F_y, F_x)$ (rotated 90°). The slope of the tangent is:

$$\frac{T_y}{T_x} = \frac{F_x}{-F_y} = -\frac{F_x}{F_y}$$

This matches the implicit differentiation formula exactly.

### Logarithmic Differentiation

For functions of the type $y = u(x)^{v(x)}$, the cleanest method is to take the logarithm before differentiating:

$$\ln y = v(x)\,\ln u(x) \implies \frac{y'}{y} = v'(x)\,\ln u(x) + v(x)\,\frac{u'(x)}{u(x)}$$

Example: $y = x^x$. Then $\ln y = x \ln x$, and $y'/y = \ln x + 1$, so $y' = x^x(\ln x + 1)$.

### Implicit Second Derivative

Given $y' = -F_x/F_y$, apply $d/dx$ again using the quotient rule and remembering that $F_x, F_y$ depend on $x$ and $y(x)$.

For $F(x,y) = x^2 + y^2 - r^2$: $F_x = 2x$, $F_y = 2y$, $y' = -x/y$.

$$y'' = -\frac{y - x\,y'}{y^2} = -\frac{y + x^2/y}{y^2} = -\frac{y^2 + x^2}{y^3} = -\frac{r^2}{y^3}$$

### Cross-Domain Applications

- **Thermodynamics**: equation of state $f(P,V,T)=0$. Holding $T$ constant: $\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial V}\right)_T = -F_V/F_P$ (implicit differentiation with a parameter).
- **Lagrangian Mechanics**: holonomic constraints $g(q_1,\ldots,q_n)=0$ — Lagrange multipliers use the gradient of $g$, which is the generalized implicit derivative.
- **Economics**: indifference curves $U(x_1, x_2) = \bar{U}$ — the marginal rate of substitution is $-U_{x_1}/U_{x_2}$, exactly the implicit $y'$.
- **Machine Learning**: "Differentiable optimization" (CvxpyLayers, OptNet) uses the Implicit Function Theorem to propagate gradients through optimization subproblems.

> "The key idea is that even though $y$ is defined implicitly, we can still compute $dy/dx$ by treating $y$ as a function of $x$ and applying the chain rule." — [Active Calculus 2.0, §2.7](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-2-7-implicit.html)

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

### Implicit Function Theorem in Banach Spaces

Scalar implicit differentiation is the $n=m=1$ case of a much more general result.

<Teorema titulo="IFT — Banach Version (informal)">

Let $X, Y, Z$ be Banach spaces, $F: X \times Y \to Z$ be of class $C^1$. If $F(a,b) = 0$ and $D_y F(a,b): Y \to Z$ is an isomorphism (continuous linear bijection with continuous inverse), then there exists a neighborhood $U \times V$ of $(a,b)$ and a unique $g: U \to V$ of class $C^1$ satisfying $F(x, g(x)) = 0$. The derivative of $g$ is:

$$g'(a) = -\bigl[D_y F(a,b)\bigr]^{-1} \circ D_x F(a,b)$$

</Teorema>

<strong>Degenerate cases.</strong> When $D_y F$ is singular (non-invertible), the IFT fails and **bifurcations** arise — these are precisely the points of interest in bifurcation theory (Crandall–Rabinowitz, 1971).

### Implied Volatility (Black–Scholes)

In finance, $\sigma^*$ (implied volatility) is defined by $V_{\text{BS}}(S, K, T, r, \sigma^*) = V_{\text{mkt}}$. By the IFT:

$$\frac{d\sigma^*}{dV_{\text{mkt}}} = \frac{1}{\partial V_{\text{BS}}/\partial \sigma} = \frac{1}{\mathcal{V}}$$

where $\mathcal{V}$ is Vega. The implicit derivative is, literally, the inverse of Vega — and this is how traders calibrate volatility models in real-time.

### Constrained Optimization and KKT

The KKT conditions for $\min f(x)$ s.t. $g(x) = 0$ form an implicit system in $(x^*, \lambda^*)$. The sensitivity of the optimal solution to parameters (Envelope Theorem) is implicitly differentiated from the KKT system. This is why implicit differentiation is at the heart of all sensitivity analysis in nonlinear programming.

### Numerical Continuation

Predictor-corrector methods (Keller, 1977) track solution branches of $F(x, \lambda) = 0$ as $\lambda$ varies. The predictor step uses $dx/d\lambda$ (implicit); the corrector step uses Newton. Basis for software like AUTO and MATCONT.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practical Applications and Common Mistakes">

### When to Use Implicit Differentiation

Use it whenever:
- The equation relates $x$ and $y$ without isolating $y$ easily (circles, ellipses, implicit curves).
- Isolating $y$ would produce a more complex expression (cubic roots, transcendental functions).
- You need $dy/dx$ at a specific point without needing the global function.

### Complete Workflow

1. Identify the equation $F(x, y) = 0$.
2. Differentiate **each term** with respect to $x$. Rules to remember:
   - $\dfrac{d}{dx}[y] = y'$
   - $\dfrac{d}{dx}[y^2] = 2y\,y'$
   - $\dfrac{d}{dx}[y^n] = n\,y^{n-1}\,y'$
   - $\dfrac{d}{dx}[x\,y] = y + x\,y'$ (product rule)
   - $\dfrac{d}{dx}[\sin y] = \cos y \cdot y'$
   - $\dfrac{d}{dx}[e^y] = e^y \cdot y'$
3. Group terms with $y'$ on the left.
4. Isolate $y'$.
5. Evaluate at the point to get the numerical slope.

### Classic Mistakes

1. **Forgetting the $y'$ when differentiating terms with $y$.** $\dfrac{d}{dx}[y^2] = 2y$ — wrong. Correct: $2y\,y'$.
2. **Forgetting the product rule in $xy$:** derivative is $y + x\,y'$, not just $y'$.
3. **Not checking where the denominator zeros.** If $F_y = 0$ at the point, $y'$ doesn't exist there (vertical tangent).
4. **Confusing $\partial F/\partial y$ with $dy/dx$.** They are different objects: one is a partial derivative, the other is the desired total derivative.

### Types of Test Questions

- Calculate $dy/dx$ for the given curve.
- Find the equation of the tangent line at $(a, b)$.
- Find points of horizontal or vertical tangency.
- Calculate $y''$ implicitly.

</Porta>

## Solved Examples

<Exemplo titulo="Implicit Differentiation of a Circle (Foundation)" numero="1">

**Problem.** Find $dy/dx$ for the circle $x^2 + y^2 = 25$.

**Strategy.** This is the standard example for implicit differentiation. Differentiate directly with respect to $x$, remembering that $y$ is a function of $x$.

**Solution.**

1. Differentiate both sides with respect to $x$:

$$\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^2 + y^2\bigr] = \frac{d}{dx}[25]$$

2. Apply the chain rule to $y^2$:

$$2x + 2y\,\frac{dy}{dx} = 0$$

3. Isolate $dy/dx$:

$$2y\,\frac{dy}{dx} = -2x \implies \frac{dy}{dx} = -\frac{x}{y}$$

**Check at point $(3, 4)$:** The point is on the curve ($9 + 16 = 25$). The slope is $dy/dx = -3/4$. The tangent line is $y - 4 = -\tfrac{3}{4}(x - 3)$, or $3x + 4y = 25$. Geometrically: the radius to $(3,4)$ has slope $4/3$, and $(-3/4)(4/3) = -1$, confirming tangent and radius are perpendicular.

**Source.** [OpenStax Calculus Volume 1 §3.8](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/3-8-implicit-differentiation), example 3.68, p. 318 — CC-BY-NC-SA 4.0 license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Implicit Differentiation of an Ellipse — Tangent at a Specific Point" numero="2">

**Problem.** For the ellipse $\dfrac{x^2}{16} + \dfrac{y^2}{9} = 1$, find $dy/dx$ and the equation of the tangent line at $\left(2, \tfrac{3\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)$.

**Strategy.** Apply implicit differentiation, then evaluate at the given point.

**Solution.**

1. Differentiating:

$$\frac{d}{dx}\left[\frac{x^2}{16} + \frac{y^2}{9}\right] = 0 \implies \frac{2x}{16} + \frac{2y}{9}\,y' = 0$$

2. Simplifying:

$$\frac{x}{8} + \frac{2y}{9}\,y' = 0 \implies y' = -\frac{9x}{16y}$$

3. At the point $\left(2, \tfrac{3\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)$:

$$y' = -\frac{9 \cdot 2}{16 \cdot \tfrac{3\sqrt{3}}{2}} = -\frac{18}{24\sqrt{3}} = -\frac{3}{4\sqrt{3}} = -\frac{\sqrt{3}}{4}$$

4. Equation of the tangent line:

$$y - \frac{3\sqrt{3}}{2} = -\frac{\sqrt{3}}{4}\left(x - 2\right) \implies y = -\frac{\sqrt{3}}{4}\,x + 2\sqrt{3}$$

**Check:** Substituting $x = 2$: $y = -\sqrt{3}/2 + 2\sqrt{3} = 3\sqrt{3}/2$. Correct.

**Source.** [Active Calculus 2.0 §2.7](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-2-7-implicit.html), Preview Activity 2.7.1, p. 154 — CC-BY-NC-SA 4.0 license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Folium of Descartes — Implicit Differentiation with Product Rule" numero="3">

**Problem.** Find $dy/dx$ for the Folium of Descartes $x^3 + y^3 = 3xy$.

**Strategy.** The right side $3xy$ requires the product rule when differentiating. Then, group terms and isolate $y'$.

**Solution.**

1. Differentiating both sides with respect to $x$:

$$3x^2 + 3y^2\,y' = 3\,(y + x\,y')$$

(On the right side: $\dfrac{d}{dx}[3xy] = 3\left[(1)\cdot y + x \cdot y'\right] = 3y + 3x\,y'$.)

2. Expand and group terms with $y'$:

$$3x^2 + 3y^2\,y' = 3y + 3x\,y'$$

$$3y^2\,y' - 3x\,y' = 3y - 3x^2$$

$$y'(3y^2 - 3x) = 3(y - x^2)$$

3. Isolate $y'$:

$$y' = \frac{3(y - x^2)}{3(y^2 - x)} = \frac{y - x^2}{y^2 - x}$$

**Check:** At the point $\left(\tfrac{3}{2}, \tfrac{3}{2}\right)$ (which lies on the Folium with $a=1$):

$$y' = \frac{3/2 - 9/4}{9/4 - 3/2} = \frac{-3/4}{3/4} = -1$$

The tangent has slope $-1$, which makes sense due to the curve's symmetry about the line $y = x$ at that point.

**Source.** [OpenStax Calculus Volume 1 §3.8](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/3-8-implicit-differentiation), exercise 175, p. 325 — CC-BY-NC-SA 4.0 license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Implicit Second Derivative" numero="4">

**Problem.** For $x^2 + y^2 = r^2$, find $d^2y/dx^2$ in terms of $x$, $y$, and $r$.

**Strategy.** We already know $y' = -x/y$. Differentiate one more time with respect to $x$, applying the quotient rule and substituting $y'$ into the resulting expression.

**Solution.**

1. Start with $y' = -\dfrac{x}{y}$.

2. Apply $\dfrac{d}{dx}$ using the quotient rule:

$$y'' = -\frac{(x)'\,y - x\,(y)'}{y^2} = -\frac{y - x\,y'}{y^2}$$

3. Substitute $y' = -x/y$:

$$y'' = -\frac{y - x \cdot (-x/y)}{y^2} = -\frac{y + x^2/y}{y^2} = -\frac{y^2 + x^2}{y^3}$$

4. Use $x^2 + y^2 = r^2$:

$$y'' = -\frac{r^2}{y^3}$$

**Interpretation.** For $y > 0$ (upper part of the circle), $y'' = -r^2/y^3 < 0$ — the curve is concave down, which is geometrically correct for the upper semicircle.

**Source.** [Active Calculus 2.0 §2.7](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-2-7-implicit.html), Activity 2.7.3, p. 158 — CC-BY-NC-SA 4.0 license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Logarithmic Derivative and Trigonometric Implicit Curve" numero="5">

**Problem (two parts).**
(a) Use logarithmic differentiation to find $y'$ if $y = x^{\sin x}$ (with $x > 0$).
(b) Find $dy/dx$ for $\sin(xy) = x - y$. Evaluate at $(0, 0)$.

**Strategy.** (a) Taking the logarithm before differentiating eliminates the variable exponent. (b) Implicit differentiation with the chain rule on $\sin(xy)$ requires the product rule in the argument.

**Solution — part (a).**

1. Take $\ln$ of both sides: $\ln y = \sin x \cdot \ln x$.

2. Differentiate with respect to $x$:

$$\frac{y'}{y} = \cos x \cdot \ln x + \sin x \cdot \frac{1}{x}$$

3. Multiply by $y = x^{\sin x}$:

$$y' = x^{\sin x}\!\left(\cos x\,\ln x + \frac{\sin x}{x}\right)$$

**Solution — part (b).**

1. Differentiating $\sin(xy) = x - y$:

$$\cos(xy) \cdot \frac{d}{dx}[xy] = 1 - y'$$

$$\cos(xy) \cdot (y + x\,y') = 1 - y'$$

2. Expand and group:

$$y\cos(xy) + x\cos(xy)\,y' = 1 - y'$$

$$y' \bigl(x\cos(xy) + 1\bigr) = 1 - y\cos(xy)$$

$$y' = \frac{1 - y\cos(xy)}{1 + x\cos(xy)}$$

3. At $(0, 0)$: $\cos(0 \cdot 0) = \cos 0 = 1$.

$$y'(0,0) = \frac{1 - 0 \cdot 1}{1 + 0 \cdot 1} = \frac{1}{1} = 1$$

**Check:** Does $(0,0)$ satisfy $\sin(0 \cdot 0) = 0 - 0$? $\sin(0) = 0$. Correct.

**Source.** [APEX Calculus §2.6](https://www.apexcalculus.com/), examples 2.6.4 and 2.6.5, p. 117 — CC-BY-NC 4.0 license.
</Exemplo>
