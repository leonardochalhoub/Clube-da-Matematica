### Definition of the derivative

<Definicao titulo="Derivative at a point">

Let $f: I \to \mathbb{R}$ be defined on an open interval $I$ and $a \in I$. The **derivative of $f$ at $a$** is the limit

$$f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h}$$

when that limit exists (and is finite). In that case we say that $f$ is **differentiable** at $a$.

**Alternative form** (substitution $x = a + h$, $h = x - a$):

$$f'(a) = \lim_{x \to a} \frac{f(x) - f(a)}{x - a}$$

</Definicao>

> "We say that a function $f$ is differentiable at $x = a$ whenever $f'(a)$ exists. […] The derivative measures the instantaneous rate of change of the function, as well as the slope of the tangent line to the function at the given point." — [Boelkins, Active Calculus §1.3](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-1-3-derivative-pt.html)

> "The derivative of a function $f(x)$ at a point $a$ in its domain, if it exists, is $f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0}\frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h}$." — [OpenStax Calculus Vol. 1, §3.1](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/3-1-defining-the-derivative)

### Equivalent notations

<Equation explicacao="All notations below represent the same object — the derivative of f. Leibniz (dy/dx), Lagrange (f'), Newton (f with a dot), and the operator D are the most common." latex="f'(x) \;=\; \frac{df}{dx} \;=\; \frac{dy}{dx} \;=\; Df(x) \;=\; \dot{f}(x)" />

### Tangent line and normal line

If $f$ is differentiable at $a$:

- **Tangent line** at $(a, f(a))$: $\quad y - f(a) = f'(a)(x - a)$
- **Normal line** at $(a, f(a))$ (perpendicular to the tangent, when $f'(a) \neq 0$): $\quad y - f(a) = -\dfrac{1}{f'(a)}(x - a)$

### Fundamental theorem of differentiability

<Teorema titulo="Differentiability implies continuity">

If $f$ is differentiable at $a$, then $f$ is continuous at $a$.

**Proof.** Write $f(a+h) - f(a) = \dfrac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h} \cdot h$. As $h \to 0$, the first factor approaches $f'(a)$ (by hypothesis) and $h \to 0$. Therefore $f(a+h) - f(a) \to f'(a) \cdot 0 = 0$, that is, $\lim_{h \to 0} f(a+h) = f(a)$. $\square$

**The converse is false.** $f(x) = |x|$ is continuous at $0$ but not differentiable at $0$ (left and right derivatives $-1$ and $+1$ differ).

</Teorema>

> "If $f$ is differentiable at $a$, then $f$ is continuous at $a$. […] The converse is not true, and a function can be continuous but fail to be differentiable at a point." — [OpenStax Calculus Vol. 1, §3.2](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/3-2-the-derivative-as-a-function)

### Points of non-differentiability

<Definicao titulo="Classification of pathological points">

- **Corner (kink)**: one-sided derivatives exist but differ. Example: $|x|$ at $x = 0$.
- **Cusp**: $f'(x) \to \pm\infty$ from both sides. Example: $x^{2/3}$ at $x = 0$.
- **Vertical tangent**: $f'(a) = +\infty$ (or $-\infty$). Example: $\sqrt[3]{x}$ at $x = 0$.
- **Discontinuity**: if $f$ is not continuous at $a$, it is certainly not differentiable there.

</Definicao>

### Basic derivatives via the definition

| Function $f(x)$ | $f'(x)$ |
|---|---|
| $c$ (constant) | $0$ |
| $x$ | $1$ |
| $x^2$ | $2x$ |
| $x^3$ | $3x^2$ |
| $x^n$ ($n \in \mathbb{Z}$) | $n x^{n-1}$ |
| $\dfrac{1}{x}$ | $-\dfrac{1}{x^2}$ |
| $\sqrt{x}$ | $\dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}$ |

But the derivative of any function that does not fit the ready-made rules always falls back to the definition.

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

Imagine you are in a car going from one city to another. Sometimes the car goes **slowly**, sometimes it goes **fast**.

The car's speedometer shows your speed **right now**, at this exact second.

The **derivative** is like a mathematical speedometer. It says "how fast this function is changing right now." If the function rises quickly, the derivative is large. If the function is standing still, the derivative is zero.

Think of it this way: imagine you walk 100 meters in 10 seconds. Your **average speed** is 10 meters per second. But did you walk at exactly the same pace the whole time? Maybe you ran at the start and walked slowly at the end.

Your **exact speed** at one specific second — that is the derivative.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

You already know how to calculate **average speed**: distance divided by time. I walked 100 meters in 10 seconds, average speed = 10 m/s.

But what about your speed **at this exact instant**? To find it, take a very small time interval, calculate the average speed over that interval, and keep shrinking the interval smaller and smaller.

The **derivative** is what happens when the interval becomes infinitely small — it is the "instantaneous speed" of the function.

**Practical example:** if your position is $s(t) = t^2$ (meters), what happens at $t = 3$ s?

- Interval of 1 s: $\frac{s(4) - s(3)}{1} = \frac{16 - 9}{1} = 7$ m/s
- Interval of 0.1 s: $\frac{s(3.1) - s(3)}{0.1} = \frac{9.61 - 9}{0.1} = 6.1$ m/s
- Interval of 0.01 s: $\frac{s(3.01) - s(3)}{0.01} = \frac{9.0601 - 9}{0.01} = 6.01$ m/s
- In the limit: exact speed = **6 m/s**.

The number 6 is the derivative of $s(t) = t^2$ at the point $t = 3$.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old teen">

The **average rate of change** of $f$ between $a$ and $a + h$ is:

$$\frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h}$$

Geometrically, this is the slope of the **secant line** through the points $(a, f(a))$ and $(a+h, f(a+h))$.

As $h \to 0$, the secant "rotates" and approaches the **tangent line** to the graph at $(a, f(a))$. The slope of that tangent line is the derivative $f'(a)$.

### Recipe for computing from the definition

1. Compute $f(a + h)$ — substitute $x$ by $a + h$ in the formula.
2. Subtract $f(a)$ and divide by $h$.
3. **Simplify algebraically** — cancel the $h$ from the denominator.
4. Take the limit $h \to 0$.

### Example: $f(x) = x^2$

$$f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{(a+h)^2 - a^2}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{a^2 + 2ah + h^2 - a^2}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{2ah + h^2}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} (2a + h) = 2a$$

Therefore $f'(x) = 2x$ — the derivative of $x^2$ is $2x$.

### When the derivative does not exist

- **Corner**: $|x|$ at $x = 0$ — from the left $f'_-(0) = -1$, from the right $f'_+(0) = +1$. They differ: no derivative.
- **Discontinuity**: if $f$ jumps, it cannot have a well-defined tangent.

<Insight>

The derivative is the central concept of Calculus. Velocity (derivative of position), acceleration (derivative of velocity), marginal cost (derivative of cost), instantaneous interest rate (derivative of capital) — all are derivatives. Learning the definition via limits is learning the DNA of these ideas.

</Insight>

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

The limit definition is the **foundation**. The differentiation rules (Lesson 52) and the chain rule (Lesson 53) are theorems proved from this definition. You need to master it not only for computations, but to **prove results**.

### Proof: differentiability implies continuity

Assume $f'(a)$ exists. Write:

$$f(a+h) - f(a) = \frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h} \cdot h \;\xrightarrow{h \to 0}\; f'(a) \cdot 0 = 0$$

The limit step uses: product of a finite limit by $h \to 0$. Therefore $\lim_{h \to 0} f(a+h) = f(a)$, i.e., $f$ is continuous at $a$. $\square$

### One-sided differentiability

$$f'_+(a) = \lim_{h \to 0^+} \frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h}, \qquad f'_-(a) = \lim_{h \to 0^-} \frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h}$$

$f$ is differentiable at $a$ if and only if $f'_+(a) = f'_-(a)$ (and both are finite).

### Derivatives from the definition — complete computations

**$f(x) = 1/x$:** For $a \neq 0$:

$$f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\frac{1}{a+h} - \frac{1}{a}}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\frac{a - (a+h)}{a(a+h)}}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{-h}{h \cdot a(a+h)} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{-1}{a(a+h)} = \frac{-1}{a^2}$$

**$f(x) = \sqrt{x}$:** For $a > 0$:

$$f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\sqrt{a+h} - \sqrt{a}}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{(\sqrt{a+h} - \sqrt{a})(\sqrt{a+h}+\sqrt{a})}{h(\sqrt{a+h}+\sqrt{a})} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{h}{h(\sqrt{a+h}+\sqrt{a})} = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{a}}$$

### Important pathologies

- $f(x) = x^2 \sin(1/x)$, $f(0) = 0$: differentiable at $0$ ($f'(0) = 0$), but $f'$ is not continuous at $0$.
- **Weierstrass function**: continuous on $\mathbb{R}$ but differentiable nowhere. Continuity $\not\Rightarrow$ differentiability.
- $f(x) = \sqrt[3]{x}$: $f'(0) = +\infty$ — vertical tangent, not differentiable in the classical sense.

### Cross-domain

- **Physics:** $v = ds/dt$, $a = dv/dt$, force $F = dp/dt$ (Newton's 2nd law in derivative form).
- **Quantitative finance:** continuous return $r = d(\ln S)/dt$; option delta $\Delta = \partial V/\partial S$.
- **Probability:** $f(x) = F'(x)$ — probability density as the derivative of the CDF.
- **Machine learning:** gradient descent — $\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\, \nabla L$; backpropagation uses derivatives at each weight.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

The Lagrange/Newton derivative is the first step on a ladder of generalizations that underpins all of modern applied mathematics.

### The derivative as a linear operator

The differentiation operator $D: C^1(I) \to C^0(I)$ is **linear** ($D(af+bg) = aD(f)+bD(g)$) but **unbounded** in the $C^0$ norm: take $f_n(x) = \sin(nx)/n$ — it converges uniformly to $0$, yet $f_n'(x) = \cos(nx)$ does not converge. This is why well-posed PDEs require Sobolev spaces $W^{k,p}$.

### Generalizations

- **Fréchet** (Banach): $f: X \to Y$ is differentiable at $x$ if there exists a continuous linear operator $A: X \to Y$ with $\|f(x+h) - f(x) - Ah\|_Y / \|h\|_X \to 0$. $A = Df(x)$ — the Jacobian in the finite-dimensional case.
- **Gâteaux** (directional): $D_v f(x) = \lim_{t\to 0}[f(x+tv)-f(x)]/t$. Weaker than Fréchet; useful in non-smooth optimization.
- **Distributional** (Schwartz): $\langle T', \varphi\rangle = -\langle T, \varphi'\rangle$. Allows differentiating the Heaviside function (result: Dirac delta). The foundation of modern PDEs.
- **Automatic (autodiff):** forward mode (jets / dual numbers) and reverse mode (cotangents); implemented in JAX/PyTorch. Cost of the gradient $\approx 2\times$ cost of the function — Baur–Strassen theorem.

### Itô calculus and finance

For an Itô process $dS_t = \mu S_t\,dt + \sigma S_t\,dW_t$, the classical derivative $dS/dt$ does not exist (paths are $\alpha$-Hölder for $\alpha < 1/2$ a.s.). One uses Itô's formula:

$$df(S_t) = f'(S_t)\,dS_t + \tfrac{1}{2}f''(S_t)\,\sigma^2 S_t^2\,dt$$

The $\frac{1}{2}f''$ term (second-order correction) is what separates Itô from classical calculus and is the origin of the $\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2$ term in the Black–Scholes equation (Lesson 119).

### Numerical finite differences

- Forward: $f'(a) \approx \frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h}$, error $O(h)$.
- Backward: $f'(a) \approx \frac{f(a)-f(a-h)}{h}$, error $O(h)$.
- Central: $f'(a) \approx \frac{f(a+h)-f(a-h)}{2h}$, error $O(h^2)$ — always prefer for numerical work.
- In IEEE-754 floating point, $h \approx \sqrt{\varepsilon_{\text{mach}}} \approx 10^{-8}$ minimizes total error for forward differences (trade-off between truncation error and cancellation error).

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practical application — when to use the definition">

### When you need the definition (not the rules)

1. **Verifying differentiability at suspect points** (piecewise junctions, corners, the origin).
2. **Proving that a function is not differentiable** — compute one-sided derivatives and show they differ.
3. **Proving rules** (product, quotient, chain) — done once; you use the result thereafter.
4. **Numerical finite differences** — when $f$ is a black box with no analytic formula.

### Workflow for diagnosing differentiability

1. Check that $f$ is defined at $a$ and is continuous (necessary condition).
2. Compute $f'_-(a)$ (left-hand limit) and $f'_+(a)$ (right-hand limit) separately.
3. Equal and finite? **Differentiable.** Different? **Corner.** Both $\pm\infty$? **Vertical tangent.** Diverge in distinct ways? **Not differentiable.**

### Symbolic computation — SymPy

```python
import sympy as sp
x, h, a = sp.symbols('x h a')
f = x**3 - 2*x
deriv = sp.limit((f.subs(x, a + h) - f.subs(x, a)) / h, h, 0)
print(deriv)  # 3*a**2 - 2
```

### Classic mistakes

- Canceling $h$ **before** combining the numerator terms — always expand first.
- Taking the limit $h \to 0$ **before** canceling $h$ from the denominator — the quotient is still $0/0$; you must eliminate $h$ algebraically first.
- Confusing $f'(a)$ (a number — the derivative at a point) with $f'(x)$ (a function — the derivative function).
- Forgetting that $h \to 0$ is two-sided — the one-sided derivatives must agree.

### Immediate applications

| Context | $f(t)$ | $f'(t)$ |
|---|---|---|
| Mechanics | Position $s(t)$ | Velocity $v(t)$ |
| Mechanics | Velocity $v(t)$ | Acceleration $a(t)$ |
| Finance | Cost $C(q)$ | Marginal cost $C'(q)$ |
| Electricity | Charge $q(t)$ | Current $i(t) = dq/dt$ |
| Biology | Population $P(t)$ | Growth rate $P'(t)$ |

## Worked examples

<Exemplo titulo="Derivative of $x^2$ from the definition" numero="1">

**Problem:** Use the definition of the derivative to compute $f'(x)$ for $f(x) = x^2$.

**Strategy:** Apply $f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h}$ directly and simplify algebraically to cancel $h$ from the denominator before taking the limit.

**Solution:**

1. **Compute $f(a+h)$:**
$$f(a+h) = (a+h)^2 = a^2 + 2ah + h^2$$

2. **Form the difference quotient:**
$$\frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h} = \frac{a^2 + 2ah + h^2 - a^2}{h} = \frac{2ah + h^2}{h}$$

3. **Cancel $h$** (valid because $h \neq 0$ in the limit process):
$$\frac{2ah + h^2}{h} = \frac{h(2a + h)}{h} = 2a + h$$

4. **Take the limit:**
$$f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0}(2a + h) = 2a$$

Since $a$ is arbitrary, the derivative function is $f'(x) = 2x$.

**Verification:** At $x = 3$, $f'(3) = 6$. The tangent line passes through $(3, 9)$ with slope 6: $y = 6x - 9$. Check: $f(3.1) = 9.61$ and $y(3.1) = 6(3.1) - 9 = 9.6$ — the tangent approximates the graph well for small $h$. $\checkmark$

**Source.** [Active Calculus, §1.3, Activity 1.3.2](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-1-3-derivative-pt.html) — license CC-BY-SA 4.0.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Derivative of $1/x$ from the definition" numero="2">

**Problem:** Compute $f'(a)$ for $f(x) = \dfrac{1}{x}$, with $a \neq 0$, using the definition.

**Strategy:** The critical step is combining the fractions in the numerator into a single fraction, which allows canceling $h$ from the denominator.

**Solution:**

1. **Difference quotient:**
$$\frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h} = \frac{\dfrac{1}{a+h} - \dfrac{1}{a}}{h}$$

2. **Combine the fractions in the numerator** (common denominator $a(a+h)$):
$$= \frac{\dfrac{a - (a+h)}{a(a+h)}}{h} = \frac{-h}{h \cdot a(a+h)}$$

3. **Cancel $h$:**
$$= \frac{-1}{a(a+h)}$$

4. **Take the limit $h \to 0$:**
$$f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{-1}{a(a+h)} = \frac{-1}{a \cdot a} = -\frac{1}{a^2}$$

**Result:** $(1/x)' = -1/x^2$. Geometrically: the hyperbola $y = 1/x$ has a tangent with negative slope for every $x \neq 0$ — the function is always decreasing on the half-planes $x > 0$ and $x < 0$.

**Verification:** At $a = 2$: $f'(2) = -1/4$. Tangent line: $y - 1/2 = -\tfrac{1}{4}(x-2) \Rightarrow y = -x/4 + 1$. At $x = 2.1$: tangent gives $0.475$; $f(2.1) = 1/2.1 \approx 0.476$. $\checkmark$

**Source.** [OpenStax Calculus Volume 1, §3.1, Example 3.6](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/3-1-defining-the-derivative) — license CC-BY-NC-SA 4.0.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Equation of the tangent line to $x^3$" numero="3">

**Problem:** Find the equation of the tangent line to the graph of $f(x) = x^3$ at the point $x = 2$.

**Strategy:** The tangent line has equation $y - f(a) = f'(a)(x - a)$. We need $f(a)$ and $f'(a)$.

**Solution:**

1. **Compute $f(2) = 2^3 = 8$**. The point of tangency is $(2, 8)$.

2. **Compute $f'(a)$ from the definition:**
$$\frac{(a+h)^3 - a^3}{h} = \frac{a^3 + 3a^2h + 3ah^2 + h^3 - a^3}{h} = \frac{3a^2h + 3ah^2 + h^3}{h} = 3a^2 + 3ah + h^2$$

$$f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0}(3a^2 + 3ah + h^2) = 3a^2$$

Therefore $f'(2) = 3 \cdot 4 = 12$.

3. **Equation of the tangent line:**
$$y - 8 = 12(x - 2) \implies y = 12x - 16$$

**Verification:** At $x = 2.1$: $f(2.1) = (2.1)^3 = 9.261$; tangent: $y = 12(2.1) - 16 = 9.2$. Absolute error $0.061$ — grows with $h^2$ as expected for a linear approximation. $\checkmark$

**Source.** [APEX Calculus, §2.1, Example 2.1.3](https://www.apexcalculus.com/) — license CC-BY-NC 4.0.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Non-differentiability: the corner of the absolute value" numero="4">

**Problem:** Investigate whether $f(x) = |x|$ is differentiable at $x = 0$.

**Strategy:** Compute the one-sided derivatives $f'_-(0)$ and $f'_+(0)$ separately. If they differ, $f$ is not differentiable at $0$.

**Solution:**

1. **Right-hand derivative** ($h \to 0^+$, so $h > 0$, hence $|h| = h$):
$$f'_+(0) = \lim_{h \to 0^+} \frac{|0+h| - |0|}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0^+} \frac{h}{h} = 1$$

2. **Left-hand derivative** ($h \to 0^-$, so $h < 0$, hence $|h| = -h$):
$$f'_-(0) = \lim_{h \to 0^-} \frac{|0+h| - |0|}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0^-} \frac{-h}{h} = -1$$

3. **Conclusion:** $f'_+(0) = 1 \neq -1 = f'_-(0)$.

Since the one-sided derivatives differ, $f(x) = |x|$ **is not differentiable at $x = 0$** — there is a corner in the graph. Note that $f$ is continuous at $0$ (since $\lim_{x\to 0}|x| = 0 = |0|$), confirming that **continuity does not imply differentiability**.

**Verification:** The graph of $|x|$ has slope $+1$ for $x > 0$ and $-1$ for $x < 0$. There is no unique tangent line at $x = 0$. $\checkmark$

**Source.** [Active Calculus, §1.7, Activity 1.7.3](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-1-7-lim-cont-diff.html) — license CC-BY-SA 4.0.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Marginal cost — economic application" numero="5">

**Problem:** A company produces $q$ units of a product with total cost $C(q) = q^2 + 30q + 500$ (in dollars). Compute the marginal cost at $q = 50$ units using the definition of the derivative and interpret the result.

**Strategy:** Marginal cost = $C'(q)$, the instantaneous rate of change of cost with respect to quantity. We compute it from the definition and evaluate at $q = 50$.

**Solution:**

1. **Difference quotient:**
$$\frac{C(q+h) - C(q)}{h} = \frac{(q+h)^2 + 30(q+h) + 500 - (q^2 + 30q + 500)}{h}$$

$$= \frac{q^2 + 2qh + h^2 + 30q + 30h + 500 - q^2 - 30q - 500}{h} = \frac{2qh + h^2 + 30h}{h}$$

$$= 2q + h + 30$$

2. **Take the limit:**
$$C'(q) = \lim_{h \to 0}(2q + h + 30) = 2q + 30$$

3. **Evaluate at $q = 50$:**
$$C'(50) = 2(50) + 30 = 130 \text{ dollars}$$

**Interpretation:** When producing 50 units, each additional unit costs approximately $130. Note that $C(51) - C(50) = (2601 + 1530 + 500) - (2500 + 1500 + 500) = 4631 - 4500 = 131$ dollars — the marginal cost underestimates by $1 (a second-order difference in $h$).

**Verification:** $C'(q) = 2q + 30 > 0$ for all $q > 0$: costs are increasing, as expected. At $q = 0$: $C'(0) = 30$ — the marginal cost of the first unit is $30. $\checkmark$

**Source.** [OpenStax Calculus Volume 1, §3.1, Applied example — marginal cost](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/3-1-defining-the-derivative) — license CC-BY-NC-SA 4.0.
</Exemplo>
