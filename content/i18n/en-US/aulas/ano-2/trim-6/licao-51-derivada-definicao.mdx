### Definition of the Derivative

<Definicao titulo="Derivative at a Point">

Let $f: I \to \mathbb{R}$ be defined on an open interval $I$ and $a \in I$. The **derivative of $f$ at $a$** is the limit

$$f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h}$$

when this limit exists (and is finite). In this case, we say that $f$ is **differentiable** at $a$.

**Alternative form** (substitution $x = a + h$, $h = x - a$):

$$f'(a) = \lim_{x \to a} \frac{f(x) - f(a)}{x - a}$$

</Definicao>

> "We say that a function $f$ is differentiable at $x = a$ whenever $f'(a)$ exists. […] The derivative measures the instantaneous rate of change of the function, as well as the slope of the tangent line to the function at the given point." — [Boelkins, Active Calculus §1.3](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-1-3-derivative-pt.html)

> "The derivative of a function $f(x)$ at a point $a$ in its domain, if it exists, is $f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0}\frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h}$." — [OpenStax Calculus Vol. 1, §3.1](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/3-1-defining-the-derivative)

### Equivalent Notations

<Equation explicacao="All the notations below represent the same object — the derivative of f. Leibniz (dy/dx), Lagrange (f'), Newton (f with dot) and the operator D are the most commonly used." latex="f'(x) \;=\; \frac{df}{dx} \;=\; \frac{dy}{dx} \;=\; Df(x) \;=\; \dot{f}(x)" />

### Tangent Line and Normal Line

Given $f$ is differentiable at $a$:

- **Tangent line** at $(a, f(a))$: $\quad y - f(a) = f'(a)(x - a)$
- **Normal line** at $(a, f(a))$ (perpendicular to the tangent, if $f'(a) \neq 0$): $\quad y - f(a) = -\dfrac{1}{f'(a)}(x - a)$

### Fundamental Theorem of Differentiability

<Teorema titulo="Differentiability Implies Continuity">

If $f$ is differentiable at $a$, then $f$ is continuous at $a$.

**Proof.** Write $f(a+h) - f(a) = \dfrac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h} \cdot h$. As $h \to 0$, the first factor tends to $f'(a)$ (by hypothesis) and $h \to 0$. Thus $f(a+h) - f(a) \to f'(a) \cdot 0 = 0$, which means $\lim_{h \to 0} f(a+h) = f(a)$. $\square$

**The converse is false.** $f(x) = |x|$ is continuous at $0$ but not differentiable at $0$ (left and right derivatives $-1$ and $+1$ differ).

</Teorema>

> "If $f$ is differentiable at $a$, then $f$ is continuous at $a$. […] The converse is not true, and a function can be continuous but fail to be differentiable at a point." — [OpenStax Calculus Vol. 1, §3.2](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/3-2-the-derivative-as-a-function)

### Points of Non-Differentiability

<Definicao titulo="Classification of Pathological Points">

- **Cusp (corner)**: left and right derivatives exist but differ. Example: $|x|$ at $x = 0$.
- **Cusp**: $f'(x) \to \pm\infty$ from both sides. Example: $x^{2/3}$ at $x = 0$.
- **Vertical tangent**: $f'(a) = +\infty$ (or $-\infty$). Example: $\sqrt[3]{x}$ at $x = 0$.
- **Discontinuity**: if $f$ is not continuous at $a$, it is certainly not differentiable.

</Definicao>

### Fundamental Derivatives via Definition

| Function $f(x)$ | $f'(x)$ |
|---|---|
| $c$ (constant) | $0$ |
| $x$ | $1$ |
| $x^2$ | $2x$ |
| $x^3$ | $3x^2$ |
| $x^n$ ($n \in \mathbb{Z}$) | $n x^{n-1}$ |
| $\dfrac{1}{x}$ | $-\dfrac{1}{x^2}$ |
| $\sqrt{x}$ | $\dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}$ |

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-Year-Old">

Imagine you're in a car going from one city to another. Sometimes the car goes **slowly**, sometimes it goes **fast**.

The car's speedometer shows the speed **right now**, at this exact second.

The **derivative** is like a mathematical speedometer. It tells you "how much this function is changing right now." If the function goes up fast, the derivative is large. If the function is stopped, the derivative is zero.

Think of it this way: imagine you walk 100 meters in 10 seconds. Your **average speed** is 10 meters per second. But did you walk at exactly the same pace the whole time? Maybe you ran at the beginning and walked slowly at the end.

The **exact speed** at a specific second — that's the derivative.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-Year-Old">

You already know how to calculate **average speed**: distance divided by time. I walked 100 meters in 10 seconds, average speed = 10 m/s.

But what about the speed **at this exact instant**? For that, you take a very small time interval, calculate the average speed over that interval, and keep making the interval smaller and smaller.

The **derivative** is what happens when the interval becomes infinitely small — it's the "instantaneous speed" of the function.

**Practical example:** if your position is $s(t) = t^2$ (meters), what happens at $t = 3$ s?

- 1 s interval: $\frac{s(4) - s(3)}{1} = \frac{16 - 9}{1} = 7$ m/s
- 0.1 s interval: $\frac{s(3{,}1) - s(3)}{0{,}1} = \frac{9{,}61 - 9}{0{,}1} = 6{,}1$ m/s
- 0.01 s interval: $\frac{s(3{,}01) - s(3)}{0{,}01} = \frac{9{,}0601 - 9}{0{,}01} = 6{,}01$ m/s
- In the limit: exact speed = **6 m/s**.

The number 6 is the derivative of $s(t) = t^2$ at the point $t = 3$.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-Year-Old">

The **average rate of change** of $f$ between $a$ and $a + h$ is:

$$\frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h}$$

Geometrically, this is the slope of the **secant line** that passes through the points $(a, f(a))$ and $(a+h, f(a+h))$.

As $h \to 0$, the secant "rotates" and approaches the **tangent line** to the graph at $(a, f(a))$. The slope of this tangent line is the derivative $f'(a)$.

### Recipe for Calculating by Definition

1. Compute $f(a + h)$ — substitute $x$ with $a + h$ in the formula.
2. Subtract $f(a)$ and divide by $h$.
3. **Simplify algebraically** — cancel out the $h$ in the denominator.
4. Take the limit $h \to 0$.

### Example: $f(x) = x^2$

$$f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{(a+h)^2 - a^2}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{a^2 + 2ah + h^2 - a^2}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{2ah + h^2}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} (2a + h) = 2a$$

Thus $f'(x) = 2x$ — the derivative of $x^2$ is $2x$.

### When the Derivative Does Not Exist

- **Cusp**: $|x|$ at $x = 0$ — from the left $f'_-(0) = -1$, from the right $f'_+(0) = +1$. Different: no derivative.
- **Discontinuity**: if $f$ jumps, it cannot have a defined tangent.

<Insight>

The derivative is the central concept of Calculus. Velocity (derivative of position), acceleration (derivative of velocity), marginal cost (derivative of cost), instantaneous interest rate (derivative of capital) — all are derivatives. Learning the definition via limit is learning the DNA of these ideas.

</Insight>

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an Engineering Student">

The definition via limit is the **foundation**. The differentiation rules (Lesson 52) and the chain rule (Lesson 53) are theorems proven from this definition. You need to master it not only for calculations, but also to **demonstrate results**.

### Proof: Differentiability Implies Continuity

Suppose $f'(a)$ exists. Write:

$$f(a+h) - f(a) = \frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h} \cdot h \;\xrightarrow{h \to 0}\; f'(a) \cdot 0 = 0$$

The limit passage uses: product of a finite limit by $h \to 0$. Thus $\lim_{h \to 0} f(a+h) = f(a)$, i.e., $f$ is continuous at $a$. $\square$

### One-Sided Differentiability

$$f'_+(a) = \lim_{h \to 0^+} \frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h}, \qquad f'_-(a) = \lim_{h \to 0^-} \frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h}$$

$f$ is differentiable at $a$ if and only if $f'_+(a) = f'_-(a)$ (and both are finite).

### Derivatives via Definition — Complete Calculations

**$f(x) = 1/x$:** For $a \neq 0$:

$$f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\frac{1}{a+h} - \frac{1}{a}}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\frac{a - (a+h)}{a(a+h)}}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{-h}{h \cdot a(a+h)} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{-1}{a(a+h)} = \frac{-1}{a^2}$$

**$f(x) = \sqrt{x}$:** For $a > 0$:

$$f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\sqrt{a+h} - \sqrt{a}}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{(\sqrt{a+h} - \sqrt{a})(\sqrt{a+h}+\sqrt{a})}{h(\sqrt{a+h}+\sqrt{a})} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{h}{h(\sqrt{a+h}+\sqrt{a})} = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{a}}$$

### Important Pathologies

- $f(x) = x^2 \sin(1/x)$, $f(0) = 0$: differentiable at $0$ ($f'(0) = 0$), but $f'$ is not continuous at $0$.
- **Weierstrass function**: continuous on $\mathbb{R}$, but not differentiable at any point. Continuity $\not\Rightarrow$ differentiability.
- $f(x) = \sqrt[3]{x}$: $f'(0) = +\infty$ — vertical tangent, not differentiable in the classical sense.

### Cross-Domain

- **Physics:** $v = ds/dt$, $a = dv/dt$, force $F = dp/dt$ (Newton's 2nd law in derivative form).
- **Quantitative Finance:** continuous return $r = d(\ln S)/dt$; option delta $\Delta = \partial V/\partial S$.
- **Probability:** $f(x) = F'(x)$ — probability density as the derivative of the CDF.
- **Machine Learning:** gradient descent — $\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\, \nabla L$; backpropagation uses derivatives for each weight.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a Senior Professional">

The Lagrange/Newton derivative is the first step on a ladder of generalizations that underpins all modern applied mathematics.

### The Derivative as a Linear Operator

The differentiation operator $D: C^1(I) \to C^0(I)$ is **linear** ($D(af+bg) = aD(f)+bD(g)$) but **unbounded** in $C^0$ norm: take $f_n(x) = \sin(nx)/n$ — it converges uniformly to $0$, but $f_n'(x) = \cos(nx)$ does not converge. This is why well-posed PDEs require Sobolev spaces $W^{k,p}$.

### Generalizations

- **Fréchet** (Banach): $f: X \to Y$ is differentiable at $x$ if there exists a continuous linear operator $A: X \to Y$ with $\|f(x+h) - f(x) - Ah\|_Y / \|h\|_X \to 0$. $A = Df(x)$ — Jacobian in the finite-dimensional case.
- **Gâteaux** (directional): $D_v f(x) = \lim_{t\to 0}[f(x+tv)-f(x)]/t$. Weaker than Fréchet; useful in non-smooth optimization.
- **Distributional** (Schwartz): $\langle T', \varphi\rangle = -\langle T, \varphi'\rangle$. Allows differentiating Heaviside (result: Dirac delta). Basis of modern PDEs.
- **Automatic (autodiff):** forward mode (jets / dual numbers) and reverse mode (cotangents); implementation in JAX/PyTorch. Gradient cost $\approx 2\times$ function cost — Baur–Strassen theorem.

### Itô Calculus and Finance

For an Itô process $dS_t = \mu S_t\,dt + \sigma S_t\,dW_t$, the classical derivative $dS/dt$ does not exist (paths are $\alpha$-Hölder for $\alpha < 1/2$ a.s.). Itô's formula is used:

$$df(S_t) = f'(S_t)\,dS_t + \tfrac{1}{2}f''(S_t)\,\sigma^2 S_t^2\,dt$$

The term $\frac{1}{2}f''$ (second-order correction) is what separates Itô from classical calculus and is the origin of the $\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2$ term in the Black–Scholes equation (Lesson 119).

### Numerical Finite Differences

- Forward: $f'(a) \approx \frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h}$, error $O(h)$.
- Backward: $f'(a) \approx \frac{f(a)-f(a-h)}{h}$, error $O(h)$.
- Central: $f'(a) \approx \frac{f(a+h)-f(a-h)}{2h}$, error $O(h^2)$ — always prefer for numerical calculation.
- In IEEE-754 floating point, $h \approx \sqrt{\varepsilon_{\text{mach}}} \approx 10^{-8}$ minimizes the total error for forward difference (trade-off between truncation error and cancellation error).

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practical Application — When to Use the Definition">

### When You Need the Definition (and Not the Rules)

1. **Verify differentiability at suspicious points** (piecewise junctions, cusps, origin).
2. **Prove that a function is not differentiable** — calculate one-sided derivatives and show they diverge.
3. **Demonstrate rules** (product, quotient, chain) — done once; then you use the result.
4. **Numerical finite differences** — when $f$ is a black box without an analytical formula.

### Workflow for Diagnosing Differentiability

1. Check if $f$ is defined at $a$ and if it is continuous (necessary condition).
2. Calculate $f'_-(a)$ (left-hand limit) and $f'_+(a)$ (right-hand limit) separately.
3. Equal and finite? **Differentiable.** Different? **Cusp.** Both $\pm\infty$? **Vertical tangent.** Diverge distinctly? **Not differentiable.**

### Symbolic Computation — SymPy

```python
import sympy as sp
x, h, a = sp.symbols('x h a')
f = x**3 - 2*x
deriv = sp.limit((f.subs(x, a + h) - f.subs(x, a)) / h, h, 0)
print(deriv)  # 3*a**2 - 2
```

### Classic Errors

- Canceling $h$ **before** combining the terms in the numerator — always expand first.
- Taking the limit $h \to 0$ **before** canceling $h$ in the denominator — the quotient is still $0/0$; you need to eliminate $h$ algebraically first.
- Confusing $f'(a)$ (number — derivative at a point) with $f'(x)$ (function — derivative function).
- Forgetting that $h \to 0$ is bilateral — the one-sided derivatives must coincide.

## Solved Examples

<Exemplo titulo="Derivative of $x^2$ by Definition" numero="1">

**Problem:** Use the definition of the derivative to calculate $f'(x)$ for $f(x) = x^2$.

**Strategy:** We directly apply $f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h}$ and simplify algebraically to eliminate $h$ from the denominator before taking the limit.

**Solution:**

1. **Calculate $f(a+h)$:**
$$f(a+h) = (a+h)^2 = a^2 + 2ah + h^2$$

2. **Set up the incremental quotient:**
$$\frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h} = \frac{a^2 + 2ah + h^2 - a^2}{h} = \frac{2ah + h^2}{h}$$

3. **Cancel $h$** (valid because $h \neq 0$ in the limit process):
$$\frac{2ah + h^2}{h} = \frac{h(2a + h)}{h} = 2a + h$$

4. **Take the limit:**
$$f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0}(2a + h) = 2a$$

Since $a$ is arbitrary, the derivative function is $f'(x) = 2x$.

**Verification:** At $x = 3$, $f'(3) = 6$. The tangent line passes through $(3, 9)$ with a slope of 6: $y = 6x - 9$. Check point: $f(3{,}1) = 9{,}61$ and $y(3{,}1) = 6(3{,}1) - 9 = 9{,}6$ — the tangent approximates the graph well for small $h$. $\checkmark$

**Source.** [Active Calculus, §1.3, Activity 1.3.2](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-1-3-derivative-pt.html) — CC-BY-SA 4.0 license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Derivative of $1/x$ by Definition" numero="2">

**Problem:** Calculate $f'(a)$ for $f(x) = \dfrac{1}{x}$, with $a \neq 0$, using the definition.

**Strategy:** The critical step is to combine the fractions in the numerator into a single fraction, which allows canceling $h$ from the denominator.

**Solution:**

1. **Incremental quotient:**
$$\frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h} = \frac{\dfrac{1}{a+h} - \dfrac{1}{a}}{h}$$

2. **Combine the fractions in the numerator** (least common denominator $a(a+h)$):
$$= \frac{\dfrac{a - (a+h)}{a(a+h)}}{h} = \frac{-h}{h \cdot a(a+h)}$$

3. **Cancel $h$:**
$$= \frac{-1}{a(a+h)}$$

4. **Take the limit $h \to 0$:**
$$f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{-1}{a(a+h)} = \frac{-1}{a \cdot a} = -\frac{1}{a^2}$$

**Result:** $(1/x)' = -1/x^2$. Geometrically: the hyperbola $y = 1/x$ has a tangent with a negative slope for all $x \neq 0$ — the function is always decreasing in the half-planes $x > 0$ and $x < 0$.

**Verification:** At $a = 2$: $f'(2) = -1/4$. Tangent line: $y - 1/2 = -\tfrac{1}{4}(x-2) \Rightarrow y = -x/4 + 1$. At $x = 2{,}1$: tangent gives $0{,}475$; $f(2{,}1) = 1/2{,}1 \approx 0{,}476$. $\checkmark$

**Source.** [OpenStax Calculus Volume 1, §3.1, Example 3.6](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/3-1-defining-the-derivative) — CC-BY-NC-SA 4.0 license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Equation of the Tangent Line to $x^3$" numero="3">

**Problem:** Find the equation of the tangent line to the graph of $f(x) = x^3$ at the point $x = 2$.

**Strategy:** The tangent line has equation $y - f(a) = f'(a)(x - a)$. We need $f(a)$ and $f'(a)$.

**Solution:**

1. **Calculate $f(2) = 2^3 = 8$**. The point of tangency is $(2, 8)$.

2. **Calculate $f'(a)$ via definition:**
$$\frac{(a+h)^3 - a^3}{h} = \frac{a^3 + 3a^2h + 3ah^2 + h^3 - a^3}{h} = \frac{3a^2h + 3ah^2 + h^3}{h} = 3a^2 + 3ah + h^2$$

$$f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0}(3a^2 + 3ah + h^2) = 3a^2$$

Thus $f'(2) = 3 \cdot 4 = 12$.

3. **Equation of the tangent line:**
$$y - 8 = 12(x - 2) \implies y = 12x - 16$$

**Verification:** At $x = 2{,}1$: $f(2{,}1) = (2{,}1)^3 = 9{,}261$; tangent: $y = 12(2{,}1) - 16 = 9{,}2$. Absolute error $0{,}061$ — grows with $h^2$ as expected for a linear approximation. $\checkmark$

**Source.** [APEX Calculus, §2.1, Example 2.1.3](https://www.apexcalculus.com/) — CC-BY-NC 4.0 license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Non-Differentiability: The Absolute Value Cusp" numero="4">

**Problem:** Investigate whether $f(x) = |x|$ is differentiable at $x = 0$.

**Strategy:** We calculate the left-hand and right-hand derivatives $f'_-(0)$ and $f'_+(0)$ separately. If they differ, $f$ is not differentiable at $0$.

**Solution:**

1. **Right-hand derivative** ($h \to 0^+$, so $h > 0$, thus $|h| = h$):
$$f'_+(0) = \lim_{h \to 0^+} \frac{|0+h| - |0|}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0^+} \frac{h}{h} = 1$$

2. **Left-hand derivative** ($h \to 0^-$, so $h < 0$, thus $|h| = -h$):
$$f'_-(0) = \lim_{h \to 0^-} \frac{|0+h| - |0|}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0^-} \frac{-h}{h} = -1$$

3. **Conclusion:** $f'_+(0) = 1 \neq -1 = f'_-(0)$.

Since the one-sided derivatives differ, $f(x) = |x|$ **is not differentiable at $x = 0$** — there is a cusp in the graph. Note that $f$ is continuous at $0$ (since $\lim_{x\to 0}|x| = 0 = |0|$), confirming that **continuity does not imply differentiability**.

**Verification:** The graph of $|x|$ has a slope of $+1$ for $x > 0$ and $-1$ for $x < 0$. There is no unique tangent line at $x = 0$. $\checkmark$

**Source.** [Active Calculus, §1.7, Activity 1.7.3](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-1-7-lim-cont-diff.html) — CC-BY-SA 4.0 license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Marginal Cost — Economic Application" numero="5">

**Problem:** A company produces $q$ units of a product with a total cost $C(q) = q^2 + 30q + 500$ R$. Calculate the marginal cost at $q = 50$ units using the definition of the derivative and interpret the result.

**Strategy:** Marginal cost = $C'(q)$, the instantaneous rate of change of cost with respect to quantity. We calculate it by definition and evaluate it at $q = 50$.

**Solution:**

1. **Incremental quotient:**
$$\frac{C(q+h) - C(q)}{h} = \frac{(q+h)^2 + 30(q+h) + 500 - (q^2 + 30q + 500)}{h}$$

$$= \frac{q^2 + 2qh + h^2 + 30q + 30h + 500 - q^2 - 30q - 500}{h} = \frac{2qh + h^2 + 30h}{h}$$

$$= 2q + h + 30$$

2. **Take the limit:**
$$C'(q) = \lim_{h \to 0}(2q + h + 30) = 2q + 30$$

3. **Evaluate at $q = 50$:**
$$C'(50) = 2(50) + 30 = 130 \text{ R\$}$$

**Interpretation:** When producing 50 units, each additional unit costs approximately R\$ 130. Note that $C(51) - C(50) = (2601 + 1530 + 500) - (2500 + 1500 + 500) = 4631 - 4500 = 131$ R\$ — the marginal cost underestimates by R\$ 1 (second-order difference in $h$).

**Verification:** $C'(q) = 2q + 30 > 0$ for all $q > 0$: increasing costs, as expected. At $q = 0$: $C'(0) = 30$ — marginal cost of the first unit is R\$ 30. $\checkmark$

**Source.** [OpenStax Calculus Volume 1, §3.1, Applied Example — marginal cost](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/3-1-defining-the-derivative) — CC-BY-NC-SA 4.0 license.
</Exemplo>
