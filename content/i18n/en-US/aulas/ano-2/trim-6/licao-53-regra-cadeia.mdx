### Formal Statement

<Definicao titulo="Chain Rule">

If $g$ is differentiable at $x$ and $f$ is differentiable at $g(x)$, then the composite function $h(x) = f(g(x))$ is differentiable at $x$ and:

<Equation numero="RC" explicacao="The derivative of the composition is the product of the outer derivative (evaluated at the inner point) and the inner derivative. Read from outside in." latex="\frac{d}{dx}[f(g(x))] = f'(g(x)) \cdot g'(x)" />

In Leibniz notation, defining $u = g(x)$ and $y = f(u)$:

<Equation numero="RC-Leibniz" explicacao="Leibniz notation makes the rule visually mnemonic: the du units formally 'cancel', like in a fraction." latex="\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy}{du} \cdot \frac{du}{dx}" />

</Definicao>

> "The chain rule states that the derivative of $f(g(x))$ is $f'(g(x)) \cdot g'(x)$. That is, we differentiate the outer function $f$, evaluate it at the inner function $g(x)$, and multiply by the derivative of the inner function." — [OpenStax Calculus Volume 1, §3.6](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/3-6-the-chain-rule)

> "Think of the process from outside in: identify the outer function, differentiate it while keeping the inner function unchanged, then multiply by the derivative of the inner function." — [Boelkins, Active Calculus §2.5](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-2-5-chain.html)

### Rigorous Proof

The difficulty lies in the fact that $g(x+h) - g(x)$ can be zero for $h \neq 0$, invalidating the naive argument of canceling $\Delta g$. The solution uses the auxiliary function:

$$Q(y) = \begin{cases} \dfrac{f(y) - f(g(a))}{y - g(a)} & y \neq g(a) \\ f'(g(a)) & y = g(a) \end{cases}$$

$Q$ is continuous at $g(a)$ (by differentiability of $f$). Since $f(g(a+h)) - f(g(a)) = Q(g(a+h)) \cdot [g(a+h) - g(a)]$, dividing by $h$ and taking the limit $h \to 0$ yields $(f \circ g)'(a) = f'(g(a)) \cdot g'(a)$.

### Fundamental Special Cases

| Composite Function | Derivative |
|---|---|
| $[g(x)]^n$ | $n\,[g(x)]^{n-1} \cdot g'(x)$ |
| $\sin(g(x))$ | $\cos(g(x)) \cdot g'(x)$ |
| $\cos(g(x))$ | $-\sin(g(x)) \cdot g'(x)$ |
| $e^{g(x)}$ | $e^{g(x)} \cdot g'(x)$ |
| $\ln(g(x))$ | $g'(x)/g(x)$ |
| $\sqrt{g(x)}$ | $g'(x) / (2\sqrt{g(x)})$ |
| $a^{g(x)}$ | $a^{g(x)} \ln a \cdot g'(x)$ |

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

There's a **machine inside another machine**. You put a fruit into the outer machine. It passes the fruit to the inner machine, and juice comes out.

To know **how much the juice changes** when you change the fruit, you multiply:

- how much the juice changes when the pulp changes
- how much the pulp changes when you change the fruit

It's like a **chain**: each link passes the change to the next. More links, more multiplications.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

Imagine you're pedaling. Each pedal stroke turns the wheel once. Each wheel turn moves you a certain distance down the road.

To know the total speed (how many meters you move per pedal stroke):

- How many meters the road advances per **wheel turn**
- How many turns the wheel makes per **pedal stroke**

Multiply the two. This is the chain rule.

In formula:

$$\frac{\text{meters}}{\text{pedal stroke}} = \frac{\text{meters}}{\text{turn}} \times \frac{\text{turns}}{\text{pedal stroke}}$$

It's exactly the same as $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{dy}{du} \cdot \dfrac{du}{dx}$.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old">

The chain rule is the **most used** differentiation rule in practice. Almost every real-world function is a composition of simpler functions.

### 3-Step Recipe

**Step 1.** Identify the outer layer $f$ and the inner layer $g$: write the function in the form $f(g(x))$.

**Step 2.** Calculate the derivative of $f$ treating $g(x)$ as the variable — evaluate at $g(x)$, not $x$.

**Step 3.** Multiply by the derivative of $g(x)$.

### Example: $((x^2 + 1)^5)'$

- Inner: $g(x) = x^2 + 1$, derivative $g'(x) = 2x$.
- Outer: $f(u) = u^5$, derivative $f'(u) = 5u^4$, evaluated at $g(x)$: $5(x^2+1)^4$.
- **Result:** $5(x^2+1)^4 \cdot 2x = 10x(x^2+1)^4$.

### Example: $(\ln(\sin x))'$

- Inner: $g(x) = \sin x$, derivative $\cos x$.
- Outer: $f(u) = \ln u$, derivative $1/u$, evaluated at $\sin x$: $1/\sin x$.
- **Result:** $\dfrac{\cos x}{\sin x} = \cot x$.

<Insight>

<strong>The most common mistake:</strong> Differentiating $(e^{x^2})'$ as $xe^{x^2}$ — forgetting to multiply by $2x$ (the derivative of the exponent). The correct result is $2x e^{x^2}$. Whenever there's "something inside," multiply by the derivative of what's inside.

</Insight>

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

The chain rule is the **vehicle for all variable transformations** in calculus. Without it, integration by substitution, separable differential equations, and any change of coordinates are impossible.

### Direct Applications

<strong>Substitution in integrals:</strong> $\displaystyle\int f(g(x))\,g'(x)\,dx = \int f(u)\,du$ where $u = g(x)$. The chain rule is integration by substitution read backward.

<strong>Differential equations:</strong> For $y' = ky$, the solution $y = Ce^{kx}$ is verified via the chain rule: $(Ce^{kx})' = Ce^{kx} \cdot k = ky$.

<strong>Physics:</strong> $\dfrac{dE}{dt} = \dfrac{dE}{dx} \cdot \dfrac{dx}{dt} = F \cdot v$. Power = force times velocity. This is the chain rule in physical language.

<strong>Backpropagation:</strong> each layer of a neural network propagates gradients exactly via the chain rule applied backward (reverse-mode autodiff).

### Chain Rule in Parametric Curves

If $x = x(t)$ and $y = y(t)$:

<Equation explicacao="Derivative of y with respect to x via parameterization. Useful in kinematics and implicit curves." latex="\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy/dt}{dx/dt}" />

### Multivariate Preview

In $\mathbb{R}^n$, the chain rule involves Jacobians:

$$\nabla (f \circ g)(x) = [Jg(x)]^\top \nabla f(g(x))$$

where $Jg$ is the Jacobian matrix of $g$. Each output coordinate chains with each input coordinate.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

The chain rule is the **central law of differentiation** in any differentiable space.

### Categorical View

In the category of smooth manifolds with smooth maps as morphisms, differentiation $D$ is a **covariant functor**: $D(f \circ g) = Df \circ Dg$ (composition of pushforwards, i.e., composition of Jacobians). This is the coordinate-free formulation of the chain rule.

### Backpropagation as Reverse Chain Rule

In a neural network with layers $f_1, \ldots, f_L$ and loss $\ell$, the gradient with respect to parameter $\theta_k$ of layer $k$ is:

$$\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \theta_k} = \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial a_L} \cdot \frac{\partial a_L}{\partial a_{L-1}} \cdots \frac{\partial a_{k+1}}{\partial a_k} \cdot \frac{\partial a_k}{\partial \theta_k}$$

The <strong>reverse mode</strong> (back-to-front) is asymptotically optimal when $\dim(\text{output}) \ll \dim(\text{params})$ — which is the universal case in ML.

### Black–Scholes Greeks via Chain Rule

The Delta of an option: $\Delta = \partial V / \partial S$. When $V = f(d_+(S))$ and $d_+$ is a function of $S$, use the chain rule: $\Delta = f'(d_+) \cdot \partial d_+ / \partial S$. The entire family of Greeks (Delta, Gamma, Vega, Theta) is the chain rule applied to $V(S, t, \sigma, r)$.

### Adjoint Method

In PDE optimization, the <strong>adjoint method</strong> is the continuous reverse chain rule: for $J(\theta) = \int_0^T \ell(u(t;\theta)) dt$ where $u$ satisfies a PDE dependent on $\theta$, the gradient $\nabla_\theta J$ is computed via the adjoint equation (a PDE run "backward in time"). Cost: 1 forward PDE + 1 backward PDE, independent of $\dim(\theta)$.

### Pathological Case

The chain rule requires differentiability. Lipschitz functions are differentiable almost everywhere (Rademacher), but the chain rule may fail at non-differentiable points. In spaces of distributions ($\mathcal{D}'$), there is no canonical product of distributions, and the chain rule does not generalize directly.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practical Application">

### Scenario 1: Social Security Adjustment and Effective Rate Impact

The effective Social Security contribution rate for a salary $s$ (in Brazilian Reais) in 2025 is calculated through progressive brackets, resulting approximately in a composite function $A(s) = f(g(s))$, where $g(s)$ is the salary capped at the contribution limit and $f$ is the rate function. For the bracket between R\$ 2,592 and R\$ 3,088, the rate is 9%. The sensitivity of the contribution to salary — that is, how much the deduction increases per additional real of salary — is directly the derivative $A'(s) = f'(g(s)) \cdot g'(s)$.

In practice, for someone in this bracket: for every R\$ 1 more in gross salary, R\$ 0.09 more goes to Social Security. This is the chain rule applied to fiscal policy.

### Scenario 2: Food Cooling (Newton's Law of Cooling)

The temperature of food removed from the oven and placed in a 25°C environment evolves as $T(t) = 25 + 175\,e^{-0.04\,t}$ (in degrees Celsius, $t$ in minutes). The cooling rate at any instant:

$$T'(t) = 175 \cdot e^{-0.04\,t} \cdot (-0.04) = -7\,e^{-0.04\,t}$$

In the first 10 minutes: $T'(10) = -7 e^{-0.4} \approx -7 \cdot 0.67 \approx -4.7$ degrees per minute — it cools fast. After 1 hour: $T'(60) = -7 e^{-2.4} \approx -0.63$ degrees per minute — it has almost stabilized. The chain rule captures this deceleration precisely.

### Scenario 3: Compound Inflation on Purchasing Power

If the accumulated monthly inflation index IPCA follows approximately $P(m) = 100 \cdot e^{0.006\,m}$ (base 100 in January), the purchasing power of a fixed salary decreases proportionally $C(m) = S / P(m) = S \cdot e^{-0.006\,m}$. The erosion rate is:

$$C'(m) = S \cdot e^{-0.006\,m} \cdot (-0.006) = -0.006 \cdot C(m)$$

Each month, purchasing power loses 0.6% of its current value. This is the chain rule explaining the mathematics of continuous inflation.

</Porta>

## Solved Examples

<Exemplo titulo="Generalized Power Rule — Composition with a Polynomial" numero="1">

**Problem:** Calculate the derivative of $h(x) = (x^2 + 1)^5$.

**Strategy:** The function $h$ is a composition: the outer function is $u^5$ and the inner function is $u = x^2 + 1$. We apply the chain rule by explicitly identifying each layer.

**Solution:**

1. **Identify the layers:**
   - Outer function: $f(u) = u^5$
   - Inner function: $g(x) = x^2 + 1$

2. **Derivative of the outer layer:** $f'(u) = 5u^4$. Evaluated at $g(x)$: $f'(g(x)) = 5(x^2+1)^4$.

3. **Derivative of the inner layer:** $g'(x) = 2x$.

4. **Apply the chain rule:**
$$h'(x) = f'(g(x)) \cdot g'(x) = 5(x^2+1)^4 \cdot 2x = 10x(x^2 + 1)^4$$

**Verification:** For $x = 0$: $h(0) = 1^5 = 1$, $h'(0) = 10 \cdot 0 \cdot 1 = 0$. The function has a minimum at $x = 0$ (a shifted parabola raised to the 5th power), which is consistent with $h'(0) = 0$.

<strong>Source.</strong> [Active Calculus §2.5](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-2-5-chain.html) — Boelkins · 2024 · CC-BY-NC-SA.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Chain Rule with Exponential and Trigonometry" numero="2">

**Problem:** Calculate the derivative of $f(x) = e^{\sin x}$.

**Strategy:** Composition with the exponential function. The formula $\frac{d}{dx}[e^{g(x)}] = e^{g(x)} \cdot g'(x)$ is a direct special case of the chain rule.

**Solution:**

1. **Outer layer:** $f(u) = e^u$, derivative $e^u$.
2. **Inner layer:** $g(x) = \sin x$, derivative $\cos x$.
3. **Result:**
$$f'(x) = e^{\sin x} \cdot \cos x$$

<strong>Interpretation:</strong> The derivative changes sign whenever $\cos x = 0$, i.e., at $x = \pi/2 + k\pi$. At the maxima of $\sin x$, the derivative becomes zero — because $\cos(\pi/2) = 0$.

<strong>Source.</strong> [OpenStax Calculus Volume 1 §3.6](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/3-6-the-chain-rule) — OpenStax · 2016 · CC-BY-NC-SA.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Chain Rule with Natural Logarithm" numero="3">

**Problem:** Calculate the derivative of $g(x) = \ln(3x^2 + 5)$.

**Strategy:** Composition with the natural logarithm. The formula $\frac{d}{dx}[\ln(g(x))] = \frac{g'(x)}{g(x)}$ is the direct special case.

**Solution:**

1. **Outer layer:** $f(u) = \ln u$, derivative $1/u$. Evaluated at $g(x)$: $\dfrac{1}{3x^2 + 5}$.
2. **Inner layer:** $g(x) = 3x^2 + 5$, derivative $g'(x) = 6x$.
3. **Result:**
$$g'(x) = \frac{1}{3x^2 + 5} \cdot 6x = \frac{6x}{3x^2 + 5}$$

<strong>Verification:</strong> For large $x$, $g'(x) \approx \frac{6x}{3x^2} = \frac{2}{x} \to 0$. This makes sense: the logarithm grows slower and slower.

<strong>Source.</strong> [Active Calculus §2.5](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-2-5-chain.html) — Boelkins · 2024 · CC-BY-NC-SA.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Triple Composition — Nested Chain" numero="4">

**Problem:** Calculate the derivative of $h(x) = \sin(e^{x^2})$.

**Strategy:** There are three nested layers. We apply the chain rule from outside to inside, one layer at a time.

**Solution:**

Write the layers explicitly:
- Layer 1 (outermost): $f_1(u) = \sin u$, derivative $\cos u$
- Layer 2 (intermediate): $f_2(v) = e^v$, derivative $e^v$
- Layer 3 (innermost): $f_3(x) = x^2$, derivative $2x$

Applying the chain rule from outside in:

$$h'(x) = \cos(e^{x^2}) \cdot e^{x^2} \cdot 2x$$

<strong>Verification:</strong> For $x = 0$: $h'(0) = \cos(1) \cdot 1 \cdot 0 = 0$. Numerically verifiable: $h(0+0.001) - h(0) \approx 0$.

<strong>Source.</strong> [OpenStax Calculus Volume 1 §3.6, Example 3.47](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/3-6-the-chain-rule) — CC-BY-NC-SA.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Chain Rule Combined with Product Rule" numero="5">

**Problem:** Calculate the derivative of $f(x) = x^2 \cdot e^{-x^2}$.

**Strategy:** This is a product of two functions, one of which is composite. We apply the product rule first, then the chain rule to the second factor.

**Solution:**

Identify the two factors of the product:
- $u(x) = x^2$, derivative $u'(x) = 2x$
- $v(x) = e^{-x^2}$, derivative via chain rule: $v'(x) = e^{-x^2} \cdot (-2x) = -2x e^{-x^2}$

Applying the product rule $[u \cdot v]' = u' v + u v'$:

$$f'(x) = 2x \cdot e^{-x^2} + x^2 \cdot (-2x e^{-x^2}) = 2x e^{-x^2} - 2x^3 e^{-x^2}$$

Factoring out $2x e^{-x^2}$:

$$f'(x) = 2x e^{-x^2}(1 - x^2)$$

<strong>Interpretation:</strong> The critical points are $x = 0$ and $x = \pm 1$. This function appears in the normal distribution: the $(1-x^2)$ factor determines the extrema at $x = \pm 1$.

<strong>Source.</strong> [Active Calculus §2.5, Activity 2.5.3](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-2-5-chain.html) — Boelkins · 2024 · CC-BY-NC-SA.
</Exemplo>

  solucao={<>Velocity: <Eq>{`s'(t) = \\omega\\cos(\\omega t)`}</Eq> (chain rule).   passos={<>
    <ol>
      <li><strong>Velocity.</strong> <Eq>{`s(t) = \\sin(\\omega t)`}</Eq>. By the chain rule: inner <Eq>{`\\omega t`}</Eq>, derivative <Eq>{`\\omega`}</Eq>. Thus <Eq>{`s'(t) = \\cos(\\omega t) \\cdot \\omega = \\omega\\cos(\\omega t)`}</Eq>.</li>

    </ol>
    <p><em>Curiosity: any function satisfying <Eq>{`y'' = -\\omega^2 y`}</Eq> is a combination of <Eq>{`\\sin(\\omega t)`}</Eq> and <Eq>{`\\cos(\\omega t)`}</Eq>. This is the basis of second-order differential equations.</em></p>
  </>}
>
