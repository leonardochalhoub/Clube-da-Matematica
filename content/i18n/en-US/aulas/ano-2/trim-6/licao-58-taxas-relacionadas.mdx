### General strategy for related rates

1. **Identify** the dynamic variables (depend on $t$) and the constants of the problem.
2. **Write the equation** geometric or physical that relates the variables — valid for all $t$.
3. **Differentiate both sides with respect to $t$**, using the chain rule on each dynamic variable.
4. **Substitute** the numerical values of the instant of interest (never before differentiating).
5. **Isolate** the desired rate and verify the unit and sign.

<Definicao titulo="Related rate">
Given two quantities $u(t)$ and $v(t)$ linked by an equation $F(u, v) = 0$ valid for all $t$, differentiating with respect to $t$ by the chain rule yields:

$$\frac{\partial F}{\partial u}\,\dot u + \frac{\partial F}{\partial v}\,\dot v = 0$$

Given one of the rates ($\dot u$ or $\dot v$) and the values of the variables at the instant of interest, we isolate the unknown rate.
</Definicao>

> "A related rate is the rate of change of one quantity in terms of the rate of change of another quantity. We can find this rate of change using an equation that relates the two quantities and differentiating both sides with respect to time." — [OpenStax Calculus Vol. 1, §4.1](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/4-1-related-rates)

### Canonical models

| Scenario | Fundamental equation | Dynamic variables |
|---|---|---|
| Spherical balloon | $V = \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3$ | $V(t),\; r(t)$ |
| Sliding ladder | $x^2 + y^2 = L^2$ | $x(t),\; y(t)$ |
| Conical tank | $V = \frac{1}{3}\pi r^2 h$ | $V(t),\; r(t),\; h(t)$ |
| Two diverging cars | $D^2 = x^2 + y^2$ | $x(t),\; y(t),\; D(t)$ |
| Shadow (similarity) | proportional ratio | distance, shadow |
| Angle of elevation | $\tan\theta = h/x$ | $\theta(t),\; x(t)$ |

### Chain rule — general form

If $F(x_1(t), \ldots, x_n(t)) = C$ (constant), then:

$$\frac{d}{dt}F = \sum_{i=1}^n \frac{\partial F}{\partial x_i}\,\dot x_i = 0$$

Implicit differentiation in $t$. The result is a linear equation in the rates $\dot x_i$, from which we isolate the desired one.

<Teorema titulo="Uniqueness of local solution">
If the constraint equation $F(\mathbf{x}) = 0$ has gradient $\nabla F \neq \mathbf{0}$ at a point, by the Implicit Function Theorem there exists a local variable that can be expressed as a function of the others in a neighborhood — and the related rate results from that differentiated expression.
</Teorema>

### Classic error: substituting before differentiating

If $r = 5$ is the value at the instant of interest, substituting $r = 5$ **before** differentiating reduces $r$ to a constant and makes $dr/dt$ disappear. The error eliminates the information we want to calculate.

<Insight>
The equation you differentiate must be an **identity** — true for all $t$. If you substitute a value before differentiating, you transform the identity into an equation at that instant, destroying the dynamic information.
</Insight>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old student">

**Classic problem**: a spherical balloon is being inflated at 100 cm³/s. How fast is the radius growing when $r = 10$ cm?

**Step by step:**

1. Volume of sphere: $V = \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3$.
2. Differentiate everything in $t$: $\frac{dV}{dt} = 4\pi r^2 \cdot \frac{dr}{dt}$.
3. Substitute the known data: $100 = 4\pi (10)^2 \cdot \frac{dr}{dt} = 400\pi\,\frac{dr}{dt}$.
4. Isolate: $\frac{dr}{dt} = \frac{100}{400\pi} = \frac{1}{4\pi} \approx 0.08$ cm/s.

**The ladder problem**: a 5 m ladder leans against a wall. The foot slides outward at 0.5 m/s. How fast does the top descend when the foot is 3 m from the wall?

1. Pythagorean theorem: $x^2 + y^2 = 25$.
2. Differentiate in $t$: $2x\,\dot x + 2y\,\dot y = 0$.
3. At $x = 3$: $y = \sqrt{25 - 9} = 4$. With $\dot x = 0.5$:

$$6(0.5) + 8\,\dot y = 0 \implies \dot y = -\tfrac{3}{8} = -0.375 \text{ m/s.}$$

The negative sign indicates that the top **descends**.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old teen">

Related rates is **implicit differentiation with respect to time**. Every variable in the scenario is a function of $t$, even if the statement doesn't write "$x(t)$" explicitly.

### Rigorous workflow

1. **Draw** the scenario. This step alone is worth 60% of the solution.
2. **Define variables**: identify what changes with $t$ and what is constant in the problem.
3. **Fundamental equation**: geometric or physical relationship true for all $t$, before any substitution.
4. **Differentiate in $t$**, applying the chain rule to each dynamic variable.
5. **Substitute** the values at the instant of interest (never before step 4).
6. **Isolate** the desired rate.
7. **Verify** unit and sign.

### Example: conical tank

Conical tank with top radius 4 m and height 6 m. Water enters at 2 m³/min. How fast does the water level rise when the water is 3 m deep?

By similar triangles: $r/h = 4/6 = 2/3$, so $r = 2h/3$.

$$V = \frac{1}{3}\pi r^2 h = \frac{1}{3}\pi\left(\frac{2h}{3}\right)^2 h = \frac{4\pi h^3}{27}$$

$$\frac{dV}{dt} = \frac{4\pi \cdot 3h^2}{27}\,\frac{dh}{dt} = \frac{4\pi h^2}{9}\,\frac{dh}{dt}$$

At $h = 3$ m with $dV/dt = 2$ m³/min:

$$2 = \frac{4\pi \cdot 9}{9}\,\frac{dh}{dt} = 4\pi\,\frac{dh}{dt} \implies \frac{dh}{dt} = \frac{1}{2\pi} \approx 0.16 \text{ m/min.}$$

### Why not substitute before?

If you substituted $h = 3$ before differentiating, you would get $V = \frac{4\pi \cdot 27}{27} = 4\pi$, a constant. The derivative of a constant is zero — and the entire problem vanishes.

<Insight>

**The general principle**: the equation you differentiate must be an **identity** — true for all $t$. If you substitute a value before differentiating, you transform the identity into an equation at that particular instant, losing the dynamic information.

</Insight>

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

Related rates are the elementary form of **algebraic ordinary differential equations** (DAE). In engineering, almost every dynamic process can be described by algebraic constraints between variables, whose time derivatives relate via the chain rule.

### Connection with ODEs

The mass balance equation in a tank with Torricelli drainage:

$$\pi R^2\,\frac{dh}{dt} = -A_s\sqrt{2gh}$$

is obtained directly by related rate (volume = area × height, differentiated in $t$) combined with Torricelli's law. The result is an autonomous ODE with analytical solution.

### Application domains

- **Fluid mechanics**: continuity equation $\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} + \nabla\!\cdot(\rho\mathbf{v}) = 0$. In 1D uniform: $\dot\rho A + \rho\dot A + \rho A \partial_x v = 0$ — multivariate related rate.
- **Thermodynamics**: ideal gas $PV = nRT$. If heating occurs at constant pressure: $\dot V / V = \dot T / T$.
- **Robot kinematics**: end-effector position $\mathbf{p} = f(\mathbf{q})$, velocity $\dot{\mathbf{p}} = J(\mathbf{q})\,\dot{\mathbf{q}}$ (Jacobian). Matrix-valued related rate.
- **Epidemiology**: $\dot S = -\beta SI$, $\dot I = \beta SI - \gamma I$ — SIR system, coupled rates.
- **Optics**: camera tracking object. Angle $\theta = \arctan(x/d)$, derivative in $t$ gives the angular velocity required.

### Signs and directions

When defining variables, assign positive sign in the natural physical sense. If $x$ is distance (always positive), $\dot x > 0$ indicates moving away and $\dot x < 0$ indicates approaching. The sign of the answer reveals the direction of change.

### Computational approach

```python
import sympy as sp
t = sp.Symbol('t')
r = sp.Function('r')(t)
V = sp.Rational(4, 3) * sp.pi * r**3
dVdt = sp.diff(V, t)
# Result: 4*pi*r(t)**2 * Derivative(r(t), t)
drdt = sp.solve(sp.Eq(dVdt, 100), r.diff(t))[0]
print(drdt.subs(r, 10))  # 1/(4*pi)
```

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

Related rates are the scalar projection of **holonomic constraint equations** onto the tangent space of the state manifold.

### Geometric viewpoint

The equation $F(\mathbf{x}(t)) = 0$ defines a **constraint manifold** $\mathcal{M} \subset \mathbb{R}^n$. The velocity vector $\dot{\mathbf{x}}$ belongs to the tangent space $T_{\mathbf{x}}\mathcal{M}$. The condition $\nabla F \cdot \dot{\mathbf{x}} = 0$ — exactly the result of differentiating the constraint in $t$ — is the projection onto the null space of the Jacobian $\nabla F$.

### Multibody dynamics

In mechanisms with $n$ generalized coordinates and $m$ holonomic constraints $\Phi(\mathbf{q}) = 0$:

$$\dot\Phi = \frac{\partial \Phi}{\partial \mathbf{q}}\,\dot{\mathbf{q}} = 0$$

The effective degree of freedom is $n - m$. The velocities $\dot{\mathbf{q}}$ live in the kernel of the Jacobian $\partial\Phi/\partial\mathbf{q}$. Software like ADAMS and MBDyn solve this numerically at each integration step.

### Parametric sensitivity

For system $\dot{\mathbf{x}} = F(\mathbf{x}, \boldsymbol\theta)$, the sensitivity $S = \partial\mathbf{x}/\partial\boldsymbol\theta$ satisfies:

$$\dot S = \frac{\partial F}{\partial \mathbf{x}} S + \frac{\partial F}{\partial \boldsymbol\theta}$$

This is a parametric related rate — fundamental in system identification and process optimization.

### Quant finance: delta-hedging

Portfolio $\Pi = V(S, t) - \Delta S$. By Itô's formula:

$$d\Pi = \left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial t} + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2}\right)dt + \left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial S} - \Delta\right)dS$$

Choosing $\Delta = \partial V/\partial S$ (delta hedge), we eliminate $dS$ — stochastic related rate. This yields the Black-Scholes equation.

### Optimal control

In the Pontryagin Maximum Principle, the adjoint $\lambda(t)$ satisfies $\dot\lambda = -\partial H/\partial x$ — related rate via the augmented Hamiltonian. All of optimal control theory rests on generalized related rates.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practice: universal workflow">

### Checklist in 7 steps

1. **Draw** the problem (physical or geometric).
2. **List** dynamic variables with name and unit.
3. **Write** the fundamental equation relating the variables (Pythagorean theorem, volume formulas, similarity, trigonometry).
4. **Eliminate** extra variables using similarity relations or constraints (e.g., cone — relation $r/h$ = constant).
5. **Differentiate** both sides with respect to $t$, applying the chain rule.
6. **Substitute** numerical values and isolate the desired rate.
7. **Verify** unit (e.g., cm/s, m³/min) and sign (positive = growth, negative = decay).

### Essential geometric equations

| Shape | Equation |
|---|---|
| Sphere | $V = \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3$, $\quad A = 4\pi r^2$ |
| Cylinder | $V = \pi r^2 h$ |
| Cone | $V = \frac{1}{3}\pi r^2 h$ |
| Right triangle | $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$ |
| Angle of elevation | $\tan\theta = h/d$ |
| Disk | $A = \pi r^2$ |

### Most common errors

- Substituting the value of a variable **before** differentiating.
- Forgetting the chain rule on one of the dynamic variables.
- Confusing sign: a variable that decreases has negative derivative.
- Not verifying the unit of the answer.
- Not eliminating extra variables before differentiating (e.g., in cone with both $r$ and $h$ varying, use similarity to express $r$ in terms of $h$).

### Real-world scenario: SABESP reservoir (Cantareira)

The Cantareira System is monitored by volume and water level. In a cylindrical simplification with equivalent radius $R = 8$ km, if the volume decreases at rate $dV/dt = -1.2$ km³/month, the level drop is:

$$V = \pi R^2 h \implies \frac{dh}{dt} = \frac{dV/dt}{\pi R^2} = \frac{-1.2}{\pi \cdot 64} \approx -0.006 \text{ km/month} = -6 \text{ m/month.}$$

Direct related rate: same idea as the cylindrical tank in exercise 58.9, at real scale.

## Worked examples

<Exemplo titulo="Spherical balloon — radius growth rate (direct application)" numero="1">

**Problem:** A spherical balloon is inflated so that its volume increases at a rate of 100 cm³/s. Find the rate of change of the radius when $r = 5$ cm.

**Strategy:** The dynamic quantities are $V(t)$ and $r(t)$, linked by the equation for the volume of a sphere. We differentiate this equation with respect to $t$ by the chain rule and isolate $dr/dt$.

**Solution:**

1. **Fundamental equation:** $V = \dfrac{4}{3}\pi r^3$.

2. **Differentiate in $t$:**
$$\frac{dV}{dt} = 4\pi r^2\,\frac{dr}{dt}$$

3. **Data:** $dV/dt = 100$ cm³/s, $r = 5$ cm. Substitute:
$$100 = 4\pi(5)^2\,\frac{dr}{dt} = 100\pi\,\frac{dr}{dt}$$

4. **Isolate:**
$$\frac{dr}{dt} = \frac{100}{100\pi} = \frac{1}{\pi} \approx 0.318 \text{ cm/s.}$$

**Verification:** Correct unit (cm/s). Positive sign: radius grows, consistent with inflation.

**Source.** [Active Calculus §3.5, Activity 3.5.2](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-3-1-rel-rates.html) — Boelkins · CC-BY-NC-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Sliding ladder — rate of descent of top (intermediate)" numero="2">

**Problem:** A 10 m ladder leans vertically against a wall. The foot of the ladder slides outward at 0.8 m/s. Find the rate of descent of the top when the foot is 6 m from the wall.

**Strategy:** The dynamic variables are $x(t)$ (distance of foot from wall) and $y(t)$ (height of top). The constraint is Pythagorean theorem with $L = 10$ (constant).

**Solution:**

1. **Fundamental equation:** $x^2 + y^2 = 100$.

2. **Differentiate in $t$:**
$$2x\,\frac{dx}{dt} + 2y\,\frac{dy}{dt} = 0$$

3. **Data:** $dx/dt = 0.8$ m/s, $x = 6$ m. We compute $y$:
$$y = \sqrt{100 - 36} = \sqrt{64} = 8 \text{ m.}$$

4. **Substitute:**
$$2(6)(0.8) + 2(8)\,\frac{dy}{dt} = 0 \implies 9.6 + 16\,\frac{dy}{dt} = 0$$

5. **Isolate:**
$$\frac{dy}{dt} = -\frac{9.6}{16} = -0.6 \text{ m/s.}$$

The top of the ladder descends at 0.6 m/s. The negative sign is expected: $y$ decreases when $x$ increases.

**Source.** [OpenStax Calculus Vol. 1 §4.1, Example 4.3](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/4-1-related-rates) — OpenStax · CC-BY-NC-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Conical tank filling — rate of level rise (intermediate)" numero="3">

**Problem:** Water is pumped into an inverted conical tank (vertex down) at a rate of 3 m³/min. The tank has total height 6 m and top radius 2 m. How fast does the water level rise when the depth is $h = 4$ m?

**Strategy:** The volume of the cone depends on $r$ and $h$, both dynamic. The geometry of similarity allows expressing $r$ as a function of $h$, reducing the problem to a single variable.

**Solution:**

1. **Similarity:** In the cone, $r/h = 2/6 = 1/3$, so $r = h/3$.

2. **Express $V$ in terms of $h$ only:**
$$V = \frac{1}{3}\pi r^2 h = \frac{1}{3}\pi\left(\frac{h}{3}\right)^2 h = \frac{\pi h^3}{27}$$

3. **Differentiate in $t$:**
$$\frac{dV}{dt} = \frac{3\pi h^2}{27}\,\frac{dh}{dt} = \frac{\pi h^2}{9}\,\frac{dh}{dt}$$

4. **Data:** $dV/dt = 3$ m³/min, $h = 4$ m:
$$3 = \frac{\pi(16)}{9}\,\frac{dh}{dt} \implies \frac{dh}{dt} = \frac{27}{16\pi} \approx 0.537 \text{ m/min.}$$

**Verification:** As expected, as $h$ increases, the rise is slower (cross-section area grows with $h^2$).

**Source.** [Active Calculus §3.5, Example 3.5.1](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-3-1-rel-rates.html) — Boelkins · CC-BY-NC-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Two cars moving apart — distance increasing (modeling)" numero="4">

**Problem:** Car A leaves an intersection heading north at 60 km/h and car B leaves the same intersection heading east at 80 km/h. How fast is the distance between them increasing after 30 min of travel?

**Strategy:** After 30 min, $x = 40$ km (B) and $y = 30$ km (A). The distance $D$ satisfies Pythagorean theorem. All three variables $x$, $y$, $D$ are dynamic.

**Solution:**

1. **Fundamental equation:** $D^2 = x^2 + y^2$.

2. **Differentiate in $t$:**
$$2D\,\frac{dD}{dt} = 2x\,\frac{dx}{dt} + 2y\,\frac{dy}{dt}$$

3. **After $t = 0.5$ h:** $x = 80 \times 0.5 = 40$ km; $y = 60 \times 0.5 = 30$ km.
$$D = \sqrt{40^2 + 30^2} = \sqrt{2500} = 50 \text{ km.}$$

4. **Data:** $dx/dt = 80$ km/h, $dy/dt = 60$ km/h. Substitute:
$$2(50)\,\frac{dD}{dt} = 2(40)(80) + 2(30)(60) = 6400 + 3600 = 10000$$

5. **Isolate:**
$$\frac{dD}{dt} = \frac{10000}{100} = 100 \text{ km/h.}$$

**Source.** [OpenStax Calculus Vol. 1 §4.1, Example 4.5](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/4-1-related-rates) — OpenStax · CC-BY-NC-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Angle of elevation of a plane — angular rate (advanced)" numero="5">

**Problem:** An observer on the ground sees a plane flying horizontally at 800 m altitude and 200 m/s. What is the rate of change of the angle of elevation when the plane is directly 600 m away (horizontally) from the observer?

**Strategy:** $\theta$ and $x$ (horizontal distance) are dynamic, with $h = 800$ m constant. The trigonometric relation $\tan\theta = h/x$ is the fundamental equation.

**Solution:**

1. **Fundamental equation:** $\tan\theta = \dfrac{800}{x}$.

2. **Differentiate in $t$** (left side via chain rule):
$$\sec^2\!\theta\,\frac{d\theta}{dt} = -\frac{800}{x^2}\,\frac{dx}{dt}$$

3. **Data:** $x = 600$ m; the plane approaches, so $dx/dt = -200$ m/s. Calculate $\theta$:
$$\tan\theta = \frac{800}{600} = \frac{4}{3} \implies \sec^2\!\theta = 1 + \tan^2\!\theta = 1 + \frac{16}{9} = \frac{25}{9}$$

4. **Substitute:**
$$\frac{25}{9}\,\frac{d\theta}{dt} = -\frac{800}{360000}\,(-200) = \frac{160000}{360000} = \frac{4}{9}$$

5. **Isolate:**
$$\frac{d\theta}{dt} = \frac{4}{9} \cdot \frac{9}{25} = \frac{4}{25} = 0.16 \text{ rad/s.}$$

The angle of elevation grows at 0.16 rad/s at that instant.

**Source.** [APEX Calculus §4.2, Example 4.2.4](https://www.apexcalculus.com/) — Hartman et al. · CC-BY-NC.

</Exemplo>

      <li><strong>Substitute.</strong> <Eq>{`D = \\sqrt{480^2+360^2} = 600`}</Eq>; <Eq>{`dD/dt = (480^2 + 360^2)/600 = 600`}</Eq> km/h.</li>
    </ol>
    <p><em>Surprising result: the separation rate after 1 hour is exactly 600 km/h — the norm of the velocity vector. This occurs because after 1 hour the angle between <Eq>{`\\vec{D}`}</Eq> and each axis is exactly the angle of the 3-4-5 Pythagorean triple.</em></p>
  </>}
