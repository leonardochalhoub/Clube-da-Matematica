### General Strategy for Related Rates

1. **Identify** the dynamic variables (depend on $t$) and constants in the problem.
2. **Write the equation** (geometric or physical) relating the variables — valid for all $t$.
3. **Differentiate both sides with respect to $t$**, using the chain rule for each dynamic variable.
4. **Substitute** the numerical values for the instant of interest (never before differentiating).
5. **Isolate** the desired rate and check units and sign.

<Definicao titulo="Related Rate">
Given two quantities $u(t)$ and $v(t)$ linked by an equation $F(u, v) = 0$ valid for all $t$, differentiating with respect to $t$ using the chain rule yields:

$$\frac{\partial F}{\partial u}\,\dot u + \frac{\partial F}{\partial v}\,\dot v = 0$$

If one rate ($\dot u$ or $\dot v$) and the values of the variables at the instant of interest are known, the unknown rate can be isolated.
</Definicao>

> "A related rate is the rate of change of one quantity in terms of the rate of change of another quantity. We can find this rate of change using an equation that relates the two quantities and differentiating both sides with respect to time." — [OpenStax Calculus Vol. 1, §4.1](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/4-1-related-rates)

### Canonical Models

| Scenario | Fundamental Equation | Dynamic Variables |
|---|---|---|
| Spherical Balloon | $V = \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3$ | $V(t),\; r(t)$ |
| Sliding Ladder | $x^2 + y^2 = L^2$ | $x(t),\; y(t)$ |
| Conical Tank | $V = \frac{1}{3}\pi r^2 h$ | $V(t),\; r(t),\; h(t)$ |
| Two Cars Diverging | $D^2 = x^2 + y^2$ | $x(t),\; y(t),\; D(t)$ |
| Shadow (Similarity) | Proportional ratio | distance, shadow length |
| Angle of Elevation | $\tan\theta = h/x$ | $\theta(t),\; x(t)$ |

### Chain Rule — General Form

If $F(x_1(t), \ldots, x_n(t)) = C$ (constant), then:

$$\frac{d}{dt}F = \sum_{i=1}^n \frac{\partial F}{\partial x_i}\,\dot x_i = 0$$

Implicit differentiation with respect to $t$. The result is a linear equation in the rates $\dot x_i$, from which the desired rate can be isolated.

<Teorema titulo="Uniqueness of Local Solution">
If the constraint equation $F(\mathbf{x}) = 0$ has a non-zero gradient $\nabla F \neq \mathbf{0}$ at a point, the Implicit Function Theorem guarantees that one variable can be expressed as a function of the others in a neighborhood. Differentiating this expression yields the related rate.
</Teorema>

### Classic Error: Substituting Before Differentiating

If $r = 5$ is the value at the instant of interest, substituting $r = 5$ **before** differentiating reduces $r$ to a constant and makes $dr/dt$ disappear. This error eliminates the information you want to calculate.

<Insight>
The equation you differentiate must be an **identity** — true for all $t$. If you substitute a value before differentiating, you turn the identity into an equation specific to that instant, destroying the dynamic information.
</Insight>

Imagine you are **filling a balloon** with air. The balloon gets fatter — the **radius grows**. At the same time, the **volume increases**.

The faster you blow (volume in), the faster the radius grows. The two things are linked: one changes, the other changes along with it.

Math helps us figure out: "If I know the speed of one, what's the speed of the other?"

Related rates is exactly this: two quantities moving together over time.

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-Year-Old">

**Classic Problem**: A spherical balloon is being inflated at 100 cm³/s. How fast is the radius growing when $r = 10$ cm?

**Step-by-Step:**

1. Sphere volume: $V = \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3$.
2. Differentiate everything with respect to $t$: $\frac{dV}{dt} = 4\pi r^2 \cdot \frac{dr}{dt}$.
3. Substitute known values: $100 = 4\pi (10)^2 \cdot \frac{dr}{dt} = 400\pi\,\frac{dr}{dt}$.
4. Isolate: $\frac{dr}{dt} = \frac{100}{400\pi} = \frac{1}{4\pi} \approx 0.08$ cm/s.

**Ladder Problem**: A 5m ladder leans against a wall. The foot slips away at 0.5 m/s. How fast is the top sliding down when the foot is 3m from the wall?

1. Pythagorean theorem: $x^2 + y^2 = 25$.
2. Differentiate with respect to $t$: $2x\,\dot x + 2y\,\dot y = 0$.
3. At $x = 3$: $y = \sqrt{25 - 9} = 4$. With $\dot x = 0.5$:

$$6(0.5) + 8\,\dot y = 0 \implies \dot y = -\tfrac{3}{8} = -0.375 \text{ m/s.}$$

The negative sign means the top is **sliding down**.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-Year-Old">

Related rates is **implicit differentiation with respect to time**. Every variable in the scenario is a function of $t$, even if the problem doesn't explicitly write "$x(t)$".

### Rigorous Workflow

1. **Draw** the scenario. This step alone is worth 60% of the solution.
2. **Define variables**: identify what changes with $t$ and what is constant.
3. **Fundamental equation**: a geometric or physical relationship true for all $t$, before any substitutions.
4. **Differentiate with respect to $t$**, applying the chain rule to each dynamic variable.
5. **Substitute** the values for the instant of interest (never before step 4).
6. **Isolate** the requested rate.
7. **Check** units and sign.

### Example: Conical Tank

A conical tank has a top radius of 4m and a height of 6m. Water flows in at 2 m³/min. How fast is the water level rising when the water is 3m deep?

By similar triangles: $r/h = 4/6 = 2/3$, so $r = 2h/3$.

$$V = \frac{1}{3}\pi r^2 h = \frac{1}{3}\pi\left(\frac{2h}{3}\right)^2 h = \frac{4\pi h^3}{27}$$

$$\frac{dV}{dt} = \frac{4\pi \cdot 3h^2}{27}\,\frac{dh}{dt} = \frac{4\pi h^2}{9}\,\frac{dh}{dt}$$

At $h = 3$ m with $dV/dt = 2$ m³/min:

$$2 = \frac{4\pi \cdot 9}{9}\,\frac{dh}{dt} = 4\pi\,\frac{dh}{dt} \implies \frac{dh}{dt} = \frac{1}{2\pi} \approx 0.16 \text{ m/min.}$$

### Why Not Substitute Early?

If you substituted $h = 3$ before differentiating, you'd get $V = \frac{4\pi \cdot 27}{27} = 4\pi$, a constant. The derivative of a constant is zero — and the whole problem vanishes.

<Insight>

**The general principle**: the equation you differentiate must be an **identity** — true for all $t$. If you substitute a value before differentiating, you turn the identity into an equation particular to that instant, losing the dynamic information.

</Insight>

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an Engineering Student">

Related rates are the elementary form of **algebraic differential equations** (DAEs). In engineering, almost every dynamic process can be described by algebraic constraints between variables, whose time derivatives are related via the chain rule.

### Connection to ODEs

The mass balance equation in a tank with Torricelli outflow:

$$\pi R^2\,\frac{dh}{dt} = -A_s\sqrt{2gh}$$

is obtained directly from related rates (volume = area × height, differentiated w.r.t. $t$) combined with Torricelli's law. The result is an autonomous ODE with an analytical solution.

### Application Domains

- **Fluid Mechanics**: continuity equation $\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} + \nabla\!\cdot(\rho\mathbf{v}) = 0$. In 1D uniform flow: $\dot\rho A + \rho\dot A + \rho A \partial_x v = 0$ — a multivariate related rate.
- **Thermodynamics**: ideal gas $PV = nRT$. If heating occurs at constant pressure: $\dot V / V = \dot T / T$.
- **Robot Kinematics**: end-effector position $\mathbf{p} = f(\mathbf{q})$, velocity $\dot{\mathbf{p}} = J(\mathbf{q})\,\dot{\mathbf{q}}$ (Jacobian). Matrix related rate.
- **Epidemiology**: $\dot S = -\beta SI$, $\dot I = \beta SI - \gamma I$ — SIR system, coupled rates.
- **Optics**: camera tracking an object. Angle $\theta = \arctan(x/d)$, differentiating w.r.t. $t$ gives the required angular velocity.

### Signs and Directions

When defining variables, assign a positive sign in the natural physical direction. If $x$ is distance (always positive), $\dot x > 0$ indicates moving away and $\dot x < 0$ indicates approaching. The sign of the answer reveals the direction of change.

### Computational Approach

```python
import sympy as sp
t = sp.Symbol('t')
r = sp.Function('r')(t)
V = sp.Rational(4, 3) * sp.pi * r**3
dVdt = sp.diff(V, t)
# Result: 4*pi*r(t)**2 * Derivative(r(t), t)
drdt = sp.solve(sp.Eq(dVdt, 100), r.diff(t))[0]
print(drdt.subs(r, 10))  # 1/(4*pi)
```

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a Senior Professional">

Related rates are the scalar projection of **holonomic constraint equations** onto the tangent space of the state manifold.

### Geometric Perspective

The equation $F(\mathbf{x}(t)) = 0$ defines a **constraint manifold** $\mathcal{M} \subset \mathbb{R}^n$. The velocity vector $\dot{\mathbf{x}}$ belongs to the tangent space $T_{\mathbf{x}}\mathcal{M}$. The condition $\nabla F \cdot \dot{\mathbf{x}} = 0$ — precisely the result of differentiating the constraint w.r.t. $t$ — is the projection onto the null space of the Jacobian $\nabla F$.

### Multibody Dynamics

In mechanisms with $n$ generalized coordinates and $m$ holonomic constraints $\Phi(\mathbf{q}) = 0$:

$$\dot\Phi = \frac{\partial \Phi}{\partial \mathbf{q}}\,\dot{\mathbf{q}} = 0$$

The effective degrees of freedom are $n - m$. The velocities $\dot{\mathbf{q}}$ live in the null space of the Jacobian $\partial\Phi/\partial\mathbf{q}$. Software like ADAMS and MBDyn solve this numerically at each integration step.

### Parametric Sensitivity

For a system $\dot{\mathbf{x}} = F(\mathbf{x}, \boldsymbol\theta)$, the sensitivity $S = \partial\mathbf{x}/\partial\boldsymbol\theta$ satisfies:

$$\dot S = \frac{\partial F}{\partial \mathbf{x}} S + \frac{\partial F}{\partial \boldsymbol\theta}$$

This is a parametric related rate — fundamental in system identification and process optimization.

### Quant Finance: Delta-Hedging

Portfolio $\Pi = V(S, t) - \Delta S$. By Ito's formula:

$$d\Pi = \left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial t} + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2}\right)dt + \left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial S} - \Delta\right)dS$$

Choosing $\Delta = \partial V/\partial S$ (delta hedge) eliminates $dS$ — a stochastic related rate. This yields the Black-Scholes equation.

### Optimal Control

In Pontryagin's Maximum Principle, the adjoint variable $\lambda(t)$ satisfies $\dot\lambda = -\partial H/\partial x$ — a related rate via the augmented Hamiltonian. The entire theory of optimal control rests on generalized related rates.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practice: Universal Workflow">

### 7-Step Checklist

1. **Draw** the problem (physical or geometric).
2. **List** dynamic variables with names and units.
3. **Write** the fundamental equation relating the variables (Pythagorean theorem, volume formulas, similarity, trigonometry).
4. **Eliminate** extra variables using similarity relations or constraints (e.g., cone — $r/h$ = constant relation).
5. **Differentiate** both sides with respect to $t$, applying the chain rule.
6. **Substitute** numerical values and isolate the desired rate.
7. **Verify** units (e.g., cm/s, m³/min) and sign (positive = growth, negative = decay).

### Essential Geometric Equations

| Shape | Equation |
|---|---|
| Sphere | $V = \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3$, $\quad A = 4\pi r^2$ |
| Cylinder | $V = \pi r^2 h$ |
| Cone | $V = \frac{1}{3}\pi r^2 h$ |
| Right Triangle | $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$ |
| Angle of Elevation | $\tan\theta = h/d$ |
| Disk | $A = \pi r^2$ |

### Common Errors

- Substituting a variable's value **before** differentiating.
- Forgetting the chain rule for one of the dynamic variables.
- Confusing signs: a decreasing variable has a negative derivative.
- Not checking the answer's units.
- Not eliminating extra variables before differentiating (e.g., in a cone with both $r$ and $h$ as variables, use similarity to express $r$ in terms of $h$).

### Real-World Scenario: SABESP Reservoir (Cantareira)

The Cantareira System is monitored by volume and water level. In a cylindrical simplification with equivalent radius $R = 8$ km, if the volume drops at a rate of $dV/dt = -1.2$ km³/month, the water level drop is:

$$V = \pi R^2 h \implies \frac{dh}{dt} = \frac{dV/dt}{\pi R^2} = \frac{-1.2}{\pi \cdot 64} \approx -0.006 \text{ km/month} = -6 \text{ m/month.}$$

Direct related rate: same idea as the cylindrical tank in exercise 58.9, on a real-world scale.

## Solved Examples

<Exemplo titulo="Spherical Balloon — Rate of Radius Growth (Direct Application)" numero="1">

**Problem:** A spherical balloon is inflated such that its volume increases at a rate of 100 cm³/s. Find the rate of change of the radius when $r = 5$ cm.

**Strategy:** The dynamic quantities are $V(t)$ and $r(t)$, linked by the sphere's volume equation. We differentiate this equation with respect to $t$ using the chain rule and isolate $dr/dt$.

**Solution:**

1. **Fundamental Equation:** $V = \dfrac{4}{3}\pi r^3$.

2. **Differentiate w.r.t. $t$:**
$$\frac{dV}{dt} = 4\pi r^2\,\frac{dr}{dt}$$

3. **Given Data:** $dV/dt = 100$ cm³/s, $r = 5$ cm. Substitute:
$$100 = 4\pi(5)^2\,\frac{dr}{dt} = 100\pi\,\frac{dr}{dt}$$

4. **Isolate:**
$$\frac{dr}{dt} = \frac{100}{100\pi} = \frac{1}{\pi} \approx 0.318 \text{ cm/s.}$$

**Check:** Correct units (cm/s). Positive sign: radius is increasing, consistent with inflation.

**Source.** [Active Calculus §3.5, Activity 3.5.2](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-3-5-related-rates.html) — Boelkins · CC-BY-NC-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Sliding Ladder — Rate of Top Descent (Intermediate)" numero="2">

**Problem:** A 10m ladder leans vertically against a wall. The foot of the ladder slips outward at 0.8 m/s. Find the rate of descent of the top of the ladder when the foot is 6m from the wall.

**Strategy:** The dynamic variables are $x(t)$ (distance from foot to wall) and $y(t)$ (height of the top). The constraint is the Pythagorean Theorem with $L = 10$ (constant).

**Solution:**

1. **Fundamental Equation:** $x^2 + y^2 = 100$.

2. **Differentiate w.r.t. $t$:**
$$2x\,\frac{dx}{dt} + 2y\,\frac{dy}{dt} = 0$$

3. **Given Data:** $dx/dt = 0.8$ m/s, $x = 6$ m. Calculate $y$:
$$y = \sqrt{100 - 36} = \sqrt{64} = 8 \text{ m.}$$

4. **Substitute:**
$$2(6)(0.8) + 2(8)\,\frac{dy}{dt} = 0 \implies 9.6 + 16\,\frac{dy}{dt} = 0$$

5. **Isolate:**
$$\frac{dy}{dt} = -\frac{9.6}{16} = -0.6 \text{ m/s.}$$

The top of the ladder descends at 0.6 m/s. The negative sign is expected: $y$ decreases as $x$ increases.

**Source.** [OpenStax Calculus Vol. 1 §4.1, Example 4.3](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/4-1-related-rates) — OpenStax · CC-BY-NC-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Filling Conical Tank — Rate of Level Rise (Intermediate)" numero="3">

**Problem:** Water is pumped into an inverted conical tank (vertex down) at a rate of 3 m³/min. The tank has a total height of 6m and a top radius of 2m. How fast is the water level rising when the water depth is $h = 4$ m?

**Strategy:** The volume of the cone depends on $r$ and $h$, both dynamic. The geometry of similar triangles allows expressing $r$ in terms of $h$, reducing the problem to a single variable.

**Solution:**

1. **Similarity:** In the cone, $r/h = 2/6 = 1/3$, so $r = h/3$.

2. **Express $V$ solely in terms of $h$:**
$$V = \frac{1}{3}\pi r^2 h = \frac{1}{3}\pi\left(\frac{h}{3}\right)^2 h = \frac{\pi h^3}{27}$$

3. **Differentiate w.r.t. $t$:**
$$\frac{dV}{dt} = \frac{3\pi h^2}{27}\,\frac{dh}{dt} = \frac{\pi h^2}{9}\,\frac{dh}{dt}$$

4. **Given Data:** $dV/dt = 3$ m³/min, $h = 4$ m:
$$3 = \frac{\pi(16)}{9}\,\frac{dh}{dt} \implies \frac{dh}{dt} = \frac{27}{16\pi} \approx 0.537 \text{ m/min.}$$

**Check:** As expected, the higher $h$, the slower the rise (the cross-sectional area grows with $h^2$).

**Source.** [Active Calculus §3.5, Example 3.5.1](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-3-5-related-rates.html) — Boelkins · CC-BY-NC-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Two Cars Moving Apart — Distance Growing (Modeling)" numero="4">

**Problem:** Car A leaves an intersection heading north at 60 km/h, and Car B leaves the same intersection heading east at 80 km/h. How fast is the distance between them increasing after 30 min of travel?

**Strategy:** After 30 min, $x = 40$ km (Car B) and $y = 30$ km (Car A). The distance $D$ satisfies the Pythagorean theorem. All three variables $x$, $y$, $D$ are dynamic.

**Solution:**

1. **Fundamental Equation:** $D^2 = x^2 + y^2$.

2. **Differentiate w.r.t. $t$:**
$$2D\,\frac{dD}{dt} = 2x\,\frac{dx}{dt} + 2y\,\frac{dy}{dt}$$

3. **After $t = 0.5$ h:** $x = 80 \times 0.5 = 40$ km; $y = 60 \times 0.5 = 30$ km.
$$D = \sqrt{40^2 + 30^2} = \sqrt{2500} = 50 \text{ km.}$$

4. **Given Data:** $dx/dt = 80$ km/h, $dy/dt = 60$ km/h. Substitute:
$$2(50)\,\frac{dD}{dt} = 2(40)(80) + 2(30)(60) = 6400 + 3600 = 10000$$

5. **Isolate:**
$$\frac{dD}{dt} = \frac{10000}{100} = 100 \text{ km/h.}$$

**Source.** [OpenStax Calculus Vol. 1 §4.1, Example 4.5](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/4-1-related-rates) — OpenStax · CC-BY-NC-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Airplane Angle of Elevation — Angular Rate (Advanced)" numero="5">

**Problem:** A ground observer spots a plane flying horizontally at an altitude of 800m and a speed of 200 m/s. What is the rate of change of the angle of elevation when the plane is directly 600m (horizontally) from the observer?

**Strategy:** $\theta$ and $x$ (horizontal distance) are dynamic, with $h = 800$ m constant. The trigonometric relation $\tan\theta = h/x$ is the fundamental equation.

**Solution:**

1. **Fundamental Equation:** $\tan\theta = \dfrac{800}{x}$.

2. **Differentiate w.r.t. $t$** (left side via chain rule):
$$\sec^2\!\theta\,\frac{d\theta}{dt} = -\frac{800}{x^2}\,\frac{dx}{dt}$$

3. **Given Data:** $x = 600$ m; the plane is approaching, so $dx/dt = -200$ m/s. Calculate $\theta$:
$$\tan\theta = \frac{800}{600} = \frac{4}{3} \implies \sec^2\!\theta = 1 + \tan^2\!\theta = 1 + \frac{16}{9} = \frac{25}{9}$$

4. **Substitute:**
$$\frac{25}{9}\,\frac{d\theta}{dt} = -\frac{800}{360000}\,(-200) = \frac{160000}{360000} = \frac{4}{9}$$

5. **Isolate:**
$$\frac{d\theta}{dt} = \frac{4}{9} \cdot \frac{9}{25} = \frac{4}{25} = 0.16 \text{ rad/s.}$$

The angle of elevation is increasing at 0.16 rad/s at this instant.

**Source.** [APEX Calculus §4.2, Example 4.2.4](https://www.apexcalculus.com/) — Hartman et al. · CC-BY-NC.

</Exemplo>

<Exercicio
  numero="58.38"
  dificuldade="proof"
  solucao={<>Proof: <Eq>{`V = \\tfrac{4}{3}\\pi r^3`}</Eq>. Differentiate w.r.t. <Eq>{`t`}</Eq>: <Eq>{`dV/dt = \\tfrac{4}{3}\\pi \\cdot 3r^2 \\cdot dr/dt = 4\\pi r^2\\,dr/dt`}</Eq>.   passos={<>
    <ol>
      <li><strong>Starting Point.</strong> <Eq>{`V = \\tfrac{4}{3}\\pi r^3`}</Eq> with <Eq>{`r = r(t)`}</Eq> as a function of time. We want <Eq>{`dV/dt`}</Eq>.</li>
