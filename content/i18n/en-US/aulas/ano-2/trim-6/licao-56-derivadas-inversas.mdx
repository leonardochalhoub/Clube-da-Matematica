### Theorem of the Derivative of the Inverse Function

<Definicao titulo="Derivative of the Inverse">

Let $f: I \to J$ be continuous, strictly monotonic on $I$, and differentiable at $a \in I$ with $f'(a) \neq 0$. Let $b = f(a)$. Then $f^{-1}$ is differentiable at $b$ and:

$$\boxed{(f^{-1})'(b) = \frac{1}{f'(a)} = \frac{1}{f'(f^{-1}(b))}}$$

</Definicao>

> "If $f$ is a differentiable, one-to-one function with $f(a) = b$ and $f'(a) \neq 0$, then $f^{-1}$ is differentiable at $b$ and $(f^{-1})'(b) = 1/f'(a)$." — [Active Calculus §2.6, Theorem 2.6.2](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-2-6-inverse.html)

### Proof via Chain Rule

From the identity $f(f^{-1}(y)) = y$, differentiating both sides with respect to $y$ using the chain rule:

$$f'(f^{-1}(y)) \cdot (f^{-1})'(y) = 1$$

Since $f'(f^{-1}(y)) \neq 0$ by hypothesis, we can divide:

$$\boxed{(f^{-1})'(y) = \frac{1}{f'(f^{-1}(y))}}$$

### Geometric Interpretation

The graph of $f^{-1}$ is the reflection of the graph of $f$ across the line $y = x$. A tangent line with slope $m$ on the graph of $f$ at point $(a, b)$ becomes a tangent line with slope $1/m$ on the graph of $f^{-1}$ at point $(b, a)$ — the reflection swaps the roles of $\Delta x$ and $\Delta y$.

<Insight>

**The pathology when $f'(a) = 0$.** If the tangent to $f$ at $a$ is horizontal (slope 0), the tangent to $f^{-1}$ at $b = f(a)$ would be vertical — infinite slope. The formula fails because we divide by zero. A classic example: $f(x) = x^3$, $f'(0) = 0$, and $f^{-1}(y) = y^{1/3}$ has a vertical tangent at the origin. The inverse exists but is not differentiable at $y = 0$.

</Insight>

<Teorema titulo="Derivative of a^x">

For $a > 0$, $a \neq 1$, using $a^x = e^{x \ln a}$ and the chain rule:

$$\frac{d}{dx} a^x = a^x \ln a$$

In particular, $(e^x)' = e^x$ (case $a = e$, $\ln e = 1$).

</Teorema>

> "In general, there is a formula for the derivative of $a^x$ for any $a > 0$ with $a \neq 1$: $\frac{d}{dx}(a^x) = a^x \ln a$. This formula is a special case of the chain rule applied to $a^x = e^{x \ln a}$." — [OpenStax Calculus Volume 1 §3.7](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/3-7-derivatives-of-inverse-functions)

### Chain Rule with Inverse Trig

For a differentiable $u = g(x)$:

$$\frac{d}{dx}\arcsin(g(x)) = \frac{g'(x)}{\sqrt{1 - g(x)^2}}, \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\arctan(g(x)) = \frac{g'(x)}{1 + g(x)^2}$$

</Porta>

Imagine a machine that **takes a number and does something** to it. For example, the machine doubles: 3 goes in, 6 comes out.

The **inverse** machine undoes what the original did. 6 goes in, 3 comes out.

Now think about the **speed** of each machine. If the original machine goes very **fast** (changes the number a lot), the inverse machine goes very **slow** — because it undoes what the other did so quickly.

If the original machine goes 5 times faster, the inverse machine goes 5 times slower.

**Inverse of speed = inverse speed.** It's like climbing a mountain versus descending: climbing in 10 minutes becomes descending at a rate 10 times slower per unit of altitude.

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

Functions and their inverses undo each other: $f(f^{-1}(y)) = y$.

Example: $f(x) = x^2$ and $f^{-1}(y) = \sqrt{y}$. Put in 9, $f^{-1}(9) = 3$, then $f(3) = 9$. You're back.

**The inverse derivative rule:** the derivative of the inverse is 1 divided by the derivative of the original — measured at the corresponding point.

With $f(x) = x^2$: the derivative is $f'(x) = 2x$. At $x = 3$: $f'(3) = 6$. The inverse $\sqrt{y}$ at $y = 9$ has derivative $1/6$. Check: $(\sqrt{y})' = 1/(2\sqrt{y})$; at $y = 9$: $1/(2 \cdot 3) = 1/6$. It matches.

**Famous inverse functions and their derivatives:**

- $\arcsin x$: undoes sine. Derivative: $1/\sqrt{1-x^2}$.
- $\arctan x$: undoes tangent. Derivative: $1/(1+x^2)$.
- $\ln x$: undoes $e^x$. Derivative: $1/x$.

The graph of $f^{-1}$ is the reflection of $f$ across the diagonal line $y = x$. A tangent with slope $m$ becomes $1/m$ after reflection.

</Porta>

### Universal Recipe for Deriving $f^{-1}$

1. Write $y = f^{-1}(x)$. Then $f(y) = x$.
2. Differentiate both sides with respect to $x$ (chain rule on $y$): $f'(y) \cdot y' = 1$.
3. Isolate: $y' = \dfrac{1}{f'(y)}$.
4. Rewrite $f'(y)$ in terms of $x$ using $f(y) = x$ and identities.

### Complete Example: $(\arcsin x)'$

Let $y = \arcsin x$. Then $\sin y = x$ with $y \in [-\pi/2,\, \pi/2]$.

Differentiating: $\cos y \cdot y' = 1$, so $y' = \dfrac{1}{\cos y}$.

On the interval $[-\pi/2, \pi/2]$, cosine is positive, so $\cos y = \sqrt{1 - \sin^2 y} = \sqrt{1 - x^2}$.

$$(\arcsin x)' = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - x^2}}$$

### Complete Example: $(\arctan x)'$

Let $y = \arctan x$. Then $\tan y = x$.

Differentiating: $\sec^2 y \cdot y' = 1$, so $y' = \dfrac{1}{\sec^2 y} = \dfrac{1}{1 + \tan^2 y} = \dfrac{1}{1 + x^2}$.

$$(\arctan x)' = \frac{1}{1 + x^2}$$

### Complete Example: $(\ln x)'$

Let $y = \ln x$. Then $e^y = x$.

Differentiating: $e^y \cdot y' = 1$, so $y' = \dfrac{1}{e^y} = \dfrac{1}{x}$.

$$(\ln x)' = \frac{1}{x}$$

### Identities that Derive to Zero

- $\arcsin x + \arccos x = \pi/2$: the terms cancel upon differentiation.
- $\arctan x + \arctan(1/x) = \pi/2$ for $x > 0$.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

The derivative of the inverse is the **building block** for logarithms, inverse trigonometric functions, inverse hyperbolic functions, and variable transformations in probability.

### Inverse Function Theorem (Formal Version)

If $f \in C^1$ in a neighborhood of $x_0$ and $f'(x_0) \neq 0$, then $f$ is locally invertible, $f^{-1} \in C^1$ in a neighborhood of $y_0 = f(x_0)$, and:

$$(f^{-1})'(y_0) = \frac{1}{f'(x_0)}$$

In multivariate calculus: for $f: U \subset \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^n$ with invertible Jacobian $J_f(x_0)$, $D(f^{-1})(y_0) = J_f(x_0)^{-1}$.

### Derivatives of Inverse Hyperbolics via Logarithm

Since $\text{arcsinh}\, x = \ln(x + \sqrt{x^2+1})$, differentiating:

$$(\text{arcsinh}\, x)' = \frac{1 + x/\sqrt{x^2+1}}{x + \sqrt{x^2+1}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{x^2+1}}$$

Similarly: $(\text{arccosh}\, x)' = 1/\sqrt{x^2-1}$ for $x > 1$; $(\text{arctanh}\, x)' = 1/(1-x^2)$ for $|x| < 1$.

### Change of Variables in Integrals

If $Y = f(X)$ with $f$ invertible and differentiable, the change of variable formula is:

$$\int_A g(y)\,dy = \int_{f^{-1}(A)} g(f(x))\,|f'(x)|\,dx$$

The Jacobian $|f'(x)|$ appears because $(f^{-1})'(y) = 1/f'(x)$ — it's the same object.

### Transformation of Densities in Probability

If $X$ has density $f_X(x)$ and $Y = g(X)$ with $g$ monotonic:

$$f_Y(y) = f_X(g^{-1}(y)) \cdot \left|(g^{-1})'(y)\right| = f_X(g^{-1}(y)) \cdot \frac{1}{|g'(g^{-1}(y))|}$$

This appears in **sample generation** (inverse transform method): generate $U \sim \text{Unif}[0,1]$, take $X = F^{-1}(U)$.

### Engineering Applications

- **Snell's Law**: critical angle $\theta_c = \arcsin(n_2/n_1)$. Sensitivity $d\theta_c/dn_1$ via derivative of $\arcsin$.
- **GPS**: elevation angle $\theta = \arctan(h/d)$. Sensitivity to altitude variations $h$.
- **Control Systems**: inversion of nonlinear plants uses $(f^{-1})'$ for linearization around the operating point.

### Pathology: $f'(x_0) = 0$

If $f'(x_0) = 0$, the rule fails: the inverse has a vertical tangent at $y_0 = f(x_0)$, so $(f^{-1})'(y_0)$ does not exist. Example: $f(x) = x^3$, $f'(0) = 0$, $f^{-1}(y) = y^{1/3}$, and $(f^{-1})'(0)$ is infinite.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

The derivative of the inverse is a special case of the **Inverse Function Theorem** (IFT), one of the central theorems of local analysis. Versions exist in every layer of differential geometry.

### IFT on Manifolds

For a $C^1$ diffeomorphism $f: M \to N$ between manifolds, the differential $df_p: T_pM \to T_{f(p)}N$ is an isomorphism, and:

$$d(f^{-1})_{f(p)} = (df_p)^{-1}$$

The scalar equation $(f^{-1})'(b) = 1/f'(a)$ is the case $M = N = \mathbb{R}$.

### Normalizing Flows in Generative ML

Models like Real-NVP and Glow compose invertible transformations with tractable Jacobians. The log-likelihood:

$$\log p_Y(y) = \log p_X(x) - \log |\det J_f(x)|, \quad x = f^{-1}(y)$$

Computing $\det J_f$ efficiently (triangular architecture) is the core of flow design. The inverse rule guarantees that the Jacobian of $f^{-1}$ is $J_f^{-1}$, which is computable.

### Implied Volatility in Black-Scholes

In option pricing, implied volatility $\sigma_{\text{imp}}(K, T)$ is implicitly defined by $\text{BS}(\sigma) = V_{\text{market}}$, where BS is the Black-Scholes formula. The sensitivity:

$$\frac{d\sigma_{\text{imp}}}{dV} = \frac{1}{\text{BS}'(\sigma)} = \frac{1}{\text{Vega}}$$

Exactly the inverse derivative formula — and the reason Vega can never be zero in the Black-Scholes model without creating inversion problems.

### Lambert W Function

$W(x)$ satisfies $W(x) e^{W(x)} = x$. Differentiating implicitly:

$$W'(x) = \frac{W(x)}{x(1 + W(x))}$$

Appears in growth models, nonlinear heat equations, and graph theory.

### Analytic Connection: Differentiability of the Inverse

The IFT is strictly stronger than the elementary definition: it guarantees that $f^{-1}$ inherits $C^k$ from $f$ ($f \in C^k \Rightarrow f^{-1} \in C^k$). Pathological cases ($f'$ oscillating to zero) are relevant in ODEs with non-smooth boundary conditions.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practical Application">

**When to use?** Whenever you need to differentiate $\arcsin$, $\arccos$, $\arctan$, $\ln$, $\log_a$, $a^x$, or any inverse hyperbolic function.

### Differentiation Workflow

1. Identify: $y = f^{-1}(x)$, therefore $f(y) = x$.
2. Differentiate both sides with respect to $x$ (chain rule): $f'(y) \cdot y' = 1$.
3. Isolate $y'$: $y' = 1/f'(y)$.
4. Rewrite in terms of $x$ using trigonometric or algebraic identities.

### Verification with SymPy

```python
import sympy as sp
x = sp.symbols('x', real=True)
print(sp.diff(sp.asin(x), x))    # 1/sqrt(1 - x**2)
print(sp.diff(sp.acos(x), x))    # -1/sqrt(1 - x**2)
print(sp.diff(sp.atan(x), x))    # 1/(x**2 + 1)
print(sp.diff(sp.log(x), x))     # 1/x
print(sp.diff(sp.log(x, 2), x))  # 1/(x*log(2))
print(sp.diff(sp.asinh(x), x))   # 1/sqrt(x**2 + 1)
```

### Common Mistakes

- $\arcsin x \neq 1/\sin x$ — they are completely different functions.
- Domain of $\arcsin$: only $|x| \leq 1$. Outside this, it doesn't exist.
- Domain of $\text{arccosh}$: only $x \geq 1$.
- Applying the formula when $f'(a) = 0$ — the inverse has a vertical tangent then.

### Quick Reference Table

| Need to differentiate | Use |
|---|---|
| $\arctan(ax + b)$ | $a/(1+(ax+b)^2)$ |
| $\arcsin(\sqrt{x})$ | $1/(2\sqrt{x}\sqrt{1-x})$ |
| $\ln(f(x))$ | $f'(x)/f(x)$ (logarithmic differentiation) |
| $a^{g(x)}$ | $a^{g(x)} \ln a \cdot g'(x)$ |

### Brazilian Context

In financial market data analysis, the $\arctan$ function appears in modeling bounded extreme returns. In GPS (including the Brazilian GBAS system implemented in civil aviation), the angular sensitivity $d\theta/dh = d/dh[\arctan(h/d)] = d/(d^2+h^2)$ determines the altitude error propagated to position error.

## Solved Examples

<Exemplo titulo="Derivative of arctan via implicit differentiation (fundamental)" numero="1">

**Problem:** Differentiate $(\arctan x)'$ starting from the definition via implicit differentiation.

**Strategy:** Use the standard method: write the inverse relation $\tan y = x$, differentiate implicitly, and rewrite the result in terms of $x$.

**Solution:**

1. Let $y = \arctan x$. By definition of the inverse, $\tan y = x$ with $y \in (-\pi/2,\, \pi/2)$.

2. Differentiate both sides with respect to $x$:
$$\sec^2 y \cdot \frac{dy}{dx} = 1$$

3. Isolate $dy/dx$:
$$\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{1}{\sec^2 y}$$

4. Rewrite $\sec^2 y$: using the identity $\sec^2 y = 1 + \tan^2 y$ and substituting $\tan y = x$:
$$\sec^2 y = 1 + x^2$$

5. Therefore:
$$\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{1}{1 + x^2}$$

**Verification:** At $x = 0$: $(f^{-1})'(0) = 1/(1+0) = 1$. Since $f(x) = \tan x$ has $f'(0) = \sec^2 0 = 1$, and $1/f'(0) = 1$. It checks out.

**Source.** [Active Calculus §2.6](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-2-6-inverse.html) — Boelkins · CC-BY-NC-SA.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Derivative of ln via inverse of exp (fundamental)" numero="2">

**Problem:** Differentiate $(\ln x)'$ starting from $e^y = x$.

**Strategy:** $\ln$ is the inverse of $e^x$. Apply implicit differentiation and use $e^y = x$.

**Solution:**

1. Let $y = \ln x$. Then $e^y = x$ (definition of the natural logarithm).

2. Differentiate both sides with respect to $x$:
$$e^y \cdot \frac{dy}{dx} = 1$$

3. Isolate $dy/dx$:
$$\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{1}{e^y}$$

4. Substitute $e^y = x$:
$$\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{1}{x}$$

**Result:** $(\ln x)' = 1/x$ for $x > 0$.

**Derivative of $\log_a x$:** Since $\log_a x = \ln x / \ln a$:
$$(\log_a x)' = \frac{1}{x \ln a}$$

**Verification:** At $x = 1$: $(\ln x)'\big|_{x=1} = 1$. The tangent line to $y = \ln x$ at $(1, 0)$ has slope 1. Checks out.

**Source.** [OpenStax Calculus Volume 1 §3.7](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/3-7-derivatives-of-inverse-functions) · CC-BY-NC-SA.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Chain rule with arcsin — composition arcsin(2x)" numero="3">

**Problem:** Calculate $\dfrac{d}{dx}\arcsin(2x)$.

**Strategy:** Apply the formula $(\arcsin u)' = u'/\sqrt{1-u^2}$ with $u = 2x$.

**Solution:**

1. Identify $u = 2x$, so $u' = 2$.

2. Apply the chain rule:
$$\frac{d}{dx}\arcsin(2x) = \frac{2}{\sqrt{1 - (2x)^2}} = \frac{2}{\sqrt{1 - 4x^2}}$$

3. The result is valid for $|2x| < 1$, or $x \in (-1/2,\, 1/2)$.

**Verification:** For $x = 0$: derivative $= 2/\sqrt{1} = 2$. By finite difference: $\arcsin(2 \cdot 0.01) \approx 0.020$, divided by $0.01$ gives $\approx 2.00$. Checks out.

**Source.** [Active Calculus §2.6, Exercise 2.6.2](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-2-6-inverse.html) — Boelkins · CC-BY-NC-SA.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Inverse theorem without explicit formula — calculate (f^{-1})'(b)" numero="4">

**Problem:** Let $f(x) = x^3 + 2x + 1$. Calculate $(f^{-1})'(4)$.

**Strategy:** We don't know $f^{-1}$ explicitly, but we can use $(f^{-1})'(b) = 1/f'(f^{-1}(b))$. We need to find $a$ such that $f(a) = 4$.

**Solution:**

1. Find $a$ such that $f(a) = 4$:
$$a^3 + 2a + 1 = 4 \implies a^3 + 2a - 3 = 0$$

Test $a = 1$: $1 + 2 - 3 = 0$. So $a = 1$, meaning $f(1) = 4$.

2. Calculate $f'(x) = 3x^2 + 2$.

3. Evaluate at $a = 1$: $f'(1) = 3(1)^2 + 2 = 5$.

4. Apply the theorem:
$$(f^{-1})'(4) = \frac{1}{f'(1)} = \frac{1}{5}$$

**Verification:** $f$ is strictly increasing ($f'(x) = 3x^2 + 2 > 0$ for all $x$), so $f^{-1}$ exists. At $b = 4$, the slope of $f^{-1}$ is $1/5$.

**Source.** [Active Calculus §2.6, Exercise 2.6.4](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-2-6-inverse.html) — Boelkins · CC-BY-NC-SA.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Logarithmic differentiation — derivative of x^x" numero="5">

**Problem:** Differentiate $y = x^x$ for $x > 0$.

**Strategy:** $x^x$ is neither a fixed power nor a fixed exponential — use logarithmic differentiation: take $\ln$ of both sides and differentiate.

**Solution:**

1. Take $\ln$ of both sides:
$$\ln y = x \ln x$$

2. Differentiate implicitly with respect to $x$:
$$\frac{1}{y} \cdot \frac{dy}{dx} = \ln x + x \cdot \frac{1}{x} = \ln x + 1$$

3. Isolate $dy/dx$ by multiplying by $y = x^x$:
$$\frac{dy}{dx} = x^x (\ln x + 1)$$

**Particular Case:** At $x = 1$: $dy/dx = 1^1(\ln 1 + 1) = 1 \cdot 1 = 1$. The tangent line to $y = x^x$ at $(1, 1)$ has slope 1.

**Generalization:** For $y = f(x)^{g(x)}$, logarithmic differentiation gives:
$$\frac{dy}{dx} = f(x)^{g(x)} \left(g'(x)\ln f(x) + \frac{g(x) f'(x)}{f(x)}\right)$$

**Source.** [OpenStax Calculus Volume 1 §3.7](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/3-7-derivatives-of-inverse-functions) · CC-BY-NC-SA.
</Exemplo>
