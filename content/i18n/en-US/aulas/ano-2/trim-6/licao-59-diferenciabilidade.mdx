### Differentiability at a Point

<Definicao titulo="Differentiable Function at a">

Let $f$ be defined on an open interval containing $a$. We say $f$ is **differentiable at $a$** if the limit

$$f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h}$$

exists and is a finite real number. The value of this limit is the **derivative of $f$ at $a$**.

**One-sided derivatives.** We define

$$f'_-(a) = \lim_{h \to 0^-} \frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h}, \qquad f'_+(a) = \lim_{h \to 0^+} \frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h}.$$

$f$ is differentiable at $a$ if and only if $f'_-(a) = f'_+(a)$ (both finite and equal).

</Definicao>

> "If $f'(a)$ exists, we say that $f$ is differentiable at $a$. If $f$ is differentiable at every number in an open interval $(a, b)$, then $f$ is differentiable on $(a, b)$." — [OpenStax Calculus Volume 1, §3.2](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/3-2-the-derivative-as-a-function)

### Fundamental Theorem (Differentiability Implies Continuity)

<Teorema titulo="Differentiability Implies Continuity">

If $f$ is differentiable at $a$, then $f$ is continuous at $a$.

**Proof.** For $h \neq 0$:

$$f(a+h) - f(a) = \frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h} \cdot h.$$

As $h \to 0$: the first factor $\to f'(a)$ (finite, by hypothesis) and the second $\to 0$, so the product $\to 0$. Therefore $\lim_{h \to 0} f(a+h) = f(a)$, meaning $f$ is continuous at $a$. $\square$

**The converse is false.** $f(x) = |x|$ is continuous at $0$ but not differentiable at $0$.

</Teorema>

> "If $f$ is differentiable at $a$, then $f$ is continuous at $a$." — [Active Calculus, §1.7](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-1-7-diff-cont.html), Boelkins 2024 (Theorem 1.7.1)

### $C^k$ Hierarchy

<Definicao titulo="$C^k$ Classes of Regularity">

Let $I \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ be an open interval.

- $C^0(I)$: continuous functions on $I$.
- $C^1(I)$: differentiable functions on $I$ with a continuous $f'$.
- $C^k(I)$: $k$ times differentiable with continuous $f^{(k)}$ ($k \geq 1$).
- $C^\infty(I) = \bigcap_{k \geq 0} C^k(I)$: **smooth functions** (infinitely differentiable).
- $C^\omega(I)$: **analytic functions** — represented by a Taylor series convergent in every neighborhood of each point.

**Strict Inclusions:**

$$C^\omega \subsetneq C^\infty \subsetneq \cdots \subsetneq C^2 \subsetneq C^1 \subsetneq C^0.$$

</Definicao>

### Example: $C^\infty$ but not $C^\omega$ (Cauchy)

$$f(x) = \begin{cases} e^{-1/x^2}, & x > 0 \\ 0, & x \leq 0 \end{cases}$$

This function is $C^\infty(\mathbb{R})$ and $f^{(n)}(0) = 0$ for all $n \geq 0$, but $f \not\equiv 0$. Thus $f \notin C^\omega$ — it definitively separates the smooth and analytic classes.

### Weierstrass Function

$$W(x) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} a^n \cos(b^n \pi x), \quad 0 < a < 1,\ b \text{ an odd integer},\ ab > 1 + \tfrac{3}{2}\pi.$$

<Insight>

$W$ is continuous on all of $\mathbb{R}$ and not differentiable **at any point**. Weierstrass (1872) shattered the 19th-century intuition that "continuous implies differentiable almost everywhere." The uniform continuity of the series (dominated by $\sum a^n$) guarantees $W \in C^0$; the non-differentiability arises from the uncontrolled growth of the series' partial derivatives.

</Insight>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

Imagine a path drawn on the ground.

In some places, the path is **smooth** — you can walk on it without feeling any bumps. In these places, math says the function is **differentiable**.

In other places, the path has a **sharp turn** (like the letter "V") — when you reach the turn, you have to change direction suddenly. In these places, the function is **not differentiable**.

Can you keep walking on the sharp turn without lifting your foot? Yes — the path isn't torn. But can you change direction suddenly? Yes. That's the difference between **continuous** (no tears) and **differentiable** (no sharp turns).

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

A function is **differentiable** at a point if its graph is **smooth** at that point — no sharp turns, no tears, no sudden changes in direction.

### The Classic Example: $|x|$

The graph of $f(x) = |x|$ looks like the letter "V". At the point $x = 0$, there's a **sharp turn**. If you try to draw a tangent line at that point, you can't: from the left side, the slope is $-1$; from the right side, it's $+1$. The derivative doesn't exist at the sharp turn.

But the graph has no tears — you can trace it with your pencil without lifting it. So $|x|$ is **continuous** but **not differentiable** at $0$.

### Practical Rule

- Differentiable $\Rightarrow$ continuous. Always.
- Continuous $\Rightarrow$ differentiable? **Not necessarily.** The sharp turn of $|x|$ proves this.

### The 4 Villains of Differentiability

1.  **Corner:** Different slopes coming from both sides ($|x|$).
2.  **Cusp:** Slope goes to $\pm\infty$ from both sides ($x^{2/3}$).
3.  **Vertical Tangent:** Slope $\to \infty$ (from $\sqrt[3]{x}$).
4.  **Jump or Hole:** The function isn't even continuous.

### Smoothness Hierarchy

- $C^0$: Continuous (no tears).
- $C^1$: Derivative exists and is continuous (no sharp turns).
- $C^2$: Second derivative exists and is continuous (smooth curvature).
- $C^\infty$: You can differentiate as many times as you want. Polynomials, $\sin$, $\cos$, $e^x$ are all $C^\infty$.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old">

Differentiability is a **stronger** condition than continuity. Every differentiable function is continuous, but the reverse is not true.

### How to Check Differentiability at a Point $a$

**Step 1.** Is $f(a)$ defined? If not, stop.

**Step 2.** Is $f$ continuous at $a$? (Calculate $\lim_{x \to a} f(x)$ and compare with $f(a)$.) If not, it's not differentiable.

**Step 3.** Calculate the one-sided derivatives:

$$f'_-(a) = \lim_{h \to 0^-} \frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h}, \qquad f'_+(a) = \lim_{h \to 0^+} \frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h}.$$

If both exist and are equal (and finite), $f$ is differentiable at $a$, and $f'(a)$ is that common value.

### Example: $f(x) = |x|$ at $a = 0$

$f(0) = 0$, $\lim_{x \to 0} |x| = 0$. Continuous. Now the one-sided derivatives:

$$f'_+(0) = \lim_{h \to 0^+} \frac{|h| - 0}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0^+} \frac{h}{h} = 1.$$

$$f'_-(0) = \lim_{h \to 0^-} \frac{|h| - 0}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0^-} \frac{-h}{h} = -1.$$

$f'_+(0) \neq f'_-(0)$. Conclusion: **not differentiable at $0$**.

### Example: Piecewise Function

$$g(x) = \begin{cases} x^2, & x \leq 1 \\ 2x - 1, & x > 1 \end{cases}$$

At $a = 1$: $g(1) = 1$, $\lim_{x \to 1^+}(2x-1) = 1$. Continuous.

$g'_-(1) = \lim_{h \to 0^-} \dfrac{(1+h)^2 - 1}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0^-}(2 + h) = 2$.

$g'_+(1) = \lim_{h \to 0^+} \dfrac{(2(1+h)-1) - 1}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0^+} \dfrac{2h}{h} = 2$.

$g'_-(1) = g'_+(1) = 2$. Conclusion: $g$ is differentiable at $1$ and $g'(1) = 2$.

### $C^k$ Classes in Practice

A **cubic spline** in CAD is $C^2$: the curve, its slope, and its curvature are all continuous — that's why the surface looks "smooth to the touch." If it were only $C^0$, there would be visible sharp turns.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an Engineering Student">

The hierarchy of regularity $C^k$ governs which mathematics can be applied: existence of ODE solutions, convergence order of numerical methods, validity of the chain rule, etc.

### Formal Proof: Differentiability Implies Continuity

**Theorem.** If $f'(a)$ exists (and is finite), then $f$ is continuous at $a$.

**Proof.** For $h \neq 0$:

$$f(a+h) - f(a) = \frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h} \cdot h.$$

Taking $h \to 0$: the first factor $\to f'(a)$ (finite, by hypothesis) and the second $\to 0$. Thus $f(a+h) - f(a) \to 0$, therefore $f$ is continuous at $a$. $\square$

### Differentiable with Discontinuous Derivative

Not every differentiable function has a continuous derivative, i.e., differentiable $\not\Rightarrow$ $C^1$. The classic counterexample:

$$f(x) = \begin{cases} x^2 \sin(1/x), & x \neq 0 \\ 0, & x = 0 \end{cases}$$

By definition: $f'(0) = \lim_{h \to 0} \dfrac{h^2\sin(1/h)}{h} = \lim_{h\to 0} h\sin(1/h) = 0$.

For $x \neq 0$: $f'(x) = 2x\sin(1/x) - \cos(1/x)$.

The term $\cos(1/x)$ oscillates between $-1$ and $1$ as $x \to 0$, therefore $\lim_{x \to 0} f'(x)$ does not exist. Thus $f' \notin C^0$ and $f \notin C^1$.

### Applications by Domain

**Numerical Analysis.** For $C^k$ functions, quadrature error decays as $O(h^k)$ with step $h$. Functions with corners ($C^0$ but not $C^1$) degrade convergence order.

**ODEs.** Picard-Lindelöf theorem guarantees a unique local solution when the vector field $f(t, y)$ is $C^1$ in $y$. Corners in the field can break uniqueness.

**Mechanics.** Displacement $u(t) \in C^2$ is the minimum regularity for $m\ddot{u} = F$ to make classical sense. Rigid impact creates a jump in $\dot{u}$: transition to weak solution.

**Splines.** Natural cubic spline: $C^2$ at nodes — continuous curvature.

**Optimization.** First-order condition $\nabla f(x^*) = 0$ requires $f \in C^1$. For non-smooth functions (ReLU, $|x|$), use subdifferential.

### Verification of $C^1$ for Piecewise Functions

For $f$ defined by $f_1$ on $(-\infty, a]$ and $f_2$ on $[a, \infty)$, $f \in C^1$ requires simultaneously:

1.  Continuity: $f_1(a) = f_2(a)$.
2.  Equality of derivatives: $f_1'(a) = f_2'(a)$.

This yields two equations to adjust free parameters.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a Senior Professional">

Regularity is the currency of modern analysis. Each increment $C^k \to C^{k+1}$ unlocks an arsenal of tools.

### Sobolev Spaces

Beyond the $C^k$ scale (classical pointwise regularity):

- $W^{k,p}(\Omega)$: functions with $k$ weak derivatives in $L^p(\Omega)$.
- $H^k(\Omega) = W^{k,2}(\Omega)$: Hilbert space; natural structure for linear PDEs via weak formulation.
- $C^{k,\alpha}(\Omega)$ (Hölder): $|D^k f(x) - D^k f(y)| \leq C|x-y|^\alpha$. Lipschitz = $C^{0,1}$.

**Sobolev Embedding.** $W^{k,p}(\Omega) \hookrightarrow C^l(\bar\Omega)$ when $k - n/p > l$ ($n = \dim\Omega$). Converts $L^p$ regularity of derivatives into classical pointwise regularity.

### Harmonic Analysis and Regularity

Decay of $\hat f(\xi)$ (Fourier transform) encodes regularity of $f$:

$$f \in H^s(\mathbb{R}^n) \iff \int (1 + |\xi|^2)^s |\hat f(\xi)|^2 \, d\xi < \infty.$$

This allows defining $H^s$ for $s \in \mathbb{R}$ — including fractional regularity. $s > n/2$ implies $f$ is continuous (by Sobolev).

### Rademacher's Theorem

Every Lipschitz function $f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ is differentiable **almost everywhere** (in the Lebesgue measure sense). $|x|$ is Lipschitz and non-differentiable at $0$ — a set of measure zero; a qualitatively optimal result.

### Weierstrass Function — Construction

Weierstrass (1872) surprised mathematicians with

$$W(x) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty a^n \cos(b^n \pi x)$$

where $0 < a < 1$ and $b$ is an odd integer with $ab > 1 + \frac{3}{2}\pi$.

- $W$ is continuous (series uniformly convergent, dominated by geometric series $\sum a^n$).
- $W$ is not differentiable **at any point** in $\mathbb{R}$.

Hardy (1916) improved: $ab \geq 1$ suffices.

### ML and Non-Smooth Optimization

- **ReLU:** $\max(0, x)$, non-differentiable at $0$. Subgradient $\partial \text{ReLU}(0) = [0, 1]$.
- **Hinge loss:** $\max(0, 1 - y\hat y$ — corner at $y\hat y = 1$.
- **L1 regularization** ($\|w\|_1$): corners at $w_i = 0$, promotes sparsity.

**Proximal Operator.** For convex non-smooth $g$, $\text{prox}_{\lambda g}(v) = \arg\min_x \{g(x) + \|x-v\|^2/(2\lambda)\}$ replaces the gradient step. For $g = \|\cdot\|_1$: soft-thresholding.

### Quantitative Finance

Call option payoff: $\max(S_T - K, 0)$. Continuous in $S_T$ but non-differentiable at $S_T = K$. For standard European option under Black-Scholes, the Greek Delta $\Delta = \partial V/\partial S$ is continuous, but Gamma $\Gamma = \partial^2 V/\partial S^2$ diverges as maturity $T \to 0^+$.

### Whitney Extension

If $f \in C^k(F)$ for $F \subset \mathbb{R}^n$ closed (with compatible jet data), there exists $\tilde f \in C^k(\mathbb{R}^n)$ with $\tilde f|_F = f$. Central tool in differential geometry and PDEs.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practice — Step-by-Step Verification">

### When to Check Differentiability

**Scenario 1 — Function Given Explicitly.**

Polynomials, $e^x$, $\sin x$, $\cos x$, $\ln x$ (on its domain): all $C^\infty$. Composition and products of $C^k$ functions are $C^k$.

**Scenario 2 — Function with Absolute Value.**

$|g(x)|$ has a corner where $g(x) = 0$ (unless $g'(x) = 0$ too — like in $x^2|x| = |x|^3$, which is $C^1$ at $0$).

**Scenario 3 — Piecewise Defined Function.**

At the junction point $a$, check:
1.  Continuity: the one-sided limits coincide and equal $f(a)$.
2.  One-sided derivatives: calculate $f'_-(a)$ and $f'_+(a)$ and compare.

**Scenario 4 — Finding Parameters for $C^1$.**

Two branches with free parameters: solve the system $f_1(a) = f_2(a)$ and $f_1'(a) = f_2'(a)$.

### Quick Numerical Examples

**Check if $f(x) = x^{2/3}$ is differentiable at $0$:**

$$f'(0) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{h^{2/3} - 0}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} h^{-1/3}.$$

Vertical tangent — not differentiable (derivative is not a finite number).

**Find $a$ such that $f(x) = \begin{cases} ax + 3, & x \leq 2 \\ x^2 - 1, & x > 2 \end{cases}$ is $C^1$ at $2$:**

Continuity: $2a + 3 = 4 - 1 = 3 \Rightarrow a = 0$.

Derivatives: $f'_-(2) = a = 0$. $f'_+(2) = 2 \cdot 2 = 4$. With $a = 0$: $0 \neq 4$. Impossible to be $C^1$ with this functional form.

### Common Mistakes

- Concluding "continuous $\Rightarrow$ differentiable." False: $|x|$ is the canonical counterexample.
- Calculating $f'(x)$ by differentiation rules and substituting $x = a$ without checking if $a$ is a non-differentiable point.
- Applying the chain rule when the inner function has a corner at the point of interest.
- Forgetting to check continuity before checking one-sided derivatives in piecewise functions.

</Porta>

## Solved Examples

<Exemplo titulo="Verifying Differentiability of |x| at Zero (Basic)" numero="1">

**Problem.** Prove that $f(x) = |x|$ is continuous at $0$ but not differentiable at $0$.

**Strategy.** Check continuity via limit and then calculate the one-sided derivatives. If they differ, the derivative does not exist.

**Solution.**

1.  **Continuity.** $\lim_{x \to 0} |x| = 0 = f(0)$. Continuous.

2.  **Right-hand derivative.**
    $$f'_+(0) = \lim_{h \to 0^+} \frac{|0+h| - |0|}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0^+} \frac{h}{h} = 1.$$

3.  **Left-hand derivative.**
    $$f'_-(0) = \lim_{h \to 0^-} \frac{|0+h| - 0}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0^-} \frac{-h}{h} = -1.$$

4.  **Conclusion.** $f'_+(0) = 1 \neq -1 = f'_-(0)$. The two-sided limit does not exist. $f$ is not differentiable at $0$.

**Verification.** Geometrically: the graph forms a V-shape corner at $0$. Any line through $(0, 0)$ cuts the graph on one side but doesn't tangent from both.

**Source.** [Active Calculus §1.7](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-1-7-diff-cont.html) — Boelkins, 2024 · CC-BY-NC-SA.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Differentiability of a Piecewise Function with Discontinuity" numero="2">

**Problem.** Determine if the function

$$g(x) = \begin{cases} x^2 + 1, & x \leq 2 \\ 5x - 7, & x > 2 \end{cases}$$

is differentiable at $x = 2$.

**Strategy.** Check continuity first; if it's not continuous, it's already not differentiable — saves work.

**Solution.**

1.  **Continuity at $2$.**

    $$\lim_{x \to 2^-} (x^2 + 1) = 4 + 1 = 5, \qquad \lim_{x \to 2^+} (5x - 7) = 10 - 7 = 3.$$

    The one-sided limits are $5 \neq 3$. The function is **not continuous** at $2$.

2.  **Conclusion.** Discontinuity implies non-differentiability. $g$ is not differentiable at $x = 2$.

**Verification.** By the theorem: differentiable implies continuous. Therefore non-continuous implies non-differentiable (contrapositive). We don't need to calculate one-sided derivatives.

**Source.** [OpenStax Calculus Volume 1 §3.2](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/3-2-the-derivative-as-a-function) — OpenStax, 2016 · CC-BY-NC-SA.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Differentiability via Definition with Squeeze Theorem" numero="3">

**Problem.** Let

$$h(x) = \begin{cases} x^2 \sin(1/x), & x \neq 0 \\ 0, & x = 0 \end{cases}$$

Prove that $h$ is differentiable at $0$ and find $h'(0)$.

**Strategy.** Use the definition directly — differentiation rules don't apply at $x = 0$ because $1/x$ is undefined there. Estimate using the Squeeze Theorem.

**Solution.**

1.  **Apply the definition.**
    $$h'(0) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{h(0+h) - h(0)}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{h^2 \sin(1/h)}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} h\sin(1/h).$$

2.  **Estimate.**
    $$|h \sin(1/h)| \leq |h| \cdot 1 = |h| \to 0 \quad \text{as } h \to 0.$$

3.  **By the Squeeze Theorem.** $-|h| \leq h\sin(1/h) \leq |h|$, and $|h| \to 0$, therefore $h\sin(1/h) \to 0$.

4.  **Conclusion.** $h'(0) = 0$. $h$ is differentiable at $0$.

**Verification.** For $x \neq 0$: $h'(x) = 2x\sin(1/x) - \cos(1/x)$. The term $\cos(1/x)$ oscillates as $x \to 0$, so $h' \notin C^0$ — $h$ is differentiable but not $C^1$.

**Source.** [Active Calculus §1.7](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-1-7-diff-cont.html) — Boelkins, 2024 · CC-BY-NC-SA.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Finding Parameters to Ensure C¹ (Advanced)" numero="4">

**Problem.** Find the values of $a$ and $b$ such that the function

$$p(x) = \begin{cases} ax^2 + b, & x \leq 1 \\ 2x^3 + 3, & x > 1 \end{cases}$$

is $C^1$ on $\mathbb{R}$.

**Strategy.** To be $C^1$ at the junction point $x = 1$, we require (1) continuity and (2) equality of derivatives. These two requirements form a $2 \times 2$ system in $a, b$.

**Solution.**

1.  **Continuity condition at $x = 1$.**

    $$p(1^-) = a \cdot 1^2 + b = a + b, \qquad p(1^+) = 2 \cdot 1^3 + 3 = 5.$$

    Equation (I): $a + b = 5$.

2.  **Equality of derivative condition at $x = 1$.**

    For $x < 1$: $p'(x) = 2ax$, so $p'_-(1) = 2a$.

    For $x > 1$: $p'(x) = 6x^2$, so $p'_+(1) = 6$.

    Equation (II): $2a = 6 \Rightarrow a = 3$.

3.  **Solve the system.** From (II): $a = 3$. Substituting into (I): $3 + b = 5 \Rightarrow b = 2$.

4.  **Verification.**

    - $p(1) = 3 + 2 = 5 = 2 + 3$. Continuous.
    - $p'_-(1) = 2 \cdot 3 \cdot 1 = 6 = p'_+(1)$. Continuous derivative.

**Source.** [APEX Calculus §2.1](https://www.apexcalculus.com/) — Hartman et al., 2024 · CC-BY-NC.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Analyzing Cusp and C^k Hierarchy for x^(2/3) (Challenge)" numero="5">

**Problem.** Let $f(x) = x^{2/3}$.

(a) Show that $f$ is not differentiable at $0$.

(b) Determine the maximum regularity class of $f$ around $0$.

(c) Calculate $f'(x)$ for $x \neq 0$ and analyze its behavior as $x \to 0$.

**Strategy.** Use the definition of the derivative at $0$ and power rules for $x \neq 0$.

**Solution.**

**(a) Derivative at $0$ via definition.**

$$f'(0) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{h^{2/3} - 0}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} h^{2/3 - 1} = \lim_{h \to 0} h^{-1/3}.$$

As $h \to 0^+$: $h^{-1/3} \to +\infty$. As $h \to 0^-$: $h^{-1/3} = -|h|^{-1/3} \to -\infty$.

The limit is not a finite real number. Thus $f$ is **not differentiable at $0$** — a cusp.

**(b) Hierarchy.** On any interval $I$ not containing $0$, $f \in C^\infty$. But on any interval containing $0$, $f \in C^0$ (continuous) but $f \notin C^1$ (not differentiable at $0$). Maximum regularity: $C^0$.

**(c) For $x \neq 0$.**

$$f'(x) = \frac{2}{3} x^{-1/3} = \frac{2}{3x^{1/3}}.$$

As $x \to 0^+$: $f'(x) \to +\infty$. As $x \to 0^-$: $f'(x) \to -\infty$.

The graph has a cusp pointing upwards at $0$: the tangent lines become increasingly vertical as you approach $0$, diverging to $+\infty$ on the right and $-\infty$ on the left.

**Verification.** $f(0) = 0^{2/3} = 0$, $\lim_{x\to 0} x^{2/3} = 0$. Continuous at $0$. The one-sided derivatives $\to \pm\infty$: confirms a cusp, not a vertical tangent (which would have both one-sided derivatives going to $+\infty$).

**Source.** [OpenStax Calculus Volume 1 §3.2](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/3-2-the-derivative-as-a-function) — OpenStax, 2016 · CC-BY-NC-SA.
</Exemplo>

      <li><Eq>{`\\exists M > 0`}</Eq> such that <Eq>{`|f'(x)| \\leq M`}</Eq> for all <Eq>{`x \\in [a,b]`}</Eq>.</li>
      <li>By MVT: <Eq>{`f(x)-f(y) = f'(c)(x-y)`}</Eq> for some <Eq>{`c`}</Eq> between <Eq>{`x`}</Eq> and <Eq>{`y`}</Eq>.</li>
      <li><Eq>{`|f(x)-f(y)| = |f'(c)||x-y| \\leq M|x-y|`}</Eq>. Therefore Lipschitz. <Eq>{`\\square`}</Eq></li>
    </ol>
    <p><em>Mnemonic:</em> <Eq>{`C^1`}</Eq> implies Lipschitz implies <Eq>{`C^0`}</Eq> — the full hierarchy flowing downwards.</p>
