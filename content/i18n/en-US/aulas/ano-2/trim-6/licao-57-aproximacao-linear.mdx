### Linearization

<Definicao titulo="Linearization of f at a">

Let $f$ be differentiable at $a$. The **linearization** (or **linear approximation**) of $f$ at $a$ is the affine function

$$L(x) = f(a) + f'(a)(x - a).$$

It is the unique affine function satisfying $L(a) = f(a)$ and $L'(a) = f'(a)$: it has the same value and the same derivative as $f$ at the base point.

</Definicao>

> "If $f$ is differentiable at $a$, then $L(x) = f(a) + f'(a)(x - a)$ is called the *linearization* of $f$ at $a$. The approximation $f(x) \approx L(x)$ is called the *linear approximation* or *tangent line approximation* of $f$ at $a$." — [OpenStax Calculus Vol.1 §4.2](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/4-2-linear-approximations-and-differentials)

### Differential

<Definicao titulo="Differential dy">

For $y = f(x)$, the **differential** of $y$ is

$$dy = f'(x)\,dx,$$

where $dx$ is an arbitrary (not necessarily infinitesimal) change in $x$. The differential represents the **linear change** in $f$: it is how much the tangent line rises or falls when $x$ changes by $dx$.

The actual change is $\Delta y = f(x + dx) - f(x)$. As $dx \to 0$:

$$\Delta y - dy = O(dx^2), \quad \text{so} \quad \Delta y \approx dy.$$

</Definicao>

> "We define $dx$ and $dy$ as real variables so that the following equation holds: $dy = f'(x)\,dx$. The differential $dy$ is a linear approximation of the actual change $\Delta y$." — [OpenStax Calculus Vol.1 §4.2](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/4-2-linear-approximations-and-differentials)

### Error Estimation via Taylor

<Teorema titulo="Linearization Error (Taylor with Remainder)">

If $f$ is twice differentiable on an interval containing $a$ and $x$, then there exists $\xi$ between $a$ and $x$ such that

$$f(x) = f(a) + f'(a)(x - a) + \frac{f''(\xi)}{2}(x - a)^2.$$

The **error** of the linearization is therefore $f(x) - L(x) = \dfrac{f''(\xi)}{2}(x-a)^2$. If $|f''(t)| \leq M_2$ for all $t$ between $a$ and $x$, then

$$|f(x) - L(x)| \leq \frac{M_2}{2}(x - a)^2.$$

The error is **quadratic** in the distance to the base point — it shrinks much faster than the distance.

</Teorema>

### Error Propagation

For $y = f(x)$ with uncertainty $\sigma_x$ in $x$:

$$\sigma_y \approx |f'(x)|\,\sigma_x.$$

For functions of several variables $y = f(x_1, \ldots, x_n)$ with independent errors $\sigma_i$:

$$\sigma_y^2 \approx \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}\right)^2 \sigma_i^2.$$

<Insight>

**Why is the error quadratic?** Any function differentiable at $a$ "looks locally like a straight line" — this is the essence of differential calculus. Linearization captures exactly that line. The remainder starts with the 2nd-order Taylor term, so halving $|x-a|$ reduces the error by a factor of 4.

</Insight>

Imagine you want to know how much a 4.5-centimeter radius apple weighs. You only have a scale calibrated for exactly 4 centimeters.

The idea: "a slightly bigger apple weighs slightly more." How much more? If it grows in a straight line, it's easy to calculate.

This is linear approximation: when something changes a **little bit**, we pretend it changes in a straight line. It works very well for small changes.

A line is the simplest way to describe a change. And near any point, the curve looks very much like the line that touches it.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

You want to calculate $\sqrt{4.1}$ without a calculator. You know $\sqrt{4} = 2$.

The function $f(x) = \sqrt{x}$ has a **slope** at $x = 4$. This slope is the derivative:

$$f'(x) = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}, \quad f'(4) = \frac{1}{4}.$$

Starting from $x = 4$ and moving $0.1$ to the right, the function increases by $0.1 \times \frac{1}{4} = 0.025$.

$$\sqrt{4.1} \approx 2 + 0.025 = 2.025.$$

Actual value: $2.02485\ldots$ — an error of only $0.00015$. Excellent for a mental calculation.

### The General Recipe

1. Find $a$ close to $x$ where $f(a)$ is easy to calculate.
2. Calculate $f'(a)$.
3. Use $f(x) \approx f(a) + f'(a)(x - a)$.

### Why Does It Work?

The **tangent line** to the graph of $f$ at $(a, f(a))$ is the best straight line passing through that point that follows the curve. For short distances, the curve and the line are practically identical.

| Want to approximate | Chosen $a$ | Mental Calculation |
|---|---|---|
| $\sqrt{25.1}$ | $25$ | $5 + 0.1/10 = 5.01$ |
| $e^{0.1}$ | $0$ | $1.1$ |
| $\sin(0.2)$ | $0$ | $0.2$ |
| $\ln(0.95)$ | $1$ | $0.95 - 1 = -0.05$ |

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old">

The linearization $L(x) = f(a) + f'(a)(x - a)$ is the most used tool in all of applied science. Every time someone builds a "small perturbation" model, they are using linearization.

### Recipe with Error Check

1. Identify $a$ close to $x$ where $f(a)$ and $f'(a)$ are known.
2. $L(x) = f(a) + f'(a)(x - a)$.
3. Estimate the error: $|f(x) - L(x)| \leq \frac{M_2}{2}(x - a)^2$ where $M_2 = \max|f''|$ on the interval.

### Differential

$dy = f'(x)\,dx$ is the linear change in $y$ when $x$ changes by $dx$.

**Engineering Rule of Thumb**: If you measure $x$ with error $\Delta x$, the error in $y = f(x)$ is approximately $|f'(x)| \cdot |\Delta x|$.

### Example: Measurement Error

You measure the radius of a sphere as $r = 5.0 \pm 0.1$ cm. Volume: $V = \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3$.

$$dV = 4\pi r^2\,dr = 4\pi(25)(0.1) = 10\pi \approx 31.4 \text{ cm}^3.$$

Nominal volume: $\approx 524$ cm³. Relative error: $31.4/524 \approx 6\%$.

### Limit of Validity

The larger $|x - a|$, the more the tangent line deviates from the curve. For functions with large $|f''|$ (high curvature), the error grows quickly. Always compare $|f''|/2 \cdot (x-a)^2$ with the required precision before using the approximation.

<Insight>

**1% Rule of Thumb:** Linearization has less than 1% error if the function doesn't curve much over the interval. For $\sin x$ around zero, this holds up to $x \approx 0.24$ radians (about 14°). For $e^x$ around zero, up to $x \approx 0.14$. For $\sqrt{x}$ around $a$, the relative error is $\approx (x-a)^2/(8a^2)$ — it decreases for large $a$.

</Insight>

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an Engineering Student">

Linearization is the **engineering of local approximation**: 1st order (linear), 2nd order (parabolic), etc. The complete toolkit is Taylor.

### Taylor with Remainder and Error Control

$$f(x) = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}(x-a)^k + R_n(x),$$

with $R_n(x) = \dfrac{f^{(n+1)}(\xi)}{(n+1)!}(x-a)^{n+1}$ (Lagrange). Linearization corresponds to $n = 1$.

**Engineer's Strategy**: Choose $n$ such that $|R_n| <$ specified tolerance. For $\sqrt{4.1}$ with tolerance $10^{-3}$:

$$|R_1| = \frac{|f''(\xi)|}{2}(0.1)^2 = \frac{1}{8 \xi^{3/2} \cdot 2}(0.01) \leq \frac{0.01}{8 \cdot 8} = 1.56 \times 10^{-4} < 10^{-3}. \quad \checkmark$$

### Cross-Domain Applications

- **Physics**: Simple pendulum. $\ddot{\theta} = -(g/L)\sin\theta$. For small $\theta$, $\sin\theta \approx \theta$: harmonic oscillator $\ddot{\theta} = -(g/L)\theta$. Period $T = 2\pi\sqrt{L/g}$, independent of $\theta$. Valid for $|\theta| \lesssim 10°$.
- **Control**: Linearization of a nonlinear system $\dot{x} = F(x)$ at an operating point $x^*$ yields $\dot{\xi} = J_F(x^*)\xi$ — the state-space matrix.
- **Finance**: Delta-hedge $\Delta V \approx \Delta_{\text{greek}} \cdot \Delta S$. The error is Gamma (2nd order, $\Gamma \cdot (\Delta S)^2/2$).
- **Optimization**: Gradient descent $\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta \nabla L(\theta)$ minimizes the linear approximation of $L$ in the direction of steepest descent.
- **Numerical**: Newton-Raphson solves $f(x) = 0$ by iterating $x_{n+1} = x_n - f(x_n)/f'(x_n)$ — each step is local linearization + finding the zero of the tangent line. Quadratic convergence: $|e_{n+1}| \leq C\,|e_n|^2$.

### Delta Method (Statistics)

For $Y = g(X)$ with $X$ concentrated around $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$:

$$\text{Var}(g(X)) \approx \bigl(g'(\mu)\bigr)^2 \sigma^2.$$

Error propagation = linearization + variances. Basis of all sensitivity analysis.

### When Linearization Fails

- $f$ is not differentiable at $a$ (e.g., $f(x) = |x|$ at $a = 0$).
- $|x - a|$ is not small and $f''$ is not negligible.
- $f'(a) = 0$ and $f''(a) \neq 0$ (minimum/maximum point): $L(x) = f(a)$ is constant; use 2nd-order Taylor.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a Senior Professional">

Linearization is the **universal paradigm of local analysis**. In any domain where a quantity depends smoothly on parameters, the first step is always: linearize.

### Qualitative Analysis of Dynamical Systems

For $\dot{x} = F(x)$ with a fixed point $x^*$ ($F(x^*) = 0$), the **Hartman–Grobman theorem** guarantees: if the Jacobian $J = DF(x^*)$ has no eigenvalue with a zero real part, then the phase portrait of the nonlinear system is **topologically conjugate** to that of the linear system $\dot{\xi} = J\xi$ in a neighborhood of $x^*$. Local stability of the nonlinear system equals the stability of the linearized system.

### Greeks as Multivariate Taylor Coefficients

$$dV \approx \Delta\,dS + \tfrac{1}{2}\Gamma\,dS^2 + \Theta\,dt + \mathcal{V}\,d\sigma + \rho\,dr + \cdots$$

Each Greek is a partial derivative of $V(S, \sigma, r, t)$. Hedging by Greeks is a truncated Taylor approximation of the exposure. The Delta hedging error is of order $\Gamma \cdot (\Delta S)^2$.

### Adjoint Analysis in PDEs

For $J(\theta) = \int \phi(u_\theta)\,dx$ where $u_\theta$ is the solution to a PDE parameterized by $\theta$, the gradient $dJ/d\theta$ can be computed via the **adjoint method**: 1 forward PDE + 1 linearized adjoint PDE — cost independent of $\dim(\theta)$. Foundation for optimization in CFD and neural field learning.

### Machine Learning: Linearization as Theoretical Basis

- **Influence Functions** (Koh–Liang, ICML 2017): Effect of removing training point $i$ on the minimizer $\theta^*$ via linearization of the KKT conditions at $\theta^*$.
- **Neural Tangent Kernel**: Networks with infinite width behave like **linear regressors** on the kernel induced by parametrization at initialization — linearization around $\theta_0$.
- **Adversarial Examples**: Linearization of $f_\theta(x)$ explains vulnerability $\sim \epsilon \|\nabla_x f_\theta(x)\|_\infty$ (Goodfellow et al.'s FGSM attack).

### Beyond Taylor: Padé and Asymptotic Expansions

When Taylor converges slowly or diverges, **Padé approximation** $f(x) \approx P_m(x)/Q_n(x)$ offers an extended radius of convergence. **Asymptotic expansions** (Watson, stationary phase method) handle $x \to \infty$ where Taylor fails — useful in optics, quantum physics, and algorithm analysis.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practice — Real Brazilian Scenarios">

### Scenario 1: Production Cost and Marginal Analysis

A metalworking plant in São Bernardo do Campo models its production cost as $C(q) = \sqrt{50q + 100}$ thousand reais, where $q$ is the quantity in tons. To estimate the additional cost of moving from $q = 30$ to $q = 31$ tons without running the full system:

$C'(q) = \dfrac{50}{2\sqrt{50q+100}} = \dfrac{25}{\sqrt{50q+100}}$.

At $q = 30$: $C(30) = \sqrt{1600} = 40$ and $C'(30) = 25/40 = 0.625$.

Estimate: $\Delta C \approx 0.625$ thousand reais (R$625). Actual value: $C(31) = \sqrt{1650} \approx 40.620$ — an error of only R$5.

### Scenario 2: Measurement Error in Topography

A civil engineer measures the area of a square plot of land with side $s = 20.0 \pm 0.05$ m. Area: $A = s^2$.

$$dA = 2s\,ds = 2(20)(0.05) = 2.0 \text{ m}^2.$$

Nominal area: 400 m². Relative error: $2.0/400 = 0.5\%$. The INCRA (National Institute of Colonization and Agrarian Reform) requires a 1% tolerance in georeferencing — this measurement is within the limit.

### Scenario 3: Savings Yield with Variable Rate

Savings yield $(1 + r/12)^{12}$ annually, where $r$ is the monthly rate. For $r = 0.5\%$ per month, the annual yield factor is $(1.005)^{12}$. Using linearization at $x = 0$ with $f(x) = (1+x)^{12}$:

$f'(0) = 12$, $L(0.005) = 1 + 12(0.005) = 1.06$.

Result: approximately 6% annually. Exact value: $(1.005)^{12} = 1.0617$ — the approximation misses by 0.2 percentage points.

### Standard Workflow

1. Choose $a$ close to $x$ where $f(a)$ and $f'(a)$ are easily calculable.
2. Compute $L(x) = f(a) + f'(a)(x - a)$.
3. Estimate maximum error: $M_2 = \max_{t \in [a,x]}|f''(t)|$, then $|\text{error}| \leq M_2(x-a)^2/2$.
4. If the error is acceptable, use $L(x)$. If not, move to 2nd-order Taylor.

## Solved Examples

<Exemplo titulo="Linear Approximation of a Square Root (Direct Application)" numero="1">

**Problem:** Using linearization, approximate $\sqrt{9.2}$ without a calculator. Estimate the maximum error.

**Strategy:** We identify $f(x) = \sqrt{x}$ and choose $a = 9$ because $\sqrt{9} = 3$ is exact and $9.2$ is close to $9$.

**Solution:**

1. $f(x) = \sqrt{x}$, $f(9) = 3$.
2. $f'(x) = \dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}$, $f'(9) = \dfrac{1}{6}$.
3. Linearization: $L(x) = 3 + \dfrac{1}{6}(x - 9)$.
4. At $x = 9.2$: $L(9.2) = 3 + \dfrac{0.2}{6} = 3 + 0.0\overline{3} \approx 3.0333$.

**Error Estimation:**

$f''(x) = -\dfrac{1}{4x^{3/2}}$. On $[9, 9.2]$: $|f''(t)| \leq \dfrac{1}{4 \cdot 9^{3/2}} = \dfrac{1}{108}$.

$$|\text{error}| \leq \frac{1/108}{2}(0.2)^2 = \frac{0.04}{216} \approx 0.000185.$$

**Verification:** $\sqrt{9.2} \approx 3.03315$. Actual error: $|3.0333 - 3.03315| \approx 0.00015 < 0.000185$. Estimate confirmed.

**Source.** [Active Calculus §1.8](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-1-8-tan-line-approx.html) — Boelkins · CC-BY-NC-SA.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Differential and Error Propagation in Sphere Volume (Intermediate)" numero="2">

**Problem:** The radius of a sphere is measured as $r = 6.0$ cm with a maximum error of $0.05$ cm. Estimate the maximum error in the volume $V = \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3$.

**Strategy:** We use the differential $dV = V'(r)\,dr$ to linearize the error. The differential is the linear approximation of the change in $V$.

**Solution:**

1. $V(r) = \dfrac{4}{3}\pi r^3$, $V'(r) = 4\pi r^2$.
2. With $r = 6$ and $dr = 0.05$:

$$dV = 4\pi(6)^2 (0.05) = 4\pi \cdot 36 \cdot 0.05 = 7.2\pi \approx 22.62 \text{ cm}^3.$$

3. Nominal volume: $V(6) = \dfrac{4}{3}\pi(216) = 288\pi \approx 904.8$ cm³.

4. Relative error: $\dfrac{dV}{V} = \dfrac{7.2\pi}{288\pi} = \dfrac{7.2}{288} = 0.025 = 2.5\%$.

**Verification:** An uncertainty of $0.8\%$ in the radius propagates to $2.5\%$ in the volume — a factor of 3, because $V \propto r^3$ (exponent 3). General rule: for $f(x) = x^n$, the relative error in $f$ is $n$ times the relative error in $x$.

**Source.** [OpenStax Calculus Vol.1 §4.2](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/4-2-linear-approximations-and-differentials) — CC-BY-NC-SA.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Trigonometric Function Approximation and Error Estimation (Intermediate)" numero="3">

**Problem:** Use linearization to approximate $\sin(32°)$ in terms of $\sin(30°)$ and $\cos(30°)$. Compare with the exact value.

**Strategy:** We convert to radians (required in differential calculus) and linearize $\sin$ at $a = \pi/6$.

**Solution:**

1. $f(x) = \sin x$, $a = \pi/6$ (corresponding to $30°$).
2. $f(a) = \sin(\pi/6) = 1/2$, $f'(a) = \cos(\pi/6) = \sqrt{3}/2$.
3. $x = 32° = 32\pi/180$ radians. $x - a = 2\pi/180 = \pi/90$.

$$L(x) = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} \cdot \frac{\pi}{90} = 0.5 + 0.866 \cdot 0.03491 \approx 0.5 + 0.03023 = 0.53023.$$

**Comparison:** $\sin(32°) = 0.52992$. Error: $|0.53023 - 0.52992| = 0.00031$.

**Verification:** $|f''(t)| = |\sin(t)| \leq \sin(32°) < 0.530$. Bound: $\dfrac{0.530}{2}\left(\dfrac{\pi}{90}\right)^2 \approx 0.265 \cdot 0.001218 \approx 0.000323 > 0.000310$. The bound is met.

**Source.** [Active Calculus §1.8](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-1-8-tan-line-approx.html) — Boelkins · CC-BY-NC-SA.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Newton-Raphson as Iterated Linearization (Advanced)" numero="4">

**Problem:** Use Newton-Raphson to find $\sqrt[3]{2}$ to three decimal places, starting with $x_0 = 1.5$.

**Strategy:** We reformulate as $f(x) = x^3 - 2 = 0$ and apply $x_{n+1} = x_n - f(x_n)/f'(x_n)$, which comes from setting the tangent line to $f$ at $x_n$ to zero.

**Solution:**

$f(x) = x^3 - 2$, $f'(x) = 3x^2$.

| Iteration | $x_n$ | $f(x_n)$ | $f'(x_n)$ | $x_{n+1}$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | $1.5$ | $1.375$ | $6.75$ | $1.2963$ |

<Exemplo titulo="Multivariable Linearization — Ohm's Law with Two Uncertainties (Challenge)" numero="5">

**Problem:** Electrical resistance is calculated by $R = V/I$. Measurements yield $V = 12.0 \pm 0.3$ V and $I = 2.00 \pm 0.05$ A. Estimate the uncertainty in $R$ using linear error propagation.

**Strategy:** We use the error propagation formula based on the total differential for functions of two variables: $\sigma_R^2 \approx (\partial R/\partial V)^2 \sigma_V^2 + (\partial R/\partial I)^2 \sigma_I^2$.

**Solution:**

1. $R(V, I) = V/I$. Partial derivatives:

$$\frac{\partial R}{\partial V} = \frac{1}{I} = \frac{1}{2.00} = 0.5 \text{ Ω/V}, \quad \frac{\partial R}{\partial I} = -\frac{V}{I^2} = -\frac{12.0}{4.00} = -3.0 \text{ Ω/A}.$$

2. Propagated variance:

$$\sigma_R^2 \approx (0.5)^2(0.3)^2 + (-3.0)^2(0.05)^2 = 0.0225 + 0.0225 = 0.045 \text{ Ω}^2.$$

$$\sigma_R \approx \sqrt{0.045} \approx 0.212 \text{ Ω}.$$

3. Result: $R = 6.00 \pm 0.21$ Ω.

**Verification:** The uncertainties in $V$ and $I$ contribute equally to the total uncertainty in $R$ — a characteristic of this specific combination of values.

**Source.** [APEX Calculus §4.4](https://www.apexcalculus.com/) — Hartman et al. · CC-BY-NC.
</Exemplo>

  solucao={<>Let <Eq>{`x^*`}</Eq> be the exact root (<Eq>{`f(x^*) = 0`}</Eq>), <Eq>{`e_n = x_n - x^*`}</Eq>. Taylor at <Eq>{`x^*`}</Eq>: <Eq>{`f(x_n) = f'(x^*) e_n + f''(\\xi_n) e_n^2/2`}</Eq>. Newton iteration: <Eq>{`e_{n+1} = x_{n+1} - x^* = x_n - f(x_n)/f'(x_n) - x^*`}</Eq>. Expanding and simplifying: <Eq>{`e_{n+1} \\approx -f''(x^*) e_n^2 / (2f'(x^*))`}</Eq>. Thus <Eq>{`|e_{n+1}| \\leq C|e_n|^2`}</Eq> with <Eq>{`C = |f''(x^*)| / (2|f'(x^*)|)`}</Eq>. Quadratic convergence.</>}
>
Prove that Newton-Raphson has quadratic convergence: if $e_n = x_n - x^*$ is the error at the $n$-th iteration and $f'(x^*) \neq 0$, then $|e_{n+1}| \leq C\,|e_n|^2$ for some constant $C > 0$.
