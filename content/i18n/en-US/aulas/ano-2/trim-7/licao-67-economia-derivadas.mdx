<Porta nivel="formal" titulo="Definitions, maximization, and elasticity">

### Marginal functions

<Definicao titulo="Cost, revenue, profit and marginals">
Given:
- $C(q)$: **total cost** of producing $q$ units.
- $R(q) = p(q) \cdot q$: **total revenue** (in perfect competition $p$ is fixed; in monopoly $p$ depends on $q$).
- $\pi(q) = R(q) - C(q)$: **profit**.

The **marginals** are the derivatives:
$$MC(q) = C'(q), \quad MR(q) = R'(q), \quad M\pi(q) = \pi'(q) = MR(q) - MC(q).$$
$MC(q)$ approximates the cost of producing the $(q+1)$-th unit.
</Definicao>

> "The marginal cost function is $C'(x)$, the derivative of the cost function. The marginal revenue function is $R'(x)$." — [OpenStax Calculus Vol. 1 §4.7](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/4-7-applied-optimization-problems)

### Profit maximization

$\pi'(q) = 0 \iff MR(q) = MC(q)$.

**Second-order condition:** $\pi''(q^*) < 0 \iff MR'(q^*) < MC'(q^*)$ — marginal cost rises faster than marginal revenue.

### Average cost and marginal cost

<Definicao titulo="Average cost">
$\bar{C}(q) = C(q)/q$. Differentiating:
$$\bar{C}'(q) = \frac{C'(q) \cdot q - C(q)}{q^2} = \frac{MC(q) - \bar{C}(q)}{q}.$$
</Definicao>

Thus: $\bar{C}'(q) = 0 \iff MC(q) = \bar{C}(q)$. **The marginal cost curve crosses the average cost curve exactly at its minimum.**

### Price elasticity of demand

<Definicao titulo="Price elasticity">
For demand function $q = D(p)$:
$$\varepsilon(p) = \frac{dq}{dp} \cdot \frac{p}{q} = D'(p) \cdot \frac{p}{q}.$$
- $|\varepsilon| > 1$: **elastic** demand (luxuries, goods with substitutes). Lowering price increases revenue.
- $|\varepsilon| < 1$: **inelastic** demand (necessities — salt, gasoline). Raising price increases revenue.
- $|\varepsilon| = 1$: **unit elasticity**. Revenue is maximized.
</Definicao>

### Monopoly markup

For a monopolist choosing $q$ (and indirectly $p$):

$$MR = p + q\,\frac{dp}{dq} = p\!\left(1 + \frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right).$$

Profit maximization ($MR = MC$) yields the **markup rule:**
$$p^* = \frac{MC}{1 + 1/\varepsilon} = \frac{MC \cdot \varepsilon}{\varepsilon + 1}.$$

**Lerner index:** $L = (p - MC)/p = -1/\varepsilon$ measures market power.

<Insight titulo="Demand elasticity on a line">
For linear demand $q = a - bp$, elasticity $|\varepsilon| = bp/q$ increases with $p$. At $p = 0$: $\varepsilon = 0$ (inelastic). At $q = 0$: $|\varepsilon| \to \infty$ (elastic). The unit elasticity point occurs exactly halfway along the demand curve — where revenue is maximized.
</Insight>

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

Imagine you sell **popsicles** at the beach. To make one more popsicle, you spend a certain amount on ice cream and a stick — that's the **marginal cost** (cost of making one more).

You sell each popsicle for $5. That's the **marginal revenue** (how much you make from one more).

As long as the cost of making one popsicle is less than $5, it makes sense to make more. When it costs exactly $5 to make one more, you're at the **perfect spot** — after that, you start losing money.

That point where "cost of one more = gain from one more" is where **profit is biggest** of all!

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

In economics, the **derivative** becomes **marginal**:

- $C(q)$ = total cost to produce $q$ units. **MC** = $C'$ = how much it costs to make **one more unit**.
- $R(q)$ = revenue (money coming in). **MR** = $R'$ = how much comes in with **one more unit**.
- **Profit is maximized**: produce more as long as $MR > MC$. Stop when $MR = MC$.

**Example:** $C(q) = 100 + 5q + 0.1q^2$ (cost), $p = 50$ fixed (competition). $R = 50q$, $MR = 50$. $MC = 5 + 0.2q$. $MR = MC \Rightarrow 50 = 5 + 0.2q \Rightarrow q^* = 225$.

**Elasticity**: if you raise price by 1% and quantity drops by 2%, elasticity is $-2$. Demand is **elastic** — sensitive to price.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old student">

Marginal analysis is the **central model in microeconomics**: every decision boils down to "is it worth making one more unit?"

### Basic structure

- **Cost** $C(q)$: fixed part $F$ + variable part $V(q)$.
- **Revenue** $R(q) = p(q) \cdot q$. In perfect competition $p$ is given; in monopoly $p$ falls with $q$.
- **Profit** $\pi(q) = R(q) - C(q)$.

### Average cost vs marginal cost

$\bar{C}(q) = C(q)/q$: cost per unit. $MC(q) = C'(q)$: cost of the next unit.

$\bar{C}$ reaches its minimum where $MC = \bar{C}$ — it's when producing one more unit stops reducing average cost.

### Elasticity

$\varepsilon = (\Delta q/q)/(\Delta p/p)$: percentage change in quantity per percentage change in price.

- $|\varepsilon| > 1$: **elastic** — consumer is sensitive. E.g.: airline tickets, designer clothing.
- $|\varepsilon| < 1$: **inelastic** — consumer is insensitive. E.g.: cigarettes, medicine, gasoline.

### Worked example

Demand $q = 100 - 2p$ (inverse: $p = (100-q)/2$). Revenue $R(q) = (100-q)q/2 = 50q - q^2/2$.

$MR(q) = 50 - q$. Cost $C(q) = 10q + q^2/4$, $MC = 10 + q/2$.

$MR = MC \Rightarrow 50 - q = 10 + q/2 \Rightarrow 3q/2 = 40 \Rightarrow q^* \approx 26.7$.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

Marginal analysis appears in classical economics, engineering economics, finance (marginal utility), and operations research (dynamic pricing).

### Typical engineering cost function

$C(q) = F + aq + bq^2 + cq^3$: fixed cost $F$ + linear (raw material) + quadratic (overtime hours) + cubic (capacity bottlenecks). The curve $\bar{C}(q)$ has a U shape: falls with scale, rises with congestion.

### Monopoly vs perfect competition

| | Perfect competition | Monopoly |
|---|---|---|
| $p$ | given (price-taker) | $p(q)$, chosen |
| $MR$ | $p$ (constant) | $p + q\,dp/dq < p$ |
| Decision | $MC = p$ | $MC = MR < p$ |
| Surplus | maximum social | underproduction — deadweight loss |

### EOQ — Economic Order Quantity

$T(q) = \frac{Dhq}{2} + \frac{SD}{q}$ where $D$ = annual demand, $h$ = holding cost/unit/year, $S$ = order cost. $T'(q) = Dh/2 - SD/q^2 = 0 \Rightarrow q^* = \sqrt{2SD/h}$. Canonical inventory model.

### Dynamic pricing and variable elasticity

Uber, airlines, and e-commerce estimate $\varepsilon$ in real time and adjust $p$ via markup $p = MC/(1 + 1/\varepsilon)$. The challenge is estimating $\varepsilon$ from observational data — instrumental variables is the standard method.

### Tax and incidence

Tax $t$ per unit: $MC \to MC + t$. New equilibrium: $q^*$ falls. The burden split between buyer and seller depends on elasticities: buyer's share $= \varepsilon_S/(\varepsilon_S - \varepsilon_D)$.

### Black-Scholes via marginal analysis

Portfolio $\Pi = -V + \Delta S$ where $\Delta = \partial V/\partial S$ is the **delta** — marginal derivative of option price. Risk-free replication + Itô yield the Black-Scholes PDE. The [1997 Nobel Prize in Economic Sciences](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/1997/) was awarded to Merton and Scholes for this derivation.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

Marginal analysis is the starting point; modern economics works in general equilibrium, stochastic dynamics, and game theory.

### General equilibrium

Walras (1874): all markets in simultaneous equilibrium — system of equations $MC_i = p_i$ for each good $i$. Existence proved via fixed-point theorem (Arrow-Debreu, 1954 — [1972 Nobel Prize](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/1972/)). Efficiency of competitive markets = first welfare theorem.

### Game theory and Nash equilibrium

When agents interact, Nash (1950 — [1994 Nobel Prize](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/1994/)) replaces the isolated optimizer. Each agent maximizes profit given rivals' behavior — FOC is partial derivative of own choice. Cournot, Bertrand, Stackelberg model timing and structure.

### Stochastic optimal control

Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman: $V_t + \sup_u[f(x,u)V_x + \sigma^2/2 \cdot V_{xx}] = 0$. Bellman equation generalizes the marginal FOC to sequential decision-making. Black-Scholes is a special case with $f$ linear and no control.

### Asymmetric information

Akerlof (market for lemons, [2001 Nobel](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/2001/)), Spence (signaling), Stiglitz (screening) showed that standard marginal analysis fails with asymmetric information. Inefficient equilibria emerge without intervention.

### Behavioral economics

Kahneman ([2002 Nobel](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/2002/kahneman/facts/)) documents systematic deviations from marginal rationality: loss aversion, anchoring, hyperbolic discounting. Marginal analysis remains the normative benchmark — but actual behavior deviates.

### Keynes-Ramsey rule

In dynamic models (Ramsey-Cass-Koopmans), agent maximizes $\int_0^{\infty} U(c(t)) e^{-\rho t} dt$ subject to $\dot k = f(k) - c - \delta k$. Euler-Lagrange equation yields $\dot c/c = (f'(k) - \delta - \rho)/\sigma$ — Keynes-Ramsey rule. Marginal analysis operates in continuous time via calculus of variations.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practical application">

**Recipe in 5 steps:**

1. Identify $C(q)$ and $p(q)$ (or $q(p)$).
2. Compute $R(q) = p(q) \cdot q$, $MC = C'$, $MR = R'$.
3. Solve $q^*$ such that $MR(q^*) = MC(q^*)$.
4. Verify maximum: $\pi''(q^*) < 0$.
5. Calculate $p^* = p(q^*)$, $\pi^* = R(q^*) - C(q^*)$.

### Quick example — juice company

Local demand for juice: $p(q) = 20 - 0.1q$ (US$ per bottle, $q$ in units/day). Cost: $C(q) = 200 + 5q + 0.05q^2$.

$R(q) = q(20 - 0.1q) = 20q - 0.1q^2$. $MR = 20 - 0.2q$. $MC = 5 + 0.1q$.

$MR = MC \Rightarrow 20 - 0.2q = 5 + 0.1q \Rightarrow 15 = 0.3q \Rightarrow q^* = 50$.

$p^* = 20 - 5 = 15$ dollars. $\pi^* = 15 \times 50 - (200 + 250 + 125) = 750 - 575 = 175$ dollars/day.

### Verification via average cost

$\bar{C}(50) = 575/50 = 11.50$ dollars. Since $p^* = 15 > \bar{C}$, the company is profitable. ✓

### Python for verification

```python
import sympy as sp
q = sp.symbols('q', positive=True)
p_func = 20 - 0.1*q
R = p_func * q
C = 200 + 5*q + 0.05*q**2
profit = R - C
q_star = sp.solve(sp.diff(profit, q), q)
print(q_star, profit.subs(q, q_star[0]))
```

## Worked examples

<Exemplo titulo="Marginal cost and minimum average cost (basic)" numero="1">

**Problem.** For $C(q) = q^2 + 9$, find the marginal cost $MC$ and the quantity minimizing average cost $\bar{C}$.

**Strategy.** Calculate $MC = C'$. Minimize $\bar{C} = C/q$ by differentiating and setting to zero — or use the property $MC = \bar{C}$ at the minimum.

**Solution.**

1. **$MC(q) = 2q$.**
2. **$\bar{C}(q) = q + 9/q$.**
3. **$\bar{C}'(q) = 1 - 9/q^2 = 0 \Rightarrow q^2 = 9 \Rightarrow q = 3$.**
4. **Verification:** $\bar{C}(3) = 3 + 3 = 6$ and $MC(3) = 6$. ✓ — minimum average cost coincides with marginal cost.

**Check.** $\bar{C}''(q) = 18/q^3 > 0$ at $q = 3$: minimum confirmed. ✓

**Source.** [Yoshiwara — Modeling, Functions, and Graphs, ch. 5, Exercise 5.3.9](https://yoshiwarabooks.org/mfg/MFG.html) — open license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Maximum profit with linear demand (intermediate)" numero="2">

**Problem.** Demand $q = 100 - 2p$ (inverse: $p = 50 - q/2$). Cost $C(q) = 200 + 4q + q^2$. Find $q^*$, $p^*$ and $\pi^*$.

**Strategy.** Calculate $R(q)$, $MR$ and $MC$. Set $MR = MC$ to find $q^*$. Confirm with $\pi'' < 0$.

**Solution.**

1. **$R(q) = pq = (50 - q/2)q = 50q - q^2/2$. $MR = 50 - q$.**
2. **$MC = 4 + 2q$.**
3. **$MR = MC \Rightarrow 50 - q = 4 + 2q \Rightarrow 3q = 46 \Rightarrow q^* \approx 15.3$. (Approximate; if $q$ integer: $q^* = 15$.)**
4. **$p^* = 50 - 15/2 = 42.50$ US$.**
5. **$\pi^* = R(15) - C(15) = 637.50 - (200 + 60 + 225) = 637.50 - 485 = 152.50$ US$.**

**Check.** $\pi''(q) = -3 < 0$ for any $q$ — maximum. ✓

**Source.** [Yoshiwara — Modeling, Functions, and Graphs, ch. 5, Exercise 5.3.15](https://yoshiwarabooks.org/mfg/MFG.html) — open license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Elasticity and maximum revenue (intermediate)" numero="3">

**Problem.** Demand $q = 100 - 2p$. (a) Calculate elasticity at $p = 20$. (b) At what price is revenue maximized?

**Strategy.** Apply $\varepsilon = D'(p) \cdot p/q$. Maximize $R = pq = p(100 - 2p)$ by differentiating.

**Solution.**

**(a) Elasticity at $p = 20$:**
$D'(p) = -2$. $q(20) = 100 - 40 = 60$.
$\varepsilon = -2 \cdot 20/60 = -40/60 = -2/3$.

Since $|\varepsilon| < 1$: demand is **inelastic** at $p = 20$ — raising price increases revenue.

**(b) Maximum revenue:**
$R(p) = p(100 - 2p) = 100p - 2p^2$. $R'(p) = 100 - 4p = 0 \Rightarrow p^* = 25$.

$R(25) = 25 \cdot 50 = 1250$ US$. Check: $\varepsilon(25) = -2 \cdot 25/50 = -1$ (unit elasticity). ✓

**Check.** $R'' = -4 < 0$ — maximum confirmed. ✓

**Source.** [Active Calculus §3.3, Activity 3.3.4](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-3-5-optimization.html) — CC-BY-NC-SA.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Economic order quantity EOQ (real modeling)" numero="4">

**Problem.** A factory has annual demand $D = 10000$ units, order cost $S = 200$ US$ per order, and holding cost $h = 4$ US$ per unit per year. Find the economic order quantity $q^*$.

**Strategy.** Minimize $T(q) = Dhq/2 + SD/q$ (total annual cost = holding + ordering). Differentiate and set to zero.

**Solution.**

1. **$T(q) = \frac{10000 \cdot 4}{2} q + \frac{200 \cdot 10000}{q} = 20000q + \frac{2000000}{q}$.**

    Note: values were simplified: $T(q) = \frac{4q}{2} \cdot 10000/10000 + \ldots$ — using standard formula directly:

    $T(q) = \frac{Dhq}{2} + \frac{SD}{q} = \frac{4 \cdot q}{2} + \frac{200 \cdot 10000}{q} = 2q + \frac{2000000}{q}$.

2. **$T'(q) = 2 - 2000000/q^2 = 0 \Rightarrow q^2 = 1000000 \Rightarrow q^* = 1000$ units.**

3. **$T(1000) = 2000 + 2000 = 4000$ US$/year.**

**Check.** $T''(q) = 4000000/q^3 > 0$ — minimum. ✓ Direct formula $q^* = \sqrt{2SD/h} = \sqrt{2 \cdot 200 \cdot 10000/4} = \sqrt{1000000} = 1000$. ✓

**Source.** [OpenStax Calculus Vol. 1 §4.7, Example 4.38](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/4-7-applied-optimization-problems) — CC-BY-NC-SA.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Monopoly markup and welfare loss (advanced)" numero="5">

**Problem.** Monopolist has demand $p(q) = 100 - 2q$ and cost $C(q) = q^2$. (a) Find the monopoly equilibrium. (b) Compare with perfect competition ($MC = p$). (c) Calculate deadweight loss.

**Strategy.** Monopoly: $MR = MC$. Competition: $p = MC$. Loss = area of triangle between the two quantities.

**Solution.**

**(a) Monopoly:** $R = 100q - 2q^2$, $MR = 100 - 4q$. $MC = 2q$. $MR = MC \Rightarrow 100 - 4q = 2q \Rightarrow q_M = 100/6 \approx 16.7$. $p_M = 100 - 2(100/6) = 100 - 100/3 \approx 66.7$ US$. Profit: $\pi = (p_M - \bar{C})q_M = \ldots \approx 833$ US$.

**(b) Competition:** $p = MC \Rightarrow 100 - 2q = 2q \Rightarrow q_C = 25$, $p_C = 50$ US$.

**(c) Welfare loss:** monopoly produces $16.7$ instead of $25$. Loss is the area of triangle with base $q_C - q_M \approx 8.3$ and height $p_M - p_C \approx 16.7$:

Deadweight loss $\approx \frac{1}{2} \cdot 8.3 \cdot 16.7 \approx 69$ US$.

**Check.** $p_M > MC(q_M) = 2 \cdot 16.7 = 33.4$ — monopoly charges above marginal cost, confirming inefficiency. ✓

**Source.** [Yoshiwara — Modeling, Functions, and Graphs, ch. 5, Exercise 5.3.20](https://yoshiwarabooks.org/mfg/MFG.html) — open license.
</Exemplo>
