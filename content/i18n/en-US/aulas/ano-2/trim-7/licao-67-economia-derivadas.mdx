<Porta nivel="formal"titulo="Definitions, Maximization, and Elasticity">

### Marginal Functions

<Definicao titulo="Cost, Revenue, Profit, and Marginals">
Given:
- $C(q)$: <strong>total cost</strong> of producing $q$ units.
- $R(q) = p(q) \cdot q$: <strong>total revenue</strong> (in perfect competition $p$ is fixed; in monopoly $p$ depends on $q$).
- $\pi(q) = R(q) - C(q)$: <strong>profit</strong>.

The <strong>marginals</strong> are the derivatives:
$$MC(q) = C'(q), \quad MR(q) = R'(q), \quad M\pi(q) = \pi'(q) = MR(q) - MC(q).$$
$MC(q)$ approximates the cost of producing the $(q+1)$-th unit.
</Definicao>

> "The marginal cost function is $C'(x)$, the derivative of the cost function. The marginal revenue function is $R'(x)$." — [OpenStax Calculus Vol. 1 §4.7](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/4-7-applied-optimization-problems)

### Profit Maximization

$\pi'(q) = 0 \iff MR(q) = MC(q)$.

<strong>Second-order condition:</strong> $\pi''(q^*) < 0 \iff MR'(q^*) < MC'(q^*)$ — marginal cost grows faster than marginal revenue.

### Average Cost and Marginal Cost

<Definicao titulo="Average Cost">
$\bar{C}(q) = C(q)/q$. Differentiating:
$$\bar{C}'(q) = \frac{C'(q) \cdot q - C(q)}{q^2} = \frac{MC(q) - \bar{C}(q)}{q}.$$
</Definicao>

Thus: $\bar{C}'(q) = 0 \iff MC(q) = \bar{C}(q)$. <strong>The marginal cost curve intersects the average cost curve exactly at its minimum.</strong>

### Price Elasticity of Demand

<Definicao titulo="Price Elasticity">
For a demand function $q = D(p)$:
$$\varepsilon(p) = \frac{dq}{dp} \cdot \frac{p}{q} = D'(p) \cdot \frac{p}{q}.$$
- $|\varepsilon| > 1$: <strong>elastic</strong> demand (luxuries, goods with substitutes). Lowering price increases revenue.
- $|\varepsilon| < 1$: <strong>inelastic</strong> demand (necessities — salt, gasoline). Raising price increases revenue.
- $|\varepsilon| = 1$: <strong>unit elasticity</strong>. Maximum revenue.
</Definicao>

### Monopoly Markup

For a monopolist choosing $q$ (and indirectly $p$):

$$MR = p + q\,\frac{dp}{dq} = p\!\left(1 + \frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right).$$

Maximum profit ($MR = MC$) yields the <strong>markup rule:</strong>
$$p^* = \frac{MC}{1 + 1/\varepsilon} = \frac{MC \cdot \varepsilon}{\varepsilon + 1}.$$

<strong>Lerner Index:</strong> $L = (p - MC)/p = -1/\varepsilon$ measures market power.

<Insight titulo="Demand Elasticity on a Line">
For a linear demand $q = a - bp$, the elasticity $|\varepsilon| = bp/q$ increases with $p$. At $p = 0$: $\varepsilon = 0$ (inelastic). At $q = 0$: $|\varepsilon| \to \infty$ (elastic). The point of unit elasticity occurs exactly halfway down the demand curve — where revenue is maximized.
</Insight>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

Imagine you sell <strong>ice pops</strong> on the beach. To make one more ice pop, you use a certain amount of ice cream and a stick — that's the <strong>marginal cost</strong> (cost to make one more).

You sell each ice pop for $5. That's the <strong>marginal revenue</strong> (how much you earn from one more).

As long as the cost to make an ice pop is less than $5, it makes sense to make more. When it costs exactly $5 to make one more, you're at the <strong>perfect spot</strong> — after that, you start losing money.

This spot where "cost of one more = gain of one more" is where the <strong>profit is biggest</strong>!

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10"titulo="For a 10-year-old">

In economics, the <strong>derivative</strong> becomes <strong>marginal</strong>:

- $C(q)$ = total cost of making $q$ items. <strong>MC</strong> = $C'$ = how much it costs to make <strong>one more item</strong>.
- $R(q)$ = revenue (money coming in). <strong>MR</strong> = $R'$ = how much comes in from <strong>one more item</strong>.
- <strong>Maximum profit</strong>: make more as long as $MR > MC$. Stop when $MR = MC$.

<strong>Example:</strong> $C(q) = 100 + 5q + 0.1q^2$ (cost), $p = 50$ fixed (competition). $R = 50q$, $MR = 50$. $MC = 5 + 0.2q$. $MR = MC \Rightarrow 50 = 5 + 0.2q \Rightarrow q^* = 225$.

<strong>Elasticity:</strong> If you raise the price by 1% and the quantity drops by 2%, the elasticity is -2. Demand is <strong>elastic</strong> — sensitive to price.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15"titulo="For a 15-year-old">

Marginal analysis is the <strong>central model of microeconomics</strong>: every decision boils down to "is it worth making one more unit?"

### Basic Structure

- <strong>Cost</strong> $C(q)$: fixed part $F$ + variable part $V(q)$.
- <strong>Revenue</strong> $R(q) = p(q) \cdot q$. In perfect competition $p$ is given; in monopoly $p$ falls with $q$.
- <strong>Profit</strong> $\pi(q) = R(q) - C(q)$.

### Average Cost vs. Marginal Cost

$\bar{C}(q) = C(q)/q$: cost per unit. $MC(q) = C'(q)$: cost of the next unit.

$\bar{C}$ has its minimum where $MC = \bar{C}$ — this is when making one more unit no longer reduces the average cost.

### Elasticity

$\varepsilon = (\Delta q/q)/(\Delta p/p)$: percentage change in quantity per percentage change in price.

- $|\varepsilon| > 1$: <strong>elastic</strong> — consumer is sensitive. Ex: airline tickets, designer clothes.
- $|\varepsilon| < 1$: <strong>inelastic</strong> — consumer is insensitive. Ex: cigarettes, medicine, gasoline.

### Worked Example

Demand $q = 100 - 2p$ (inverse: $p = (100-q)/2$). Revenue $R(q) = (100-q)q/2 = 50q - q^2/2$.

$MR(q) = 50 - q$. Cost $C(q) = 10q + q^2/4$, $MC = 10 + q/2$.

$MR = MC \Rightarrow 50 - q = 10 + q/2 \Rightarrow 3q/2 = 40 \Rightarrow q^* \approx 26.7$.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25"titulo="For an engineering student">

Marginal analysis appears in classical economics, engineering economics, finance (marginal utility), and operations research (dynamic pricing).

### Typical Engineering Cost Function

$C(q) = F + aq + bq^2 + cq^3$: fixed cost $F$ + linear (raw materials) + quadratic (overtime) + cubic (capacity bottlenecks). The $\bar{C}(q)$ curve has a U-shape: falls with scale, rises with congestion.

### Monopoly vs. Perfect Competition

| | Perfect Competition | Monopoly |
|---|---|---|
| $p$ | Given (price taker) | $p(q)$, chosen |
| $MR$ | $p$ (constant) | $p + q\,dp/dq < p$ |
| Decision | $MC = p$ | $MC = MR < p$ |
| Surplus | Maximum social | Underproduction — deadweight loss |

### EOQ — Economic Order Quantity

$T(q) = \frac{Dhq}{2} + \frac{SD}{q}$ where $D$ = annual demand, $h$ = cost of holding per unit per year, $S$ = cost per order. $T'(q) = Dh/2 - SD/q^2 = 0 \Rightarrow q^* = \sqrt{2SD/h}$. Canonical inventory model.

### Dynamic Pricing and Variable Elasticity

Uber, airlines, and e-commerce estimate $\varepsilon$ in real-time and adjust $p$ via markup $p = MC/(1 + 1/\varepsilon)$. The challenge is estimating $\varepsilon$ from observational data — instrumental variables is the standard method.

### Tax and Incidence

Tax $t$ per unit: $MC \to MC + t$. New equilibrium: $q^*$ falls. The burden split between buyer and seller depends on elasticities: buyer's share $= \varepsilon_S/(\varepsilon_S - \varepsilon_D)$.

### Black-Scholes via Marginal Analysis

Portfolio $\Pi = -V + \Delta S$ where $\Delta = \partial V/\partial S$ is the <strong>delta</strong> — the marginal derivative of the option price. Risk-free replication + Itô calculus yield the Black-Scholes PDE. The [1997 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/1997/) was awarded to Merton and Scholes for this derivation.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40"titulo="For a senior professional">

Marginal analysis is the starting point; modern economics works with general equilibrium, stochastic dynamics, and game theory.

### General Equilibrium

Walras (1874): all markets in simultaneous equilibrium — a system of equations $MC_i = p_i$ for each good $i$. Existence proven via fixed-point theorems (Arrow-Debreu, 1954 — [1972 Nobel Prize](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/1972/)). Efficiency of competitive markets = first welfare theorem.

### Game Theory and Nash Equilibrium

When agents interact, Nash (1950 — [1994 Nobel Prize](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/1994/)) replaces the isolated optimizer. Each agent maximizes profit given rivals' behavior — FOC is a partial derivative with respect to one's own choice. Cournot, Bertrand, Stackelberg model timing and structure.

### Stochastic Optimal Control

Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman: $V_t + \sup_u[f(x,u)V_x + \sigma^2/2 \cdot V_{xx}] = 0$. The Bellman equation generalizes the marginal FOC for sequential decision-making. Black-Scholes is a special case with linear $f$ and no control.

### Asymmetric Information

Akerlof (market for lemons, [Nobel 2001](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/2001/)), Spence (signaling), Stiglitz (screening) showed that standard marginal analysis fails with asymmetric information. Inefficient equilibria emerge without intervention.

### Behavioral Economics

Kahneman ([2002 Nobel Prize](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/2002/kahneman/facts/)) documents systematic deviations from marginal rationality: loss aversion, anchoring, hyperbolic discounting. Marginal analysis remains the normative benchmark — but actual behavior deviates.

### Ramsey-Keynes Rule

In dynamic models (Ramsey-Cass-Koopmans), an agent maximizes $\int_0^\infty U(c(t)) e^{-\rho t} dt$ subject to $\dot k = f(k) - c - \delta k$. The Euler-Lagrange equation yields $\dot c/c = (f'(k) - \delta - \rho)/\sigma$ — the Ramsey-Keynes rule. Marginal analysis operates in continuous time via calculus of variations.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica"titulo="Practical Application">

<strong>Profit in 5 Steps:</strong>

1. Identify $C(q)$ and $p(q)$ (or $q(p)$).
2. Calculate $R(q) = p(q) \cdot q$, $MC = C'$, $MR = R'$.
3. Find $q^*$ such that $MR(q^*) = MC(q^*)$.
4. Confirm maximum: $\pi''(q^*) < 0$.
5. Calculate $p^* = p(q^*)$, $\pi^* = R(q^*) - C(q^*)$.

### Quick Example — Juice Company

Local juice demand: $p(q) = 20 - 0.1q$ (R$/bottle, $q$ in units/day). Cost: $C(q) = 200 + 5q + 0.05q^2$.

$R(q) = q(20 - 0.1q) = 20q - 0.1q^2$. $MR = 20 - 0.2q$. $MC = 5 + 0.1q$.

$MR = MC \Rightarrow 20 - 0.2q = 5 + 0.1q \Rightarrow 15 = 0.3q \Rightarrow q^* = 50$.

$p^* = 20 - 5 = 15$ reais. $\pi^* = 15 \times 50 - (200 + 250 + 125) = 750 - 575 = 175$ reais/day.

### Verification via Average Cost

$\bar{C}(50) = 575/50 = 11.50$ reais. Since $p^* = 15 > \bar{C}$, the company is profitable. ✓

### Python for Verification

```python
import sympy as sp
q = sp.symbols('q', positive=True)
p_func = 20 - 0.1*q
R = p_func * q
C = 200 + 5*q + 0.05*q**2
profit = R - C
q_star = sp.solve(sp.diff(profit, q), q)
print(q_star, profit.subs(q, q_star[0]))
```

## Solved Examples

<Exemplo titulo="Marginal Cost and Minimum Average Cost (Basic)" numero="1">

<strong>Problem.</strong> For $C(q) = q^2 + 9$, find the marginal cost $MC$ and the quantity that minimizes average cost $\bar{C}$.

<strong>Strategy.</strong> Calculate $MC = C'$. Minimize $\bar{C} = C/q$ by differentiating and setting to zero — or use the property $MC = \bar{C}$ at the minimum.

<strong>Solution.</strong>

1. <strong>$MC(q) = 2q$.</strong>
2. <strong>$\bar{C}(q) = q + 9/q$.</strong>
3. <strong>$\bar{C}'(q) = 1 - 9/q^2 = 0 \Rightarrow q^2 = 9 \Rightarrow q = 3$.</strong>
4. <strong>Verification:</strong> $\bar{C}(3) = 3 + 3 = 6$ and $MC(3) = 6$. ✓ — minimum average cost coincides with marginal cost.

<strong>Check.</strong> $\bar{C}''(q) = 18/q^3 > 0$ at $q = 3$: minimum confirmed. ✓

<strong>Source.</strong> [Yoshiwara — Modeling, Functions, and Graphs, Ch. 5, Exercise 5.3.9](https://yoshiwarabooks.org/mfg/Chap4.html) — free license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Maximum Profit with Linear Demand (Intermediate)" numero="2">

<strong>Problem.</strong> Demand $q = 100 - 2p$ (inverse: $p = 50 - q/2$). Cost $C(q) = 200 + 4q + q^2$. Find $q^*$, $p^*$, and $\pi^*$.

<strong>Strategy.</strong> Calculate $R(q)$, $MR$, and $MC$. Set $MR = MC$ to find $q^*$. Confirm with $\pi'' < 0$.

<strong>Solution.</strong>

1. <strong>$R(q) = pq = (50 - q/2)q = 50q - q^2/2$. $MR = 50 - q$.</strong>
2. <strong>$MC = 4 + 2q$.</strong>
3. <strong>$MR = MC \Rightarrow 50 - q = 4 + 2q \Rightarrow 3q = 46 \Rightarrow q^* \approx 15.3$. (Approximate; if $q$ is integer: $q^* = 15$.)**
4. <strong>$p^* = 50 - 15/2 = 42.50$ R$.</strong>
5. <strong>$\pi^* = R(15) - C(15) = 637.50 - (200 + 60 + 225) = 637.50 - 485 = 152.50$ R$.</strong>

<strong>Check.</strong> $\pi''(q) = -3 < 0$ for any $q$ — maximum. ✓

<strong>Source.</strong> [Yoshiwara — Modeling, Functions, and Graphs, Ch. 5, Exercise 5.3.15](https://yoshiwarabooks.org/mfg/Chap4.html) — free license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Elasticity and Maximum Revenue (Intermediate)" numero="3">

<strong>Problem.</strong> Demand $q = 100 - 2p$. (a) Calculate elasticity at $p = 20$. (b) At what price is revenue maximized?

<strong>Strategy.</strong> Apply $\varepsilon = D'(p) \cdot p/q$. Maximize $R = pq = p(100 - 2p)$ by differentiating.

<strong>Solution.</strong>

(a) <strong>Elasticity at $p = 20$:</strong>
$D'(p) = -2$. $q(20) = 100 - 40 = 60$.
$\varepsilon = -2 \cdot 20/60 = -40/60 = -2/3$.

Since $|\varepsilon| < 1$: demand is <strong>inelastic</strong> at $p = 20$ — raising the price increases revenue.

(b) <strong>Maximum Revenue:</strong>
$R(p) = p(100 - 2p) = 100p - 2p^2$. $R'(p) = 100 - 4p = 0 \Rightarrow p^* = 25$.

$R(25) = 25 \cdot 50 = 1250$ R$. Check: $\varepsilon(25) = -2 \cdot 25/50 = -1$ (unit elasticity). \checkmark

<strong>Check.</strong> $R'' = -4 < 0$ — maximum confirmed. \checkmark

<strong>Source.</strong> [Active Calculus §3.3, Activity 3.3.4](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-3-3-optimization.html) — CC-BY-NC-SA.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Economic Order Quantity EOQ (Real-World Modeling)" numero="4">

<strong>Problem.</strong> A factory has an annual demand $D = 10000$ units, an ordering cost $S = R\$200$ per order, and a holding cost $h = R\$4$ per unit per year. Find the economic order quantity $q^*$.

<strong>Strategy.</strong> Minimize $T(q) = Dhq/2 + SD/q$ (total annual cost = holding + ordering). Differentiate and set to zero.

<strong>Solution.</strong>

1. <strong>$T(q) = \frac{10000 \cdot 4}{2} q + \frac{200 \cdot 10000}{q} = 20000q + \frac{2000000}{q}$.</strong>

    Note: the values were simplified: $T(q) = \frac{4q}{2} \cdot 10000/10000 + \ldots$ — using the standard formula directly:

    $T(q) = \frac{Dhq}{2} + \frac{SD}{q} = \frac{4 \cdot q}{2} + \frac{200 \cdot 10000}{q} = 2q + \frac{2000000}{q}$.

2. <strong>$T'(q) = 2 - 2000000/q^2 = 0 \Rightarrow q^2 = 1000000 \Rightarrow q^* = 1000$ units.</strong>

3. <strong>$T(1000) = 2000 + 2000 = 4000$ R$/year.</strong>

<strong>Check.</strong> $T''(q) = 4000000/q^3 > 0$ — minimum. ✓ The direct formula $q^* = \sqrt{2SD/h} = \sqrt{2 \cdot 200 \cdot 10000/4} = \sqrt{1000000} = 1000$. ✓

<strong>Source.</strong> [OpenStax Calculus Vol. 1 §4.7, Example 4.38](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/4-7-applied-optimization-problems) — CC-BY-NC-SA.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Monopoly Markup and Welfare Loss (Advanced)" numero="5">

<strong>Problem.</strong> A monopolist has demand $p(q) = 100 - 2q$ and cost $C(q) = q^2$. (a) Find the monopoly equilibrium. (b) Compare with perfect competition ($MC = p$). (c) Calculate the deadweight loss.

<strong>Strategy.</strong> Monopoly: $MR = MC$. Competition: $p = MC$. Loss = area of the triangle between the two quantities.

<strong>Solution.</strong>

(a) <strong>Monopoly:</strong> $R = 100q - 2q^2$, $MR = 100 - 4q$. $MC = 2q$. $MR = MC \Rightarrow 100 - 4q = 2q \Rightarrow q_M = 100/6 \approx 16.7$. $p_M = 100 - 2(100/6) = 100 - 100/3 \approx 66.7$ R$. Profit: $\pi = (p_M - \bar{C})q_M = \ldots \approx 833$ R$.

(b) <strong>Competition:</strong> $p = MC \Rightarrow 100 - 2q = 2q \Rightarrow q_C = 25$, $p_C = 50$ R$.

(c) <strong>Deadweight Loss:</strong> the monopoly produces $16.7$ instead of $25$. The loss is the area of the triangle with base $q_C - q_M \approx 8.3$ and height $p_M - p_C \approx 16.7$:

Deadweight loss $\approx \frac{1}{2} \cdot 8.3 \cdot 16.7 \approx 69$ R$.

<strong>Check.</strong> $p_M > MC(q_M) = 2 \cdot 16.7 = 33.4$ — monopoly charges above marginal cost, confirming inefficiency. ✓

<strong>Source.</strong> [Yoshiwara — Modeling, Functions, and Graphs, Ch. 5, Exercise 5.3.20](https://yoshiwarabooks.org/mfg/Chap4.html) — free license.
</Exemplo>
