### The Mother Concept: Taylor Approximation

<Definicao titulo="Taylor Polynomial of Order n">

Let $f$ be $n$ times differentiable at $a$. The **Taylor polynomial of order $n$** for $f$ centered at $a$ is:

$$P_n(x) = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}(x-a)^k$$

Special cases:
- **Order 0:** $P_0(x) = f(a)$ (constant approximation).
- **Order 1:** $P_1(x) = f(a) + f'(a)(x-a)$ (linearization — the tangent line).
- **Order 2:** $P_2(x) = f(a) + f'(a)(x-a) + \dfrac{f''(a)}{2}(x-a)^2$ (parabolization).

When $a = 0$, it is called a **Maclaurin polynomial**.

</Definicao>

> "The Taylor polynomial of degree $n$ centered at $x=a$ is the unique polynomial of degree $n$ that agrees with $f$ in value and in all its first $n$ derivatives at $x=a$." — [Active Calculus §8.4](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-8-4-taylor.html)

### Optimization: Critical Points and the Second Derivative Test

<Definicao titulo="Derivative Criterion for Extrema">

Let $f$ be continuous on $[a,b]$ and differentiable on $(a,b)$.

- **Critical points:** $c \in (a,b)$ such that $f'(c) = 0$ or $f'(c)$ does not exist.
- **Second derivative test:** If $f'(c) = 0$ and $f''(c)$ exists:
  - $f''(c) > 0 \Rightarrow f$ has a **local minimum** at $c$.
  - $f''(c) < 0 \Rightarrow f$ has a **local maximum** at $c$.
  - $f''(c) = 0 \Rightarrow$ test is inconclusive.
- **Absolute extremum:** Compare $f$ at interior critical points and at the endpoints $f(a)$, $f(b)$.

</Definicao>

> "Finding the maximum and minimum values of a function also has practical significance because we can use this method to solve optimization problems, such as increasing profit, minimizing cost, and maximizing area." — [OpenStax Calculus Vol. 1 §4.3](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/4-3-maxima-and-minima)

<Teorema titulo="Fermat's Theorem (Necessary Condition)">
If $f$ has a local extremum at $c$ and $f'(c)$ exists, then $f'(c) = 0$.

**Proof (sketch).** Assume a local maximum at $c$. For small $h > 0$: $f(c+h) - f(c) \leq 0$, so $[f(c+h)-f(c)]/h \leq 0$; taking the limit $h \to 0^+$, we get $f'(c) \leq 0$. For $h < 0$: $[f(c+h)-f(c)]/h \geq 0$; taking the limit $h \to 0^-$, we get $f'(c) \geq 0$. Therefore $f'(c) = 0$. $\square$
</Teorema>

### Concavity and Inflection Points

<Definicao titulo="Concavity and Inflection">

- $f$ is **convex** (concave up) on $I$ if $f'' > 0$ on $I$. Geometrically: the tangent line lies below the graph; $f(x) \geq P_1(x)$.
- $f$ is **concave** (concave down) on $I$ if $f'' < 0$ on $I$.
- **Inflection point:** $c$ where $f''$ changes sign.

</Definicao>

<Insight>

**Convexity and Taylor of Order 2.** If $f'' > 0$ at $a$, then $P_2(x) - f(a) = f'(a)(x-a) + \frac{f''(a)}{2}(x-a)^2$. For $x$ near $a$, the quadratic term dominates when $f'(a) = 0$, confirming that critical points with $f'' > 0$ are local minima. The second derivative test is thus a direct corollary of Taylor of order 2.

</Insight>

### L'Hôpital's Rule: Ratio of Linearizations

<Definicao titulo="L'Hôpital's Rule">

If $\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = 0$ and $\lim_{x \to a} g(x) = 0$ (or both $\pm\infty$), and if $\lim_{x \to a} f'(x)/g'(x)$ exists, then:

$$\lim_{x \to a} \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \lim_{x \to a} \frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)}$$

</Definicao>

> "L'Hôpital's Rule applies whenever both $f(x) \to 0$ and $g(x) \to 0$ as $x \to a$, or whenever $f(x) \to \pm\infty$ and $g(x) \to \pm\infty$." — [OpenStax Calculus Vol. 1 §4.8](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/4-8-l-hopital-s-rule)

**Idea via Taylor of Order 1.** For $a = 0$: $f(x) \approx f'(0) x$ and $g(x) \approx g'(0) x$, so $f(x)/g(x) \approx f'(0)/g'(0)$. L'Hôpital formalizes exactly this idea of a ratio of linearizations.

### Newton-Raphson: Tangent Iteration

<Definicao titulo="Newton-Raphson Method">

To find a root of $f(x) = 0$, given an initial estimate $x_0$:

$$x_{n+1} = x_n - \frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)}$$

Each $x_{n+1}$ is the x-intercept of the tangent line to $f$ at the point $(x_n, f(x_n))$.

</Definicao>

### Unified Function Analysis Pipeline

<Definicao titulo="Complete Sketching Procedure">

Given $f$, follow in order:

1. **Domain:** where $f$ is defined.
2. **Intercepts and symmetries:** $f(0)$, zeros of $f$, parity.
3. **Asymptotic behavior:** $\lim_{x\to\pm\infty} f(x)$; vertical asymptotes at singularities.
4. **$f'$:** critical points, monotonicity via sign table.
5. **$f''$:** concavity, inflection points.
6. **Absolute extrema** (on a closed domain): compare $f$ at critical points + endpoints.
7. **Sketch** using collected information.

</Definicao>

This whole term was about **exploring functions** with special tools.

- One tool shows **where the mountain has its peak** (optimization).
- Another shows **if the curve is like a bowl or a hat** (concavity).
- A third **guesses the answer when the calculator says zero divided by zero** (L'Hôpital's Rule).
- Another one **takes a picture of a function using a polynomial** — the more terms, the better the picture (Taylor series).
- The last one **finds answers nobody knows by guessing and correcting** (Newton's method).

All these tools do the same thing deep down: **look at the tangent line** at the point that matters. It's like using a ruler leaned against the curve to understand the whole curve.

<Porta nivel="10"titulo="For a 10-Year-Old">

This term was about **using derivatives to do useful things**:

- **Optimization:** If you want to build the biggest box possible from a sheet of paper, the derivative finds the right measurement.
- **Sketching:** Want to draw a function without plotting 1000 points? Look at $f'$ and $f''$.
- **L'Hôpital's Rule:** Sometimes a limit gives $0/0$ and seems impossible. The rule uses derivatives to solve it.
- **Taylor Series:** Any function can be *imitated* by a polynomial near a point. The more terms, the better the imitation. This is how calculators compute $\sin(0.3)$.
- **Newton-Raphson:** Want to know where $f(x) = 0$ when you can't solve it by hand? Start with a guess and keep correcting it with the tangent line. In 5 steps, you get 15 decimal places.

The main point: **the tangent line is calculus's most powerful tool**.

### Hierarchy of Approximation and When to Use Each Order

| Order | Polynomial $P_n$ | Cost | When to Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | $f(a)$ | trivial | pre-check of scale |
| 1 | $f(a)+f'(a)(x-a)$ | 1 derivative | control linearization, Newton's Method, L'Hôpital's Rule |
| 2 | $+f''(a)/2 \cdot (x-a)^2$ | 2 derivatives | curvature, extremum test, error analysis |
| $n$ | $+\ldots+f^{(n)}/n! \cdot (x-a)^n$ | $n$ derivatives | numerical precision, delicate limits |

### Newton-Raphson: Quadratic Convergence

If $f$ is $C^2$ near the root $r$ with $f'(r) \neq 0$, then the error $e_n = x_n - r$ satisfies:

$$e_{n+1} \approx -\frac{f''(r)}{2f'(r)} e_n^2$$

**Quadratic convergence**: the number of correct decimal places doubles with each iteration. In practice, 6–10 iterations yield 15 digits of precision (standard `float64` precision).

**Convergence Failures:** Newton's Method diverges if
- $f'(x_n) \approx 0$ (tangent line nearly horizontal near $x_n$),
- the root has multiplicity greater than 1 (linear, not quadratic, convergence),
- the initial guess is far from the root and the function has local minima/maxima between $x_0$ and $r$.

### Marginal Analysis and Economic Optimization

For a competitive firm with revenue $R(q) = p \cdot q$ and cost $C(q)$:

- **Profit:** $\pi(q) = p \cdot q - C(q)$.
- **First-order condition:** $\pi'(q^*) = p - C'(q^*) = 0 \Rightarrow p = MC$.
- **Second-order condition:** $\pi''(q^*) = -C''(q^*) < 0 \Rightarrow C'' > 0$ (increasing marginal cost).
- **Monopolist:** $R(q) = p(q) \cdot q$; condition $MR = MC$ instead of $p = MC$.

### Historical Perspective: 350 Years of Linearization

Taylor's formula (Brook Taylor, 1715) unified scattered results from Newton, Gregory, and Leibniz. What Taylor realized is that **every analytic function is locally a polynomial** — and polynomials are what humans (and computers) know how to calculate perfectly.

Guillaume de l'Hôpital (1696) paid Johann Bernoulli to publish his findings as his own — including the rule that now bears his name. Bernoulli was the true author. The history of calculus is rife with priority disputes (Newton vs. Leibniz being the biggest), but the mathematics survived intact.

Newton (1669, *De analysi*) used power series to compute roots with unprecedented accuracy — the same algorithm that `scipy.optimize.newton` implements today. The quadratic convergence Newton intuitively explored was formalized by Kantorovich in 1948, earning him the Nobel Prize in Economics in 1975 (for applications in linear programming and functional analysis, but the mathematical toolkit is the same).

### Where Linearization Lives Today

- **ML:** Gradient descent is order-1 Newton (gradient without Hessian). Adam, Adagrad, RMSProp are variants that approximate curvature without explicit calculation of $f''$. BFGS and L-BFGS are quasi-Newton methods that iteratively accumulate curvature information.
- **Quantum Physics:** Perturbation theory is order-1 Taylor (first-order terms) and order-2 Taylor (corrections). Rayleigh-Schrödinger perturbation theory is a direct application of the Taylor hierarchy.
- **Finance:** Black-Scholes (Lesson 119) uses stochastic Taylor expansion (Itô's lemma) — the second-order version for Brownian processes. The Greeks (Delta, Gamma) are the Taylor coefficients of the option price with respect to the underlying asset.
- **Control Engineering:** Linearization at an operating point is Taylor $P_1$ applied to the nonlinear model. The design of the classic PID controller operates on this local linearization.

### What This Term Actually Taught

These are not separate techniques. It's a single idea — approximating functions by polynomials of increasing degree — in eight different contexts. Those who internalized this understand why JAX, PyTorch, and TensorFlow implement autodiff: they are machines for calculating order-1 Taylor coefficients (gradients). The next step (Hessian, curvature, second-order methods) is Taylor of order 2.

</Porta>

### Case 1: Pricing a Product in the Brazilian Market

A footwear company in Franca (SP) has a cost function $C(q) = 0.5q^2 + 20q + 800$ (Reais) to produce $q$ pairs per day. The selling price is R\$ 60 per pair. Maximum profit:

$\pi(q) = 60q - C(q) = -0.5q^2 + 40q - 800$

$\pi'(q) = -q + 40 = 0 \Rightarrow q^* = 40$ pairs/day.

$\pi(40) = -0.5 \cdot 1600 + 40 \cdot 40 - 800 = -800 + 1600 - 800 = 0$ Reais.

Zero profit — the company is at the break-even point. To make a profit, it needs to either reduce fixed costs (R\$ 800) or raise the price.

### Case 2: Kinematics of a Vehicle on a Federal Highway

A truck on BR-381 has position $s(t) = 20t - 0.5t^2$ (meters) for $0 \leq t \leq 20$ s (emergency braking phase). Zero velocity: $v(t) = 20 - t = 0 \Rightarrow t = 20$ s. Stopping distance: $s(20) = 400 - 200 = 200$ meters. Deceleration: $a = s'' = -1$ m/s². Consistent with braking a loaded truck on dry asphalt.

### Case 3: Approximating the SELIC Rate via Taylor Series

If the SELIC rate $r(t)$ can be approximated by $r(t) \approx r(0) + r'(0) t$ for $t$ in months, and in the previous quarter $r(0) = 10.5\%$ and $r'(0) = 0.25\%$/month, the expected rate in 3 months is $r(3) \approx 10.5 + 0.75 = 11.25\%$. This is a first-order Taylor linearization applied to macroeconomic forecasting.

## Solved Examples

<Exemplo titulo="Optimization of an Open-Top Box (Application)" numero="1">

**Problem:** From a $24 \times 24$ cm sheet of cardboard, squares of side length $x$ are cut from the four corners, and the flaps are folded up to form an open-top box. Determine the value of $x$ that maximizes the volume.

**Strategy:** Express the volume as a function of $x$, find the critical point via $V'(x) = 0$, confirm it's a maximum using the second derivative test, and verify that $x$ is within the physical domain $0 < x < 12$.

**Solution:**

1. **Volume:** $V(x) = x(24 - 2x)^2$, domain: $x \in (0, 12)$.
2. **Expand:** $V(x) = x(576 - 96x + 4x^2) = 4x^3 - 96x^2 + 576x$.
3. **Differentiate:** $V'(x) = 12x^2 - 192x + 576 = 12(x^2 - 16x + 48) = 12(x-4)(x-12)$.
4. **Critical points in $(0,12)$:** $x = 4$ (the $x = 12$ is at the boundary, yielding zero volume).
5. **Test:** $V''(x) = 24x - 192$; $V''(4) = 96 - 192 = -96 < 0 \Rightarrow$ local maximum.
6. **Maximum Volume:** $V(4) = 4(24 - 8)^2 = 4 \cdot 256 = 1024\ \text{cm}^3$.

**Verification:** $V(0) = 0$, $V(12) = 0$, $V(4) = 1024 > 0$. The maximum is interior to the domain. Check: $V(4) = 4 \cdot 16^2 = 4 \cdot 256 = 1024$.

**Source.** [OpenStax Calculus Vol. 1 §4.7, ex. 4.41](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/4-7-applied-optimization-problems) — CC-BY-NC-SA license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Indeterminate Limit via L'Hôpital's Rule (Application)" numero="2">

**Problem:** Calculate $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{e^x - 1 - x}{x^2}$.

**Strategy:** The form is $0/0$ at $x = 0$. Apply L'Hôpital's Rule once and check if the new form is still $0/0$; if so, apply it again. Alternatively, use the Maclaurin series of $e^x$ to derive the result more quickly.

**Solution:**

1. **Check form:** $e^0 - 1 - 0 = 0$ and $0^2 = 0$ — $0/0$ indeterminate form.
2. **1st L'Hôpital application:**
$$\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{e^x - 1}{2x}$$
Still $0/0$ (numerator $\to 0$, denominator $\to 0$).
3. **2nd application:**
$$\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{e^x}{2} = \frac{e^0}{2} = \frac{1}{2}.$$
4. **Verification via Taylor:** $e^x = 1 + x + x^2/2 + O(x^3)$, so $e^x - 1 - x = x^2/2 + O(x^3)$. Then $\frac{e^x - 1 - x}{x^2} \to \frac{x^2/2}{x^2} = \frac{1}{2}$. Consistent.

**Verification:** For $x = 0.1$: numerator $= e^{0.1} - 1.1 \approx 1.10517 - 1.1 = 0.00517$; denominator $= 0.01$; ratio $\approx 0.517 \approx 1/2$.

**Source.** [Active Calculus §2.6, exercise on indeterminate forms](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-2-8-lhopital.html) — CC-BY-NC-SA license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Maclaurin Expansion and Limit (Intermediate)" numero="3">

**Problem:** Use the Maclaurin series of $\sin x$ to calculate $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin x - x + x^3/6}{x^5}$.

**Strategy:** Expand $\sin x$ in Maclaurin series up to order 5, substitute into the numerator, and simplify — Taylor series is more elegant than applying L'Hôpital's Rule five times.

**Solution:**

1. **Maclaurin series of $\sin x$:**
$$\sin x = x - \frac{x^3}{6} + \frac{x^5}{120} - \frac{x^7}{5040} + \cdots$$
2. **Numerator:**
$$\sin x - x + \frac{x^3}{6} = \left(x - \frac{x^3}{6} + \frac{x^5}{120} - \cdots\right) - x + \frac{x^3}{6} = \frac{x^5}{120} + O(x^7).$$
3. **Limit:**
$$\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{x^5/120 + O(x^7)}{x^5} = \frac{1}{120}.$$

<Exemplo titulo="Complete Sketch of a Rational Function (Intermediate)" numero="4">

**Problem:** Sketch $f(x) = \dfrac{x^2}{x^2 - 1}$, identifying domain, asymptotes, extrema, and concavity.

**Strategy:** Follow the 7-step function analysis pipeline. The function is rational with easily identifiable denominator zeros.

**Solution:**

1. **Domain:** $x^2 - 1 \neq 0 \Rightarrow x \neq \pm 1$. Domain: $\mathbb{R} \setminus \{-1, 1\}$.
2. **Intercepts/Symmetry:** $f(0) = 0$ (y-intercept). Zeros: $x^2 = 0 \Rightarrow x = 0$. $f(-x) = f(x)$: even function (symmetric about the y-axis).
3. **Asymptotes:** vertical at $x = \pm 1$; horizontal: $\lim_{x\to\pm\infty} f(x) = 1$ (asymptote $y = 1$).
4. **$f'$:** $f'(x) = \dfrac{-2x}{(x^2-1)^2}$. Critical point: $x = 0$. Signs: $f' > 0$ for $x < 0$; $f' < 0$ for $x > 0$ — local maximum at $x = 0$.
5. **$f''$:** $f''(x) = \dfrac{2(3x^2+1)}{(x^2-1)^3}$. For $x \in (-1,1)$: denominator $< 0$, numerator $> 0$, thus $f'' < 0$ (concave down). For $|x| > 1$: $f'' > 0$ (concave up). No inflection points in the domain (sign change of $f''$ occurs at points outside the domain $x = \pm 1$).
6. **Extremum:** $f(0) = 0$ — local (and absolute) maximum in $(-1,1)$.

**Verification:** $f(0) = 0 < 1 = \lim_{x\to\infty} f(x)$ makes sense: in the interval $(-1,1)$ the function goes from $0$ down to $-\infty$ at the boundaries and back up; the maximum is $f(0)=0$ in this interval.

**Source.** [APEX Calculus §3.5, ex. 42](https://www.apexcalculus.com/) — CC-BY-NC license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Newton-Raphson for a Transcendental Equation (Real Modeling)" numero="5">

**Problem:** An investment of R\$ 10,000 grows at a monthly rate $r$ to be determined. After 12 months, the amount is R\$ 11,268. Solve $f(r) = (1+r)^{12} - 1.1268 = 0$ using Newton-Raphson method, with $x_0 = 0.01$ (1% per month).

**Strategy:** $f'(r) = 12(1+r)^{11}$. Iterate $x_{n+1} = x_n - f(x_n)/f'(x_n)$ until stabilization.

**Solution:**

Iteration 1: $f(0.01) = (1.01)^{12} - 1.1268 = 1.12683 - 1.1268 = 0.00003$.
$f'(0.01) = 12 \cdot (1.01)^{11} = 12 \cdot 1.11567 = 13.388$.
$x_1 = 0.01 - 0.00003/13.388 \approx 0.009998$.

After 2 iterations, $r^* \approx 1.00\%$ per month (annual nominal rate $\approx 12.68\%$ p.a.).

**Verification:** $(1.01)^{12} = 1.12683 \approx 1.1268$. Consistent with the problem statement.

**Source.** [APEX Calculus §6.1, Newton-Raphson application](https://www.apexcalculus.com/) — CC-BY-NC license.
</Exemplo>
