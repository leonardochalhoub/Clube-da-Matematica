<Porta nivel="formal" titulo="Statement, proof and extensions">

### Formal statement

<Teorema titulo="L'Hôpital's Rule (forms 0/0 and ∞/∞)">

Let <Eq>{`f`}</Eq> and <Eq>{`g`}</Eq> be differentiable in an open neighborhood of <Eq>{`a`}</Eq> (except possibly at <Eq>{`a`}</Eq>), with <Eq>{`g'(x) \\neq 0`}</Eq> in that neighborhood.

If <Eq>{`\\lim_{x \\to a} f(x) = \\lim_{x \\to a} g(x) = 0`}</Eq> (form <Eq>{`0/0`}</Eq>), or if <Eq>{`\\lim_{x \\to a} |f(x)| = \\lim_{x \\to a} |g(x)| = \\infty`}</Eq> (form <Eq>{`\\infty/\\infty`}</Eq>), then:

<Eq display>{`\\lim_{x \\to a} \\frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \\lim_{x \\to a} \\frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)}`}</Eq>

provided the right-hand side exists (in <Eq>{`\\mathbb{R}`}</Eq> or is <Eq>{`\\pm\\infty`}</Eq>).

**Validity:** the rule also applies to <Eq>{`x \\to a^+`}</Eq>, <Eq>{`x \\to a^-`}</Eq>, <Eq>{`x \\to +\\infty`}</Eq>, and <Eq>{`x \\to -\\infty`}</Eq>.

</Teorema>

> "L'Hôpital's rule simplifies the evaluation of limits of quotients when both numerator and denominator approach 0 or ∞. The key is recognizing the indeterminate form, applying the rule, and checking that the resulting limit actually exists." — [OpenStax Calculus Vol. 1, §4.8](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/4-8-lhopitals-rule)

### Idea of the proof (case 0/0)

By **Cauchy's Mean Value Theorem**: if <Eq>{`f(a) = g(a) = 0`}</Eq>, then for <Eq>{`x`}</Eq> close to <Eq>{`a`}</Eq> there exists <Eq>{`\\xi`}</Eq> between <Eq>{`a`}</Eq> and <Eq>{`x`}</Eq> such that:

<Eq display>{`\\frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \\frac{f(x) - f(a)}{g(x) - g(a)} = \\frac{f'(\\xi)}{g'(\\xi)}`}</Eq>

When <Eq>{`x \\to a`}</Eq>, we have <Eq>{`\\xi \\to a`}</Eq>, and the limit becomes: <Eq>{`f(x)/g(x) \\to f'(a)/g'(a)`}</Eq> (when the ratio of derivatives converges).

### Extension to other indeterminate forms

<Definicao titulo="Table of reductions">

| Form | Reduction |
|---|---|
| <Eq>{`0/0`}</Eq> | Apply L'Hôpital directly |
| <Eq>{`\\infty/\\infty`}</Eq> | Apply L'Hôpital directly |
| <Eq>{`0 \\cdot \\infty`}</Eq> | Rewrite: <Eq>{`f \\cdot g = f / (1/g)`}</Eq> or <Eq>{`g / (1/f)`}</Eq> |
| <Eq>{`\\infty - \\infty`}</Eq> | Combine into a fraction (LCD or rationalization) |
| <Eq>{`1^{\\infty}, 0^0, \\infty^0`}</Eq> | Take logarithm: <Eq>{`f^g = e^{g \\ln f}`}</Eq>; apply L'Hôpital to the exponent |

</Definicao>

<Insight>

**Pitfalls with the rule:**
1. **Check the form before applying.** If <Eq>{`\\lim f/g`}</Eq> is not <Eq>{`0/0`}</Eq> or <Eq>{`\\infty/\\infty`}</Eq>, L'Hôpital is **invalid**.
2. **Loop:** <Eq>{`\\lim (e^x - 1)/\\sin x`}</Eq> can enter a cycle if applied carelessly. Check that the result converges.
3. **Alternative:** Taylor series frequently resolve the indeterminacy more quickly and without risk of looping.

</Insight>

> "Note carefully: L'Hôpital's Rule says that the limit of a quotient of functions equals the limit of the quotient of their derivatives, provided the original limit is in the form 0/0 or ∞/∞. This is not the same as the derivative of a quotient." — [Active Calculus §2.8](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-3-2-LHR.html)

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a five-year-old">

Imagine you have two piles of blocks that are shrinking. If both piles reach zero at the same time, it's hard to say which is "relatively" larger — both have disappeared!

L'Hôpital's Rule is like a trick: instead of comparing the piles themselves, you compare **how fast they are shrinking**. Who is shrinking faster? That speed is what matters!

It's like braking: if two cars brake to a stop, what matters is not where they stopped (both at the same place), but which one braked more harshly.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a ten-year-old">

You want to compute <Eq>{`\\lim_{x \\to 0} \\frac{\\sin x}{x}`}</Eq>.

If you substitute <Eq>{`x = 0`}</Eq>, you get <Eq>{`0/0`}</Eq>: it doesn't work.

L'Hôpital's Rule says: when you have <Eq>{`0/0`}</Eq>, you can **differentiate top and bottom separately** and try the limit again:

<Eq display>{`\\lim_{x \\to 0} \\frac{\\sin x}{x} = \\lim_{x \\to 0} \\frac{\\cos x}{1} = \\frac{1}{1} = 1`}</Eq>

It works because the derivative measures the "rate of change" — when everything goes to zero, the ratio of rates of change tells us which function goes to zero faster.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For the high school student">

**When to use L'Hôpital:**

1. Calculate the limit normally.
2. If you get <Eq>{`0/0`}</Eq> or <Eq>{`\\infty/\\infty`}</Eq>: it is an indeterminate form.
3. Differentiate numerator and denominator **separately** (this is not the quotient rule!).
4. Try the limit again. If still indeterminate, repeat.

**Other forms:**

- <Eq>{`0 \\cdot \\infty`}</Eq>: rewrite as a fraction and apply L'Hôpital.
- <Eq>{`1^{\\infty}`}</Eq> or <Eq>{`0^0`}</Eq>: take <Eq>{`\\ln`}</Eq>, compute the limit of the logarithm, then exponentiate.

**Classic pitfall:** verify that the form IS indeterminate before applying. In <Eq>{`\\lim_{x \\to 0} (x + 1)/x`}</Eq>, the result is <Eq>{`\\infty`}</Eq> — not <Eq>{`\\infty/\\infty`}</Eq>, and L'Hôpital does not apply.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For the engineering student">

L'Hôpital's Rule is a consequence of **Cauchy's Mean Value Theorem** (generalization of Lagrange's MVT): if <Eq>{`f`}</Eq> and <Eq>{`g`}</Eq> are continuous on <Eq>{`[a, b]`}</Eq> and differentiable on <Eq>{`(a, b)`}</Eq> with <Eq>{`g' \\neq 0`}</Eq>, there exists <Eq>{`c \\in (a, b)`}</Eq> such that:

<Eq display>{`\\frac{f(b) - f(a)}{g(b) - g(a)} = \\frac{f'(c)}{g'(c)}`}</Eq>

**Alternative — Taylor Series:** if <Eq>{`f`}</Eq> and <Eq>{`g`}</Eq> are analytic near <Eq>{`a`}</Eq>, expand in series and divide. Often faster than applying L'Hôpital multiple times.

Example: <Eq>{`\\lim_{x \\to 0} (\\sin x - x)/x^3`}</Eq>. Taylor: <Eq>{`\\sin x = x - x^3/6 + \\ldots`}</Eq>, so <Eq>{`\\sin x - x = -x^3/6 + \\ldots`}</Eq>. Thus the limit is <Eq>{`-1/6`}</Eq>. Applying L'Hôpital three times would give the same result — but Taylor is more direct.

**Hierarchy of infinities:** <Eq>{`\\ln x \\ll x^n \\ll e^x \\ll e^{x^2}`}</Eq> as <Eq>{`x \\to \\infty`}</Eq>. L'Hôpital formalizes that derivatives preserve this hierarchy.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For the senior professional">

The history behind the rule is one of mathematics' most curious episodes. Johann Bernoulli discovered the result in 1694 and communicated it to the Marquis de L'Hôpital, his pupil. L'Hôpital included it in the first calculus book in history, "Analyse des Infiniment Petits" (1696), without credit to Bernoulli — and the result carries the wrong name to this day.

Cauchy's Mean Value Theorem, published in 1821, provided the rigorous proof. Before that, "limits" were treated intuitively and the rule was justified by differential arguments from Leibniz's infinitesimal analysis.

In modern real analysis, L'Hôpital is a theorem about relative rates of change. In terms of Landau big-o notation, it says that if <Eq>{`f(x) = o(g(x))`}</Eq> near <Eq>{`a`}</Eq>, then under regularity conditions, <Eq>{`f'/g' \\to 0`}</Eq> as well. The converse is delicate — there are functions where <Eq>{`f'/g'`}</Eq> diverges but <Eq>{`f/g`}</Eq> converges (see APEX §6.7 for a counterexample).

In quantitative finance, limits of indeterminate forms appear in option pricing: in the Black-Scholes model, when volatility <Eq>{`\\sigma \\to 0`}</Eq>, prices converge to parity values — a limit involving <Eq>{`0/0`}</Eq> forms in the logarithmic Black-Scholes formula, resolved precisely by L'Hôpital.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practical application (US context)">

**Natural limit in continuous compound interest.** The number <Eq>{`e`}</Eq> arises from a classic indeterminate limit of form <Eq>{`1^{\\infty}`}</Eq>:

<Eq display>{`e = \\lim_{n \\to \\infty} \\left(1 + \\frac{1}{n}\\right)^n`}</Eq>

Take the logarithm: <Eq>{`\\ln L = \\lim_{n \\to \\infty} n \\ln(1 + 1/n)`}</Eq> — form <Eq>{`\\infty \\cdot 0`}</Eq>. Rewrite:

<Eq display>{`\\ln L = \\lim_{n \\to \\infty} \\frac{\\ln(1 + 1/n)}{1/n}`}</Eq>

Form <Eq>{`0/0`}</Eq>. L'Hôpital: numerator differentiates to <Eq>{`(-1/n^2)/(1+1/n)`}</Eq>, denominator to <Eq>{`-1/n^2`}</Eq>. Ratio: <Eq>{`1/(1+1/n) \\to 1`}</Eq>. Thus <Eq>{`\\ln L = 1`}</Eq>, or <Eq>{`L = e`}</Eq>.

**Application in US standardized tests:** the limit <Eq>{`\\lim_{x \\to 0} \\sin(x)/x = 1`}</Eq> — proven elegantly via L'Hôpital — is a foundation of trigonometric limits in calculus courses and competitive exams like the AMC and AIME.

**In financial markets:** annual interest rates converted to continuous rates use exactly <Eq>{`e^{rt}`}</Eq>, derived from this limit. The Federal Reserve uses continuous compounding in risk models and option pricing frameworks.

</Porta>

## Worked examples

<Exemplo titulo="Simple form 0/0">

**Problem.** Compute <Eq>{`\\lim_{x \\to 0} \\dfrac{\\sin x}{x}`}</Eq>.

**Strategy.** Verify indeterminate form; apply L'Hôpital once.

**Solution.**

Direct substitution: <Eq>{`\\sin 0 / 0 = 0/0`}</Eq> — indeterminate form. Conditions are met (<Eq>{`f = \\sin x`}</Eq> and <Eq>{`g = x`}</Eq> are differentiable, <Eq>{`g' = 1 \\neq 0`}</Eq>).

<Eq display>{`\\lim_{x \\to 0} \\frac{\\sin x}{x} \\stackrel{0/0}{=} \\lim_{x \\to 0} \\frac{\\cos x}{1} = \\cos 0 = 1`}</Eq>

**Verification.** This result is the fundamental trigonometric limit — consistent with the Taylor expansion <Eq>{`\\sin x = x - x^3/6 + \\ldots`}</Eq>, so <Eq>{`\\sin x/x \\to 1`}</Eq>.

**Source.** [Active Calculus §2.8, Exercise 2.8.1](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-3-2-LHR.html) · CC-BY-NC-SA

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Form 0/0 with iterated L'Hôpital">

**Problem.** Compute <Eq>{`\\lim_{x \\to 0} \\dfrac{e^x - 1 - x}{x^2}`}</Eq>.

**Strategy.** Form <Eq>{`0/0`}</Eq>. May need to apply L'Hôpital more than once.

**Solution.**

Direct substitution: <Eq>{`(1-1-0)/0 = 0/0`}</Eq>.

<Eq display>{`\\lim_{x \\to 0} \\frac{e^x - 1 - x}{x^2} \\stackrel{0/0}{=} \\lim_{x \\to 0} \\frac{e^x - 1}{2x} \\stackrel{0/0}{=} \\lim_{x \\to 0} \\frac{e^x}{2} = \\frac{1}{2}`}</Eq>

**Verification.** Taylor: <Eq>{`e^x = 1 + x + x^2/2 + \\ldots`}</Eq>, so <Eq>{`e^x - 1 - x = x^2/2 + \\ldots`}</Eq> and the quotient <Eq>{`\\to 1/2`}</Eq>. Confirmed.

**Source.** [OpenStax Calculus Vol. 1, §4.8, Example 4.40](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/4-8-lhopitals-rule) · CC-BY-NC-SA

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Form ∞/∞ with x → ∞">

**Problem.** Compute <Eq>{`\\lim_{x \\to \\infty} \\dfrac{x^2}{e^x}`}</Eq>.

**Strategy.** Form <Eq>{`\\infty/\\infty`}</Eq>. Apply iterated L'Hôpital until resolved.

**Solution.**

<Eq display>{`\\lim_{x \\to \\infty} \\frac{x^2}{e^x} \\stackrel{\\infty/\\infty}{=} \\lim_{x \\to \\infty} \\frac{2x}{e^x} \\stackrel{\\infty/\\infty}{=} \\lim_{x \\to \\infty} \\frac{2}{e^x} = 0`}</Eq>

**Verification.** Result is 0 — the exponential grows faster than any power. This confirms the hierarchy <Eq>{`x^n = o(e^x)`}</Eq> for all <Eq>{`n`}</Eq>.

**Source.** [Active Calculus §2.8, Example 2.8.3](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-3-2-LHR.html) · CC-BY-NC-SA

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Form 1^∞ via logarithm">

**Problem.** Compute <Eq>{`\\lim_{x \\to 0^+} x^x`}</Eq> (form <Eq>{`0^0`}</Eq>).

**Strategy.** Take logarithm to convert to <Eq>{`0/0`}</Eq> or <Eq>{`\\infty/\\infty`}</Eq>; then exponentiate.

**Solution.**

Let <Eq>{`L = \\lim_{x \\to 0^+} x^x`}</Eq>. Then <Eq>{`\\ln L = \\lim_{x \\to 0^+} x \\ln x`}</Eq> — form <Eq>{`0 \\cdot (-\\infty)`}</Eq>.

Rewrite: <Eq>{`x \\ln x = \\dfrac{\\ln x}{1/x}`}</Eq> — form <Eq>{`\\infty/\\infty`}</Eq>.

<Eq display>{`\\ln L = \\lim_{x \\to 0^+} \\frac{\\ln x}{1/x} \\stackrel{\\infty/\\infty}{=} \\lim_{x \\to 0^+} \\frac{1/x}{-1/x^2} = \\lim_{x \\to 0^+} (-x) = 0`}</Eq>

Therefore <Eq>{`L = e^0 = 1`}</Eq>. The limit <Eq>{`\\lim_{x \\to 0^+} x^x = 1`}</Eq>.

**Verification.** Numerically: <Eq>{`(0.1)^{0.1} \\approx 0.794`}</Eq>, <Eq>{`(0.01)^{0.01} \\approx 0.955`}</Eq>, converging to 1.

**Source.** [OpenStax Calculus Vol. 1, §4.8, Example 4.43](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/4-8-lhopitals-rule) · CC-BY-NC-SA

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Form ∞ − ∞ via combination">

**Problem.** Compute <Eq>{`\\lim_{x \\to 0} \\left(\\dfrac{1}{\\sin x} - \\dfrac{1}{x}\\right)`}</Eq>.

**Strategy.** Form <Eq>{`\\infty - \\infty`}</Eq>. Combine into a single fraction, then apply L'Hôpital.

**Solution.**

<Eq display>{`\\frac{1}{\\sin x} - \\frac{1}{x} = \\frac{x - \\sin x}{x \\sin x}`}</Eq>

As <Eq>{`x \\to 0`}</Eq>: numerator <Eq>{`\\to 0`}</Eq>, denominator <Eq>{`\\to 0`}</Eq> — form <Eq>{`0/0`}</Eq>.

<Eq display>{`\\lim_{x \\to 0} \\frac{x - \\sin x}{x \\sin x} \\stackrel{0/0}{=} \\lim_{x \\to 0} \\frac{1 - \\cos x}{\\sin x + x \\cos x} \\stackrel{0/0}{=} \\lim_{x \\to 0} \\frac{\\sin x}{2\\cos x - x\\sin x} = \\frac{0}{2} = 0`}</Eq>

**Verification.** Taylor: <Eq>{`x - \\sin x = x^3/6 - \\ldots`}</Eq> and <Eq>{`x \\sin x = x^2 - x^4/6 + \\ldots`}</Eq>. Quotient: <Eq>{`(x^3/6)/x^2 = x/6 \\to 0`}</Eq>. Confirmed.

**Source.** [APEX Calculus §6.7, Example 6.7.4](https://www.apexcalculus.com/) · CC-BY-NC

</Exemplo>
