<Porta nivel="formal" titulo="Statement, proof, and extensions">

### Formal Statement

<Teorema titulo="L'Hôpital's Rule (0/0 and ∞/∞ forms)">

Let <Eq>{`f`}</Eq> and <Eq>{`g`}</Eq> be differentiable on an open interval containing <Eq>{`a`}</Eq> (except possibly at <Eq>{`a`}</Eq>), with <Eq>{`g'(x) \\neq 0`}</Eq> on this interval.

If <Eq>{`\\lim_{x \\to a} f(x) = \\lim_{x \\to a} g(x) = 0`}</Eq> (form <Eq>{`0/0`}</Eq>), or if <Eq>{`\\lim_{x \\to a} |f(x)| = \\lim_{x \\to a} |g(x)| = \\infty`}</Eq> (form <Eq>{`\\infty/\\infty`}</Eq>), then:

<Eq display>{`\\lim_{x \\to a} \\frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \\lim_{x \\to a} \\frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)}`}</Eq>

provided the right-hand side exists (in <Eq>{`\\mathbb{R}`}</Eq> or is <Eq>{`\\pm\\infty`}</Eq>).

<strong>Validity:</strong> the rule also applies to <Eq>{`x \\to a^+`}</Eq>, <Eq>{`x \\to a^-`}</Eq>, <Eq>{`x \\to +\\infty`}</Eq>, and <Eq>{`x \\to -\\infty`}</Eq>.

</Teorema>

> "L'Hôpital's rule simplifies the evaluation of limits of quotients when both numerator and denominator approach 0 or ∞. The key is recognizing the indeterminate form, applying the rule, and checking that the resulting limit actually exists." — [OpenStax Calculus Vol. 1, §4.8](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/4-8-lhopitals-rule)

### Proof Idea (0/0 case)

By **Cauchy's Mean Value Theorem**: if <Eq>{`f(a) = g(a) = 0`}</Eq>, for <Eq>{`x`}</Eq> near <Eq>{`a`}</Eq> there exists <Eq>{`\\xi`}</Eq> between <Eq>{`a`}</Eq> and <Eq>{`x`}</Eq> such that:

<Eq display>{`\\frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \\frac{f(x) - f(a)}{g(x) - g(a)} = \\frac{f'(\\xi)}{g'(\\xi)}`}</Eq>

As <Eq>{`x \\to a`}</Eq>, we have <Eq>{`\\xi \\to a`}</Eq>, and the limit becomes: <Eq>{`f(x)/g(x) \\to f'(a)/g'(a)`}</Eq> (when the ratio of derivatives converges).

### Extension to Other Indeterminate Forms

<Definicao titulo="Reduction Table">

| Form | Reduction |
|---|---|
| <Eq>{`0/0`}</Eq> | Apply L'Hôpital directly |
| <Eq>{`\\infty/\\infty`}</Eq> | Apply L'Hôpital directly |
| <Eq>{`0 \\cdot \\infty`}</Eq> | Rewrite: <Eq>{`f \\cdot g = f / (1/g)`}</Eq> or <Eq>{`g / (1/f)`}</Eq> |
| <Eq>{`\\infty - \\infty`}</Eq> | Combine into a single fraction (common denominator or rationalization) |
| <Eq>{`1^\\infty, 0^0, \\infty^0`}</Eq> | Take the logarithm: <Eq>{`f^g = e^{g \\ln f}`}</Eq>; apply L'Hôpital to the exponent |

</Definicao>

<Insight>

<strong>Cautions about the rule:</strong>
1. <strong>Verify the form before applying.</strong> If <Eq>{`\\lim f/g`}</Eq> is not <Eq>{`0/0`}</Eq> or <Eq>{`\\infty/\\infty`}</Eq>, L'Hôpital is <strong>invalid</strong>.
2. <strong>Loops:</strong> <Eq>{`\\lim (e^x - 1)/\\sin x`}</Eq> can enter a cycle if misapplied. Check if the result converges.
3. <strong>Alternative:</strong> Taylor series often resolve indeterminate forms more quickly and without the risk of loops.

</Insight>

> "Note carefully: L'Hôpital's Rule says that the limit of a quotient of functions equals the limit of the quotient of their derivatives, provided the original limit is in the form 0/0 or ∞/∞. This is not the same as the derivative of a quotient." — [Active Calculus §2.8](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-2-8-LHR.html)

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

Imagine you have two piles of blocks that are shrinking. If both piles reach zero at the same time, it's hard to say which one was bigger 'comparatively' — they both disappeared!

L'Hôpital's Rule is like a trick: instead of comparing the piles, you compare <strong>how fast they are shrinking</strong>. Which one is shrinking faster? That speed is what matters!

It's like a race: if two cars brake to a stop, what matters isn't where they stopped (both at the same spot), but how sharply they braked.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10"titulo="For a 10-year-old">

You want to calculate <Eq>{`\\lim_{x \\to 0} \\frac{\\sin x}{x}`}</Eq>.

If you substitute <Eq>{`x = 0`}</Eq>, you get <Eq>{`0/0`}</Eq>: that doesn't work.

L'Hôpital's Rule says: when you have <Eq>{`0/0`}</Eq>, you can <strong>take the derivative of the top and bottom separately</strong> and try the limit again:

<Eq display>{`\\lim_{x \\to 0} \\frac{\\sin x}{x} = \\lim_{x \\to 0} \\frac{\\cos x}{1} = \\frac{1}{1} = 1`}</Eq>

This works because the derivative measures the 'rate of change' — when everything is going to zero, the ratio of the rates of change tells us which function goes to zero faster.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15"titulo="For the high school student">

<strong>When to use L'Hôpital:</strong>

1. Calculate the limit normally.
2. If you get <Eq>{`0/0`}</Eq> or <Eq>{`\\infty/\\infty`}</Eq>: it's an indeterminate form.
3. Differentiate the numerator and denominator <strong>separately</strong> (this is not the quotient rule!).
4. Try the limit again. If still indeterminate, repeat.

<strong>Other forms:</strong>

- <Eq>{`0 \\cdot \\infty`}</Eq>: rewrite as a fraction and apply L'Hôpital.
- <Eq>{`1^\\infty`}</Eq> or <Eq>{`0^0`}</Eq>: take the <Eq>{`\\ln`}</Eq>, calculate the limit of the logarithm, then exponentiate.

<strong>Classic trap:</strong> check if the form IS indeed indeterminate before applying. In <Eq>{`\\lim_{x \\to 0} (x + 1)/x`}</Eq>, the result is <Eq>{`\\infty`}</Eq> — it's not <Eq>{`\\infty/\\infty`}</Eq>, so L'Hôpital doesn't apply.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25"titulo="For the engineering student">

L'Hôpital's Rule is a consequence of <strong>Cauchy's Mean Value Theorem</strong> (a generalization of Lagrange's MVT): if <Eq>{`f`}</Eq> and <Eq>{`g`}</Eq> are continuous on <Eq>{`[a, b]`}</Eq> and differentiable on <Eq>{`(a, b)`}</Eq> with <Eq>{`g' \\neq 0`}</Eq>, there exists <Eq>{`c \\in (a, b)`}</Eq> such that:

<Eq display>{`\\frac{f(b) - f(a)}{g(b) - g(a)} = \\frac{f'(c)}{g'(c)}`}</Eq>

<strong>Alternative — Taylor Series:</strong> if <Eq>{`f`}</Eq> and <Eq>{`g`}</Eq> are analytic around <Eq>{`a`}</Eq>, expand them into series and divide. This is often faster than applying L'Hôpital multiple times.

Example: <Eq>{`\\lim_{x \\to 0} (\\sin x - x)/x^3`}</Eq>. Taylor series: <Eq>{`\\sin x = x - x^3/6 + \\ldots`}</Eq>, so <Eq>{`\\sin x - x = -x^3/6 + \\ldots`}</Eq>. Thus the limit is <Eq>{`-1/6`}</Eq>. Applying L'Hôpital three times would yield the same result — but Taylor series are more direct.

<strong>Hierarchy of infinities:</strong> <Eq>{`\\ln x \\ll x^n \\ll e^x \\ll e^{x^2}`}</Eq> as <Eq>{`x \\to \\infty`}</Eq>. L'Hôpital formalizes that derivatives preserve this hierarchy.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40"titulo="For the senior professional">

The history behind the rule is one of mathematics' most curious episodes. Johann Bernoulli discovered the result in 1694 and communicated it to the Marquis de L'Hôpital, his student. L'Hôpital included it in the first calculus textbook ever written, "Analyse des Infiniment Petits" (1696), without crediting Bernoulli — and the result carries the wrong name to this day.

Cauchy's Mean Value Theorem, published in 1821, provided the rigorous proof. Before that, 'limits' were treated intuitively, and the rule was justified by Leibniz's differential arguments.

In modern real analysis, L'Hôpital is a theorem about relative rates of change. In terms of Landau's little-o notation, it states that if <Eq>{`f(x) = o(g(x))`}</Eq> near <Eq>{`a`}</Eq>, then under regularity conditions, <Eq>{`f'/g' \\to 0`}</Eq> as well. The converse is delicate — there are functions where <Eq>{`f'/g'`}</Eq> diverges but <Eq>{`f/g`}</Eq> converges (see APEX §6.7 for a counterexample).

In quantitative finance, limits of indeterminate forms appear in option pricing: in the Black-Scholes model, as volatility <Eq>{`\\sigma \\to 0`}</Eq>, prices converge to parity values — a limit involving <Eq>{`0/0`}</Eq> forms in the logarithmic Black-Scholes formula, resolved precisely by L'Hôpital.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica"titulo="Brazilian practical application">

<strong>Natural limit in continuous interest calculation.</strong> The number <Eq>{`e`}</Eq> arises from a classic limit involving an <Eq>{`1^\\infty`}</Eq> indeterminate form:

<Eq display>{`e = \\lim_{n \\to \\infty} \\left(1 + \\frac{1}{n}\\right)^n`}</Eq>

Take the logarithm: <Eq>{`\\ln L = \\lim_{n \\to \\infty} n \\ln(1 + 1/n)`}</Eq> — an <Eq>{`\\infty \\cdot 0`}</Eq> form. Rewrite:

<Eq display>{`\\ln L = \\lim_{n \\to \\infty} \\frac{\\ln(1 + 1/n)}{1/n}`}</Eq>

This is a <Eq>{`0/0`}</Eq> form. Applying L'Hôpital: the numerator's derivative is <Eq>{`(-1/n^2)/(1+1/n)`}</Eq>, the denominator's is <Eq>{`-1/n^2`}</Eq>. The ratio is <Eq>{`1/(1+1/n) \\to 1`}</Eq>. Thus <Eq>{`\\ln L = 1`}</Eq>, meaning <Eq>{`L = e`}</Eq>.

<strong>Application in ENEM and university entrance exams:</strong> the limit <Eq>{`\\lim_{x \\to 0} \\sin(x)/x = 1`}</Eq> — elegantly proven via L'Hôpital — is the basis for trigonometric limits requested in Brazilian exams like FUVEST and Unicamp.

<strong>In the Brazilian Financial System:</strong> annual interest rates converted to continuous rates use exactly <Eq>{`e^{rt}`}</Eq>, derived from this limit. The Central Bank uses continuous compounding in risk models.

</Porta>

## Solved Examples

<Exemplo titulo="Simple 0/0 form">

<strong>Problem.</strong> Calculate <Eq>{`\\lim_{x \\to 0} \\dfrac{\\sin x}{x}`}</Eq>.

<strong>Strategy.</strong> Verify indeterminate form; apply L'Hôpital once.

<strong>Solution.</strong>

Direct substitution: <Eq>{`\\sin 0 / 0 = 0/0`}</Eq> — indeterminate form. Conditions are met (<Eq>{`f = \\sin x`}</Eq> and <Eq>{`g = x`}</Eq> are differentiable, <Eq>{`g' = 1 \\neq 0`}</Eq>).

<Eq display>{`\\lim_{x \\to 0} \\frac{\\sin x}{x} \\stackrel{0/0}{=} \\lim_{x \\to 0} \\frac{\\cos x}{1} = \\cos 0 = 1`}</Eq>

<strong>Verification.</strong> This result is the fundamental trigonometric limit — consistent with the Taylor expansion <Eq>{`\\sin x = x - x^3/6 + \\ldots`}</Eq>, therefore <Eq>{`\\sin x/x \\to 1`}</Eq>.

<strong>Source.</strong> [Active Calculus §2.8, Exercise 2.8.1](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-2-8-LHR.html) · CC-BY-NC-SA

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="0/0 form with iterated L'Hôpital">

<strong>Problem.</strong> Calculate <Eq>{`\\lim_{x \\to 0} \\dfrac{e^x - 1 - x}{x^2}`}</Eq>.

<strong>Strategy.</strong> <Eq>{`0/0`}</Eq> form. May require applying L'Hôpital more than once.

<strong>Solution.</strong>

Direct substitution: <Eq>{`(1-1-0)/0 = 0/0`}</Eq>.

<Eq display>{`\\lim_{x \\to 0} \\frac{e^x - 1 - x}{x^2} \\stackrel{0/0}{=} \\lim_{x \\to 0} \\frac{e^x - 1}{2x} \\stackrel{0/0}{=} \\lim_{x \\to 0} \\frac{e^x}{2} = \\frac{1}{2}`}</Eq>

<strong>Verification.</strong> Taylor series: <Eq>{`e^x = 1 + x + x^2/2 + \\ldots`}</Eq>, so <Eq>{`e^x - 1 - x = x^2/2 + \\ldots`}</Eq> and the quotient <Eq>{`\\to 1/2`}</Eq>. Confirmed.

<strong>Source.</strong> [OpenStax Calculus Vol. 1, §4.8, Example 4.40](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/4-8-lhopitals-rule) · CC-BY-NC-SA

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="∞/∞ form with x → ∞">

<strong>Problem.</strong> Calculate <Eq>{`\\lim_{x \\to \\infty} \\dfrac{x^2}{e^x}`}</Eq>.

<strong>Strategy.</strong> <Eq>{`\\infty/\\infty`}</Eq> form. Apply iterated L'Hôpital until resolved.

<strong>Solution.</strong>

<Eq display>{`\\lim_{x \\to \\infty} \\frac{x^2}{e^x} \\stackrel{\\infty/\\infty}{=} \\lim_{x \\to \\infty} \\frac{2x}{e^x} \\stackrel{\\infty/\\infty}{=} \\lim_{x \\to \\infty} \\frac{2}{e^x} = 0`}</Eq>

<strong>Verification.</strong> Result is 0 — the exponential grows faster than any power. This confirms the hierarchy <Eq>{`x^n = o(e^x)`}</Eq> for all <Eq>{`n`}</Eq>.

<strong>Source.</strong> [Active Calculus §2.8, Example 2.8.3](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-2-8-LHR.html) · CC-BY-NC-SA

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="1^∞ form via logarithm">

<strong>Problem.</strong> Calculate <Eq>{`\\lim_{x \\to 0^+} x^x`}</Eq> (form <Eq>{`0^0`}</Eq>).

<strong>Strategy.</strong> Take the logarithm to convert to <Eq>{`0/0`}</Eq> or <Eq>{`\\infty/\\infty`}</Eq> form; then exponentiate.

<strong>Solution.</strong>

Let <Eq>{`L = \\lim_{x \\to 0^+} x^x`}</Eq>. Then <Eq>{`\\ln L = \\lim_{x \\to 0^+} x \\ln x`}</Eq> — an <Eq>{`0 \\cdot (-\\infty)`}</Eq> form.

Rewrite: <Eq>{`x \\ln x = \\dfrac{\\ln x}{1/x}`}</Eq> — an <Eq>{`\\infty/\\infty`}</Eq> form.

<Eq display>{`\\ln L = \\lim_{x \\to 0^+} \\frac{\\ln x}{1/x} \\stackrel{\\infty/\\infty}{=} \\lim_{x \\to 0^+} \\frac{1/x}{-1/x^2} = \\lim_{x \\to 0^+} (-x) = 0`}</Eq>

Therefore <Eq>{`L = e^0 = 1`}</Eq>. The limit <Eq>{`\\lim_{x \\to 0^+} x^x = 1`}</Eq>.

<strong>Verification.</strong> Numerically: <Eq>{`(0.1)^{0.1} \\approx 0.794`}</Eq>, <Eq>{`(0.01)^{0.01} \\approx 0.955`}</Eq>, converging to 1.

<strong>Source.</strong> [OpenStax Calculus Vol. 1, §4.8, Example 4.43](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/4-8-lhopitals-rule) · CC-BY-NC-SA

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="∞ − ∞ form via combination">

<strong>Problem.</strong> Calculate <Eq>{`\\lim_{x \\to 0} \\left(\\dfrac{1}{\\sin x} - \\dfrac{1}{x}\\right)`}</Eq>.

<strong>Strategy.</strong> <Eq>{`\\infty - \\infty`}</Eq> form. Combine into a single fraction, then apply L'Hôpital.

<strong>Solution.</strong>

<Eq display>{`\\frac{1}{\\sin x} - \\frac{1}{x} = \\frac{x - \\sin x}{x \\sin x}`}</Eq>

As <Eq>{`x \\to 0`}</Eq>: the numerator <Eq>{`\\to 0`}</Eq>, the denominator <Eq>{`\\to 0`}</Eq> — an <Eq>{`0/0`}</Eq> form.

<Eq display>{`\\lim_{x \\to 0} \\frac{x - \\sin x}{x \\sin x} \\stackrel{0/0}{=} \\lim_{x \\to 0} \\frac{1 - \\cos x}{\\sin x + x \\cos x} \\stackrel{0/0}{=} \\lim_{x \\to 0} \\frac{\\sin x}{2\\cos x - x\\sin x} = \\frac{0}{2} = 0`}</Eq>

<strong>Verification.</strong> Taylor series: <Eq>{`x - \\sin x = x^3/6 - \\ldots`}</Eq> and <Eq>{`x \\sin x = x^2 - x^4/6 + \\ldots`}</Eq>. The quotient: <Eq>{`(x^3/6)/x^2 = x/6 \\to 0`}</Eq>. Confirmed.

<strong>Source.</strong> [APEX Calculus §6.7, Example 6.7.4](https://www.apexcalculus.com/) · CC-BY-NC

</Exemplo>
