### Fundamental Definitions

<Definicao titulo="Position, Velocity, Acceleration, Jerk">
Let $s: I \subset \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ be a position function in terms of time $t$, with $s$ differentiable.

$$v(t) = \frac{ds}{dt} = s'(t), \qquad a(t) = \frac{dv}{dt} = s''(t), \qquad j(t) = \frac{da}{dt} = s'''(t).$$

- **Speed**: $|v(t)|$ (non-negative scalar; differs from velocity when $v < 0$).
- **Displacement** over $[t_1, t_2]$: $\Delta s = s(t_2) - s(t_1)$ (can be negative).
- **Distance traveled**: $\int_{t_1}^{t_2} |v(t)|\, dt$ (always non-negative).
</Definicao>

> "The instantaneous velocity of an object is the limit of the average velocities of the object over shorter and shorter time intervals." — [Active Calculus §1.1](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-1-1-vel.html)

> "The position function $s(t)$ gives the position of an object along a number line at time $t$. The velocity function $v(t) = s'(t)$ gives the velocity of the object at time $t$." — [OpenStax Calculus Vol.1 §3.4](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/3-4-derivatives-as-rates-of-change)

### Standard Motion Cases

| Motion | $s(t)$ | $v(t)$ | $a(t)$ | Observation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rest | $s_0$ | $0$ | $0$ | fixed point |
| Uniform (MRU) | $s_0 + v_0 t$ | $v_0$ | $0$ | straight line on $s \times t$ graph |
| Uniformly Accelerated (MUV) | $s_0 + v_0 t + \tfrac{1}{2}a_0 t^2$ | $v_0 + a_0 t$ | $a_0$ | parabola |
| Simple Harmonic (MHS) | $A\cos(\omega t + \phi)$ | $-A\omega\sin(\omega t + \phi)$ | $-A\omega^2\cos(\omega t + \phi)$ | $a = -\omega^2 s$ |
| With Air Resistance | ODE solution | $v_\infty(1-e^{-kt/m})$ | decays to 0 | terminal velocity |

### Torricelli's Theorem (derivation via calculus)

<Teorema titulo="Torricelli's Equation">
For uniformly accelerated motion with constant acceleration $a_0$, the displacement $\Delta s$ and velocities $v_0, v_f$ satisfy:
$$v_f^2 = v_0^2 + 2a_0\,\Delta s.$$
**Proof:** $v(t) = v_0 + a_0 t$, $s(t) = s_0 + v_0 t + \tfrac{1}{2}a_0 t^2$. Isolate $t = (v - v_0)/a_0$ and substitute into $\Delta s$:
$$\Delta s = v_0 \cdot \frac{v_f - v_0}{a_0} + \frac{1}{2}a_0\left(\frac{v_f - v_0}{a_0}\right)^2 = \frac{v_f^2 - v_0^2}{2a_0}. \quad \blacksquare$$
</Teorema>

### Simple Harmonic Motion (SHM)

$x(t) = A\cos(\omega t + \phi)$ satisfies the ODE $\ddot x + \omega^2 x = 0$.

- Period: $T = 2\pi/\omega$.
- Frequency: $f = 1/T$.
- For a spring: $\omega = \sqrt{k/m}$; for a pendulum (small oscillations): $\omega = \sqrt{g/L}$.

<Insight>
In SHM, acceleration is always **proportional and opposite** to displacement: $a = -\omega^2 x$. The magnitude of acceleration is maximum at the extremes (where $|x| = A$) and zero at the equilibrium point. The magnitude of velocity is maximum at equilibrium and zero at the extremes — exactly the opposite.
</Insight>

### Kinematics in $\mathbb{R}^n$

For $\vec{r}(t) = (x(t), y(t), z(t)) \in \mathbb{R}^3$:

$$\vec{v}(t) = \dot{\vec{r}}(t), \qquad \vec{a}(t) = \ddot{\vec{r}}(t), \qquad |\vec{v}| = \text{speed}.$$

Each component is differentiated independently. Centripetal acceleration in curved trajectory: $a_c = v^2/\rho$ (where $\rho$ is the radius of curvature).

Imagine a toy car on a straight track.

**Where is the car?** That's its **position** — which number on the ruler.

**How fast is it going?** That's its **velocity** — how many inches per second.

**Is it speeding up or slowing down?** That's its **acceleration** — is the speed getting bigger or smaller?

All three are connected: velocity is how position changes each second. Acceleration is how velocity changes each second. It's a ladder: position → velocity → acceleration.

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

In physics, time passes and objects move. **Kinematics** studies this using math.

- **Position** $s(t)$: where the object is at any moment.
- **Velocity** $v(t) = s'(t)$: how fast the position changes. It can be negative (moving backward).
- **Acceleration** $a(t) = v'(t)$: how fast the velocity changes. Positive = speeding up; negative = slowing down.

**Example**: A rock dropped from a building. $s(t) = 100 - 5t^2$ meters.
- $v(t) = -10t$ (each second, it gets 10 m/s faster downwards).
- $a(t) = -10$ m/s² (constant: that's gravity!).
- It hits the ground when $s = 0 \Rightarrow t = \sqrt{20} \approx 4.5$ seconds.

Differentiation turns position into velocity, and velocity into acceleration. It's the same operation applied twice.

</Porta>

Kinematics is the **most direct application** of derivatives in physics. The same formulas from calculus describe cars, planets, springs.

### The Three Fundamental Functions

| Quantity | Definition | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Position $s(t)$ | given | where the object is |
| Velocity $v(t) = s'(t)$ | 1st derivative | how fast and in which direction |
| Acceleration $a(t) = s''(t)$ | 2nd derivative | change in velocity |

### Standard Motions

**Uniform Motion (MRU)**: $a = 0$, $v$ constant, $s(t) = s_0 + v_0 t$ (straight line).

**Uniformly Accelerated Motion (MUV)** (free fall, inclined plane): $a$ constant, $s(t) = s_0 + v_0 t + \tfrac{1}{2}at^2$ (parabola).

Torricelli's Theorem: $v^2 = v_0^2 + 2a\Delta s$ — eliminates time when not asked for.

**Simple Harmonic Motion (SHM)** (spring, pendulum): $s(t) = A\cos(\omega t + \phi)$. Acceleration is $a = -\omega^2 s$ — always pointing back to equilibrium. Period $T = 2\pi/\omega$.

### Common Pitfalls

- **Zero velocity** does not mean zero acceleration (a ball at its peak has $v = 0$ but $a = -g$).
- **Displacement** ($\int v\, dt$) can be zero even if distance traveled ($\int |v|\, dt$) is positive.
- Sign indicates direction; speed is the absolute value of velocity.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

Kinematics is the foundation of classical mechanics, control systems, robotics, and numerical simulation.

### Vector Formulation

$\vec{r}(t) \in \mathbb{R}^3$: $\vec{v} = \dot{\vec{r}}$, $\vec{a} = \ddot{\vec{r}}$, $\vec{j} = \dddot{\vec{r}}$.

In Frenet-Serret coordinates: $\vec{a} = \dot{v}\,\hat{T} + (v^2/\rho)\,\hat{N}$ — tangential and centripetal acceleration.

### Uniform Circular Motion

$\vec{r} = R(\cos\omega t, \sin\omega t)$: $\vec{v} = R\omega(-\sin\omega t, \cos\omega t)$, $|\vec{v}| = R\omega$.

$\vec{a} = -\omega^2\vec{r}$ — centripetal acceleration, always directed towards the center, magnitude $a_c = R\omega^2 = v^2/R$.

### ODEs and Phase Space

Kinematics with general force: $m\ddot{s} = F(t, s, \dot{s})$ — a 2nd-order ODE.

Rewritten as a system: $\dot{s} = v$, $\dot{v} = F/m$. The $(s, v)$ space is the **phase space**. Trajectories in phase space never cross (uniqueness of ODE solutions).

### Air Resistance (Drag)

$m\dot{v} = -mg - bv$ (linear drag). Solution: $v(t) = v_\infty + (v_0 - v_\infty)e^{-bt/m}$, where $v_\infty = -mg/b$ is the **terminal velocity**. For quadratic drag ($bv^2$): no simple closed-form solution — numerical integration is required.

### Jerk and Engineering Applications

Jerk is limited in: elevators (comfort), ABS (anti-lock braking), robotic arms (mechanical wear), high-speed train tracks. Modern controllers specify acceleration profiles with maximum jerk.

### Numerical Approximation (Finite Differences)

$v_n \approx (s_{n+1} - s_n)/\Delta t$, $a_n \approx (v_{n+1} - v_n)/\Delta t$. Verlet method: $s_{n+1} = 2s_n - s_{n-1} + a_n (\Delta t)^2$ — stable for conservative systems.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a Senior Professional">

Kinematics is where derivatives transition from formalism to physical measurement.

### Historical Perspective

**Galileo (1638)** in *Discorsi* experimentally deduced that free fall has $s \propto t^2$ — without calculus, using geometric proportions. **Newton (1687)** in *Principia* formalized $F = ma$ and invented calculus to describe it. **Lagrange (1788)** and **Hamilton (1835)** rewrote everything variationally: trajectories are extremals of $\int L\, dt$.

### Special Relativity

For $v \to c$: position and time become intertwined. Proper velocity $u^\mu = dx^\mu/d\tau$ (four-velocity). Conservation of four-momentum $p^\mu = mu^\mu$ generalizes $F = ma$. The "derivative" becomes a covariant derivative with a Christoffel connection.

### Quantum Mechanics

Position $\hat{X}$ and momentum $\hat{P}$ operators obey $[\hat{X}, \hat{P}] = i\hbar$. Heisenberg equation: $d\hat{A}/dt = \frac{i}{\hbar}[\hat{H}, \hat{A}]$ — quantum analog of $\{A, H\}_\text{Poisson}$ from Hamiltonian mechanics.

### Fluid Mechanics

Material derivative $D/Dt = \partial_t + \vec{v}\cdot\nabla$ captures the acceleration of a particle moving with the fluid. Navier-Stokes: $\rho D\vec{v}/Dt = -\nabla p + \mu\nabla^2\vec{v} + \rho\vec{g}$.

### Robotics and Optimal Control

Optimal trajectory problem: minimize $\int_0^T j(t)^2\, dt$ subject to boundary conditions on position and velocity — leads to 5th-degree polynomials (*minimum-jerk trajectory*). Used in industrial manipulators, autonomous vehicles, and surgical arms.

### Nobel and Derivatives

The [1921 Nobel Prize in Physics](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/1921/) was awarded to Einstein for the photoelectric effect, but special relativity (1905) — which redefines derivatives in spacetime — was already established. All relativistic kinematics rests on the differential structure of Minkowski spacetime.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practical Application">

**6-Step Recipe:**

1.  **Identify** $s(t)$ — given directly, or as the integral of $v$.
2.  **Differentiate**: $v = s'$, $a = s'' = v'$.
3.  **Object at Rest**: solve $v(t) = 0$ for $t$.
4.  **Maximum Height/Position**: when $v = 0$ and $a < 0$.
5.  **Distance (not displacement)**: $\int |v|\, dt$ — change sign when $v$ changes sign.
6.  **MUV without Time**: Torricelli's $v^2 = v_0^2 + 2a\Delta s$.

### Cautions

-   **Zero velocity $\neq$ zero acceleration**: a ball at the top has $v = 0$ and $a = -10$ m/s².
-   **Displacement vs. Distance**: a car going 5 km and returning 5 km has zero displacement but 10 km distance.
-   **Sign of $a$**: opposite to the sign of $v$ means braking.

### Real Brazilian Data

**ENEM 2023 — Physics**: Car at $v_0 = 20$ m/s brakes with $a = -4$ m/s². Braking distance:
$v^2 = v_0^2 + 2a\Delta s \Rightarrow 0 = 400 - 8\Delta s \Rightarrow \Delta s = 50$ m.

**0-100 km/h Test**: Fiat Pulse (2023) accelerates from 0-100 km/h in 10.5 s.
$a_{\text{avg}} = \Delta v / \Delta t = (100/3.6)/10.5 \approx 2.6$ m/s².

## Solved Examples

<Exemplo titulo="Cubic Position: Velocity, Stops, and Distance Traveled">

**Problem.** An object has position $s(t) = t^3 - 6t^2 + 9t$ meters, $t \geq 0$. (a) Find $v(t)$ and $a(t)$. (b) When is the object stopped? (c) What is the distance traveled over $[0, 4]$?

**Strategy.** Differentiate $s$ to get $v$ and $a$. Zeros of $v$ give the stopping times. For distance, integrate $|v|$ — changing sign at each zero.

**Solution.**

(a) $v(t) = 3t^2 - 12t + 9 = 3(t-1)(t-3)$. $a(t) = 6t - 12$.

(b) $v = 0 \Rightarrow t = 1$ or $t = 3$.

(c) $v > 0$ on $[0,1)$; $v < 0$ on $(1,3)$; $v > 0$ on $(3,4]$.

$d = |s(1)-s(0)| + |s(3)-s(1)| + |s(4)-s(3)|$

$= |4 - 0| + |0 - 4| + |4 - 0| = 4 + 4 + 4 = 12$ m.

Total displacement: $s(4) - s(0) = 4$ m (much less than the distance!).

**Check.** $v(1) = 3(0)(-2) = 0$ $\checkmark$; $v(3) = 3(2)(0) = 0$ $\checkmark$; $a(1) = -6 < 0$ (braking at $t=1$) $\checkmark$.

**Source.** [Active Calculus §1.1, Exercise 1.1.4](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-1-1-vel.html) — Boelkins · CC-BY-NC-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Free Fall with Initial Velocity: Fall Time and Impact Velocity">

**Problem.** A stone is thrown from a tower of height $h = 80$ m with an initial downward velocity $v_0 = 10$ m/s. Taking $g = 10$ m/s² and the origin at the ground (positive upwards), find: (a) $s(t)$, (b) the impact time $t^*$, (c) the velocity at impact.

**Strategy.** Set up $s(t)$ with initial conditions $s(0) = 80$, $v(0) = -10$ (negative = downward). Solve $s(t^*) = 0$.

**Solution.**

$s(t) = 80 - 10t - 5t^2$.

$s(t^*) = 0 \Rightarrow 5t^2 + 10t - 80 = 0 \Rightarrow t^2 + 2t - 16 = 0$.

$t^* = \dfrac{-2 + \sqrt{4 + 64}}{2} = \dfrac{-2 + \sqrt{68}}{2} \approx \dfrac{-2 + 8.25}{2} \approx 3.12$ s.

$v(t^*) = -10 - 10(3.12) \approx -41.2$ m/s. Speed: $\approx 41.2$ m/s $\approx 148$ km/h.

**Check.** $s(3.12) = 80 - 31.2 - 48.7 \approx 0.1 \approx 0$ $\checkmark$. Torricelli: $v^2 = 100 + 2(10)(80) = 1700 \Rightarrow v \approx 41.2$ m/s $\checkmark$.

**Source.** [OpenStax Calculus Vol. 1 §3.4, Example 3.33](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/3-4-derivatives-as-rates-of-change) — CC-BY-NC-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Simple Harmonic Motion: Amplitude, Period, and Energy">

**Problem.** A mass of $m = 2$ kg is attached to a spring with $k = 50$ N/m, displaced $A = 0.2$ m, and released from rest. (a) Write $x(t)$. (b) Maximum velocity. (c) Show that the mechanical energy $E = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 + \frac{1}{2}kx^2$ is constant.

**Strategy.** $\omega = \sqrt{k/m}$. With $x(0) = A$ and $v(0) = 0$: phase $\phi = 0$. Energy: substitute $x$ and $v$ and simplify using a trigonometric identity.

**Solution.**

$\omega = \sqrt{50/2} = 5$ rad/s. $T = 2\pi/5 \approx 1.26$ s.

$x(t) = 0.2\cos(5t)$.

$v(t) = -0.2 \cdot 5 \sin(5t) = -\sin(5t)$ m/s.

Maximum velocity: $|v|_{\max} = A\omega = 0.2 \cdot 5 = 1$ m/s (at the equilibrium point).

Energy:
$$E = \tfrac{1}{2}(2)(-\sin 5t)^2 + \tfrac{1}{2}(50)(0.2\cos 5t)^2 = \sin^2(5t) + \cos^2(5t) = 1 \text{ J (constant). }\checkmark\text{}$$

**Check.** At $t = 0$: $x = 0.2$, $v = 0$, $E = 0 + \frac{1}{2}(50)(0.04) = 1$ J $\checkmark$.

**Source.** [Active Calculus §1.5, Exercise 1.5.3](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-1-5-units.html) — Boelkins · CC-BY-NC-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Air Resistance: Terminal Velocity and Analytical Solution">

**Problem.** A particle of $m = 1$ kg falls from rest under gravity $g = 10$ m/s² with linear drag $F_{\text{drag}} = bv$, $b = 0.5$ kg/s. (a) Write and solve the ODE for $v(t)$. (b) Terminal velocity. (c) How quickly (in seconds) does $v$ reach 90% of $v_\infty$?

**Strategy.** First-order separable ODE: $m\dot{v} = mg - bv$. Separate variables.

**Solution.**

$\dot{v} = 10 - 0.5v$. Solution: $v(t) = v_\infty(1 - e^{-bt/m})$ with $v_\infty = mg/b = 10/0.5 = 20$ m/s.

$v(t) = 20(1 - e^{-0.5t})$.

90% of $v_\infty$: $20(1 - e^{-0.5 t_{90}}) = 18 \Rightarrow e^{-0.5 t_{90}} = 0.1 \Rightarrow t_{90} = 2\ln(10)/0.5 \approx 4.6$ s.

**Check.** $v(0) = 0$ $\checkmark$. $\lim_{t\to\infty} v(t) = 20$ m/s $\checkmark$. $v'(0) = 20 \cdot 0.5 = 10$ m/s² = $g$ $\checkmark$.

**Source.** [APEX Calculus §2.4, Example 2.4.4](https://www.apexcalculus.com/) — Hartman et al. · CC-BY-NC.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Circular Motion: Centripetal Acceleration via Derivatives">

**Problem.** A point moves around a circle of radius $R = 3$ m with constant angular frequency $\omega = 2$ rad/s. The position is $\vec{r}(t) = 3(\cos 2t, \sin 2t)$. Calculate: (a) $\vec{v}(t)$ and $|\vec{v}|$, (b) $\vec{a}(t)$ and show that $\vec{a} = -\omega^2\vec{r}$.

**Strategy.** Differentiate each component independently. Identify the direction of $\vec{a}$.

**Solution.**

$\vec{v}(t) = 3(-2\sin 2t, 2\cos 2t) = (-6\sin 2t, 6\cos 2t)$.

$|\vec{v}| = 6$ m/s (constant) = $R\omega = 3 \cdot 2$ $\checkmark$.

$\vec{a}(t) = (-12\cos 2t, -12\sin 2t) = -4(3\cos 2t, 3\sin 2t) = -4\vec{r}$.

Since $\omega^2 = 4$: $\vec{a} = -\omega^2\vec{r}$. Magnitude: $|\vec{a}| = 12 = R\omega^2 = 3 \cdot 4$ $\checkmark$.

**Check.** $\vec{v} \cdot \vec{r} = 3 \cdot(-6\sin 2t\cos 2t + 6\cos 2t\sin 2t) = 0$ — velocity perpendicular to position in UCM $\checkmark$.

**Source.** [OpenStax Calculus Vol. 1 §3.4, Example 3.35](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/3-4-derivatives-as-rates-of-change) — CC-BY-NC-SA.

</Exemplo>
