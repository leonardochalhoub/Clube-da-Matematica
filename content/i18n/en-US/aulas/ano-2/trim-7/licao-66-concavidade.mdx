### Concavity and Convexity

<Definicao titulo="Convex Function (Concave Up)">
$f: I \to \mathbb{R}$ is **convex** on $I$ if, for all $x_1, x_2 \in I$ and $t \in [0,1]$:
$$f(tx_1 + (1-t)x_2) \leq t\,f(x_1) + (1-t)\,f(x_2).$$
Geometrically: the line segment between $(x_1, f(x_1))$ and $(x_2, f(x_2))$ lies **above or on** the graph.
</Definicao>

> "The function $f$ is concave up on an interval $I$ if $f''(x) \geq 0$ for all $x \in I$." — [OpenStax Calculus Vol. 1 §4.5](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/4-5-derivatives-and-the-shape-of-a-graph)

**Criterion via Second Derivative:** If $f$ is twice differentiable on $I$:

- $f''(x) \geq 0$ on $I$ $\iff$ $f$ is convex (concave up).
- $f''(x) \leq 0$ on $I$ $\iff$ $f$ is concave (downward).
- $f''(x) > 0$ strictly $\Rightarrow$ strict convexity.

### Inflection Point

<Definicao titulo="Inflection Point">
$x_0$ is an **inflection point** of $f$ if $f$ is continuous at $x_0$ and $f''$ **changes sign** passing through $x_0$.
</Definicao>

**Caution:** $f''(x_0) = 0$ is a necessary but NOT sufficient condition. Canonical counterexample: $f(x) = x^4$ has $f''(0) = 0$ but $f'' \geq 0$ in a neighborhood of $0$ — no sign change, thus $0$ is not an inflection point.

> "If the concavity changes at a point $(x_0, f(x_0))$, we call this a point of inflection. It must be the case that $f''(x_0)$ changes sign." — [APEX Calculus §3.4](https://www.apexcalculus.com/downloads)

### Second Derivative Test for Local Extrema

<Teorema titulo="Second Derivative Test">
Let $f'(x_0) = 0$. Then:
- $f''(x_0) > 0$ $\Rightarrow$ $x_0$ is a **local minimum**.
- $f''(x_0) < 0$ $\Rightarrow$ $x_0$ is a **local maximum**.
- $f''(x_0) = 0$ $\Rightarrow$ **inconclusive** (use higher-order derivatives or the first derivative test).
</Teorema>

**Proof for minimum:** If $f'(x_0) = 0$ and $f''(x_0) > 0$, by continuity of $f''$ there exists a neighborhood where $f''(x) > 0$, hence $f'$ is increasing in this neighborhood. Since $f'(x_0) = 0$, we have $f' < 0$ to the left and $f' > 0$ to the right of $x_0$ — by the first derivative test, $x_0$ is a local minimum. ∎

<Insight titulo="Jensen's Inequality">
$f$ convex $\Rightarrow$ $f\!\left(\frac{x_1 + x_2}{2}\right) \leq \frac{f(x_1) + f(x_2)}{2}$. More generally: for a random variable $X$, $f(E[X]) \leq E[f(X)]$. A cornerstone of information theory, thermodynamics, and ML.
</Insight>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

Get an empty ice cream **bowl** and turn it upside down: you have a curve that dips in the middle (like $\cap$). Now get a normal bowl: a curve that goes up in the middle (like $\cup$).

- The normal bowl is **concave up** — any little drop of ice cream you put in rolls down to the center.
- The upside-down bowl is **concave down** — any little drop will slide right out.

An **inflection point** is that moment when a curve **switches from bowl to cap** — or vice versa. It's where the curve "turns its head upside down" for an instant.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10"titulo="For a 10-year-old">

The **second derivative** $f''$ is the derivative of the derivative — it measures **how the slope is changing**.

- If $f'' > 0$: the slope $f'$ is **increasing**. The graph curves upward, like $\cup$.
- If $f'' < 0$: the slope $f'$ is **decreasing**. The graph curves downward, like $\cap$.

**Example with $f(x) = x^3$:**

$f'(x) = 3x^2$, $f''(x) = 6x$.

- For $x < 0$: $f'' < 0$ — curves downward.
- For $x > 0$: $f'' > 0$ — curves upward.
- At $x = 0$: $f''$ changes sign. **Inflection point!**

**Why does it matter?** It tells you if a point where $f'(x) = 0$ is a maximum (curves downward = cap) or minimum (curves upward = bowl).

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15"titulo="For a 15-year-old">

Concavity completes the "story" of $f'$ in describing the graph's shape:

| Sign of $f'$ | Sign of $f''$ | Behavior |
|---|---|---|
| $+$ | $+$ | increasing and accelerating ($\cup$ going up) |
| $+$ | $-$ | increasing and decelerating |
| $-$ | $+$ | decreasing and decelerating |
| $-$ | $-$ | decreasing and accelerating ($\cap$ going down) |

### Second Derivative Test for Extrema

If $f'(x_0) = 0$:
- $f''(x_0) > 0$: **local minimum** (bowl).
- $f''(x_0) < 0$: **local maximum** (cap).
- $f''(x_0) = 0$: **inconclusive** — do a sign test for $f'$ around the point.

### Inflection: Sign Change, Not Just Zero

An inflection point is where $f''$ **changes sign**. Example: $f(x) = e^{-x^2/2}$ (Gaussian). $f''(x) = (x^2-1)e^{-x^2/2}$. Sign change at $x = \pm 1$. Thus, inflection points at $\mu \pm \sigma$ for a normal distribution with $\sigma = 1$.

### Applications

- **Economics:** profit has a maximum where $\pi'' < 0$. Inflection of $C(q)$ = change in marginal return.
- **Physics:** minimum of potential energy $U$ (with $U'' > 0$) is stable equilibrium. Frequency of small oscillations: $\omega = \sqrt{U''(x^*)/m}$.
- **Statistics:** inflection points of the Gaussian define the "shoulders" of the curve.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25"titulo="For an Engineering Student">

Convexity and concavity are central concepts in optimization, machine learning, and stability analysis.

### Global Convexity and Optimization

$f$ convex on $I$ $\Rightarrow$:
- Every local minimum is **global**.
- The set of minimizers is **convex**.
- Gradient descent algorithms converge to the unique minimum (under mild conditions).

Practical consequence: In linear regression, the loss function $L(\beta) = \|y - X\beta\|^2$ is quadratically convex (Hessian $H = 2X^TX$ is positive semi-definite) — thus $\nabla L = 0$ yields a unique global solution: $\hat\beta = (X^TX)^{-1}X^Ty$.

### Canonical Convex Functions

- $x^2$, $e^x$, $-\ln x$ (on $(0,\infty)$), $x\ln x$, $\max(0, x)$ (ReLU).
- Sum of convex functions is convex. Pointwise maximum of convex functions is convex.
- $h(g(x))$ is convex if $h$ is convex and increasing, and $g$ is convex.

### Sigmoid: Non-convex, Inflection at 0

$\sigma(x) = 1/(1 + e^{-x})$ has $\sigma''(x) = \sigma(x)(1 - \sigma(x))(1 - 2\sigma(x))$. Changes sign at $x = 0$ — an inflection point. Neural networks with sigmoid are **non-convex** in parameter space, making global optimization intractable (hence the appeal of ReLU, which maintains piecewise convexity).

### Jensen's Inequality — ML Application

$f$ convex $\Rightarrow$ $E[f(X)] \geq f(E[X])$. With $f = -\ln$: $E[-\ln X] \geq -\ln E[X]$, the basis of entropy. With $f = -\ln$ and $X$ a probability distribution: derivation of the ELBO (Evidence Lower Bound) in VAEs.

### Hessian and Optimization Landscape

The Hessian $H(x)$ at a critical point: positive eigenvalues = local minimum, negative = maximum, mixed = saddle point. In deep neural networks, critical points are almost all **saddles**, not local minima — implications for SGD convergence.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40"titulo="For a Senior Professional">

Convexity is the heart of modern optimization, with profound ramifications in geometry, probability, and statistical physics.

### Convex Analysis (Rockafellar, 1970)

Core concepts:
- **Subdifferential** $\partial f(x)$: generalizes $\nabla f$ for convex non-differentiable functions (e.g., $|x|$).
- **Fenchel Conjugate:** $f^*(p) = \sup_x(p\cdot x - f(x))$. Fundamental duality: $(f^*)^* = f$ (under semicontinuity).
- **Support Function** $\sigma_C(x) = \sup_{y \in C} \langle x, y\rangle$ characterizes convex sets — basis of linear programming.

### Differential Geometry

The Hessian defines the second-order local shape: its signature (positive definite, indefinite, etc.) determines extrema vs. saddles. On Riemannian manifolds, **sectional curvature** generalizes $f''$: positive = ball, negative = hyperbolic plane. Ricci curvature appears in general relativity.

### Statistical Mechanics and Phase Transitions

Free energy $F(\beta) = -\ln Z(\beta)$ is **convex** in $\beta = 1/T$ — implies specific heat $C_V = k_B\beta^2\partial_\beta^2 \ln Z \geq 0$ (second law). Inflection points of $F(T)$ mark second-order phase transitions (Landau, 1937).

### Convex Optimization Theory

Convex problems are solvable in polynomial time (Khachiyan 1979 — ellipsoid; Karmarkar 1984 — interior point). Hierarchy: LP ⊂ QP ⊂ SOCP ⊂ SDP. Libraries: CVXPY, MOSEK, Gurobi.

### ML: Loss Landscape and Generalization

Keskar et al. (2017) show that "flat" minima (small Hessian eigenvalues) generalize better than "sharp" minima. Intuition: a flat minimum is more robust to training set perturbations — corresponds to $f'' \ll 1$ around the minimum. Active research area in 2024–2026.

### Historical Connection

Hadamard (1893) uses $f''$ for a rigorous extrema criterion. Minkowski (1896) on convex geometry. Carathéodory, Fenchel, Moreau consolidate convex analysis in the 20th century. Boyd & Vandenberghe (2004) make it canonical in engineering with the book *Convex Optimization* (freely available at [web.stanford.edu/~boyd/cvxbook](https://web.stanford.edu/~boyd/cvxbook/)).

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica"titulo="Practical Application">

**5-Step Recipe:**

1. **Calculate $f''(x)$.**
2. **Set to zero** and find candidates for inflection.
3. **Test the sign** of $f''$ in each subinterval. Sign change = inflection.
4. **Classify critical points** of $f$ ($f' = 0$): sign of $f''$ determines minimum, maximum, or inconclusive test.
5. **Assemble concavity table** + sketch.

### Standard Table

| Interval | Sign of $f''$ | Concavity | Visual Behavior |
|---|---|---|---|
| $(-\infty, x_0)$ | $+$ | up | $\cup$ |
| $(x_0, +\infty)$ | $-$ | down | $\cap$ |

Sign change at $x_0$ confirms inflection.

### Python/SymPy Verification

```python
import sympy as sp
x = sp.symbols('x')
f = x**3 - 3*x
f2 = sp.diff(f, x, 2)
inflexoes = sp.solve(f2, x)  # candidates
print("f'' =", f2, "| candidates:", inflexoes)
# Check sign around each candidate
```

### Application: Decreasing Marginal Cost

Production cost with diminishing returns: $C(q) = q^3 - 6q^2 + 15q + 100$. $C'' = 6q - 12 = 0$ at $q = 2$. For $q < 2$: $C'' < 0$ (decreasing marginal cost, economies of scale). For $q > 2$: $C'' > 0$ (increasing marginal cost, capacity pressure). Inflection at $q = 2$ = point of **production regime change**.

## Solved Examples

<Exemplo titulo="Concavity and Inflection of f(x) = x^3 - 3x (Basic)" numero="1">

**Problem.** For $f(x) = x^3 - 3x$, determine the intervals of concavity and the inflection points.

**Strategy.** Calculate $f''$, determine where it is positive/negative, and check for sign changes at zeros.

**Solution.**

1. **Derivatives:** $f'(x) = 3x^2 - 3$, $f''(x) = 6x$.
2. **Zero of $f''$:** $6x = 0 \Rightarrow x = 0$.
3. **Sign:** $f''(x) < 0$ for $x < 0$ (concave down); $f''(x) > 0$ for $x > 0$ (concave up).
4. **Inflection:** $f''$ changes sign at $x = 0$. Thus $(0, f(0)) = (0, 0)$ is an inflection point.

**Verification.** In the sketch: $f(-2) = -8 + 6 = -2$; $f(0) = 0$; $f(2) = 8 - 6 = 2$. The curve rises from $-2$ to $0$ concave down, then continues from $0$ to $2$ concave up — consistent. $\checkmark$

**Source.** [APEX Calculus §3.4, Example 3.4.1](https://www.apexcalculus.com/downloads) — CC-BY-NC.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Second Derivative Test for Extrema of f(x) = x^4 - 4x^2 (Intermediate)" numero="2">

**Problem.** Classify the critical points of $f(x) = x^4 - 4x^2$ using the second derivative test.

**Strategy.** Find critical points via $f' = 0$, calculate $f''$ at each, and apply the criterion.

**Solution.**

1. **$f'(x) = 4x^3 - 8x = 4x(x^2 - 2) = 0 \Rightarrow x = 0,\, x = \pm\sqrt{2}$.**
2. **$f''(x) = 12x^2 - 8$.**
3. **Evaluating:**
   - $f''(0) = -8 < 0$ $\Rightarrow$ **local maximum** at $x = 0$. $f(0) = 0$.
   - $f''(\sqrt{2}) = 24 - 8 = 16 > 0$ $\Rightarrow$ **local minimum** at $x = \sqrt{2}$. $f(\sqrt{2}) = 4 - 8 = -4$.
   - $f''(-\sqrt{2}) = 16 > 0$ $\Rightarrow$ **local minimum** at $x = -\sqrt{2}$. $f(-\sqrt{2}) = -4$.

**Verification.** As $f(x) \to +\infty$ for $x \to \pm\infty$, the minima at $\pm\sqrt{2}$ are global, and the maximum at $0$ is local (but not global). $\checkmark$

**Source.** [OpenStax Calculus Vol. 1 §4.5, Example 4.38](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/4-5-derivatives-and-the-shape-of-a-graph) — CC-BY-NC-SA.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Inflection Points of the Gaussian (Intermediate)" numero="3">

**Problem.** Find the inflection points of $f(x) = e^{-x^2/2}$ (the kernel of the normal distribution).

**Strategy.** Differentiate twice, set $f'' = 0$, and check for sign changes.

**Solution.**

1. **$f'(x) = -x\,e^{-x^2/2}$.**
2. **$f''(x) = -e^{-x^2/2} + x^2 e^{-x^2/2} = (x^2 - 1)e^{-x^2/2}$.**
3. **Zero of $f''$:** $x^2 - 1 = 0 \Rightarrow x = \pm 1$.
4. **Sign:**
   - $|x| > 1$: $x^2 - 1 > 0 \Rightarrow f'' > 0$ (concave up).
   - $|x| < 1$: $x^2 - 1 < 0 \Rightarrow f'' < 0$ (concave down).
5. **Inflection points:** at $x = \pm 1$, $f''$ changes sign. Points: $(\pm 1, e^{-1/2}) = (\pm 1, \approx 0.607)$.

**Verification.** For $\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$, inflection points are at $\mu \pm \sigma$. Here $\mu = 0$, $\sigma = 1$ — consistent. $\checkmark$

**Source.** [Active Calculus §3.1, Activity 3.1.3](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-3-1-tests.html) — CC-BY-NC-SA.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Inconclusive Case: f'' = 0 at Critical Point is Not Inflection (Advanced)" numero="4">

**Problem.** For $f(x) = x^4$, verify that $f''(0) = 0$ but $x = 0$ is a local minimum (not an inflection point).

**Strategy.** Show that $f''$ does not change sign at $x = 0$.

**Solution.**

1. **$f'(x) = 4x^3 = 0 \Rightarrow x = 0$** (critical point).
2. **$f''(x) = 12x^2 \geq 0$ for all $x$.** In particular $f''(0) = 0$ — test is inconclusive.
3. **But $f''(x) \geq 0$ in a neighborhood of $0$**: no sign change.
4. **Conclusion:** $x = 0$ is NOT an inflection point. It is a global minimum ($f(x) = x^4 \geq 0$ for all $x$, with equality only at $0$).
5. **Confirmation by $f'$ sign test:** $f'(x) = 4x^3 < 0$ for $x < 0$ and $f'(x) > 0$ for $x > 0$ — minimum confirmed.

**Verification.** The graph of $x^4$ is a parabola "flattened" at the bottom at $0$ — visually a minimum. $\checkmark$

**Source.** [APEX Calculus §3.4, Note after Example 3.4.2](https://www.apexcalculus.com/downloads) — CC-BY-NC.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Decreasing Returns Cost — Economic Inflection (Modeling)" numero="5">

**Problem.** A company has production cost $C(q) = q^3 - 6q^2 + 15q + 100$ (in dollars). Find the inflection point and interpret it economically.

**Strategy.** Calculate $C''$, find the inflection point, and analyze the behavior of the marginal cost $MC = C'$.

**Solution.**

1. **$C'(q) = 3q^2 - 12q + 15 = MC(q)$** (marginal cost).
2. **$C''(q) = 6q - 12$.**
3. **Inflection:** $C''(q) = 0 \Rightarrow q = 2$.
4. **Sign of $C''$:** for $q < 2$, $C'' < 0$ (decreasing marginal cost — economies of scale). For $q > 2$, $C'' > 0$ (increasing marginal cost — capacity pressure).
5. **Inflection point:** $C(2) = 8 - 24 + 30 + 100 = 114$. Point $(2, 114)$.

**Verification.** $MC(0) = 15$, $MC(2) = 12 - 24 + 15 = 3$, $MC(4) = 48 - 48 + 15 = 15$. Minimum $MC$ at $q = 2$ — consistent with inflection of $C$. $\checkmark$

**Source.** [Active Calculus §3.1, Exercise 3.1.5](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-3-1-tests.html) — CC-BY-NC-SA.
</Exemplo>
