<Porta nivel="formal" titulo="Definitions, Theorems, and Algorithms">

### Local and Absolute Extrema

<Definicao titulo="Local Maximum and Minimum">

Let <Eq>{`f`}</Eq> be defined on a domain <Eq>{`D`}</Eq> and <Eq>{`c \\in D`}</Eq>.

- <Eq>{`c`}</Eq> is a **local maximum** of <Eq>{`f`}</Eq> if there exists <Eq>{`\\delta > 0`}</Eq> such that <Eq>{`f(c) \\geq f(x)`}</Eq> for all <Eq>{`x \\in D`}</Eq> with <Eq>{`|x - c| < \\delta`}</Eq>.
- <Eq>{`c`}</Eq> is a **local minimum** of <Eq>{`f`}</Eq> if there exists <Eq>{`\\delta > 0`}</Eq> such that <Eq>{`f(c) \\leq f(x)`}</Eq> for all <Eq>{`x \\in D`}</Eq> with <Eq>{`|x - c| < \\delta`}</Eq>.
- **Absolute (global) extremum**: <Eq>{`f(c) \\geq f(x)`}</Eq> (or <Eq>{`\\leq`}</Eq>) for **all** <Eq>{`x \\in D`}</Eq>.

</Definicao>

<Definicao titulo="Critical Point">

<Eq>{`c`}</Eq> is a **critical point** of <Eq>{`f`}</Eq> if:
1. <Eq>{`c`}</Eq> is in the interior of the domain, **and**
2. <Eq>{`f'(c) = 0`}</Eq> or <Eq>{`f'(c)`}</Eq> does not exist.

</Definicao>

> "If f has a local maximum or minimum at c, then c is a critical point of f." — [Active Calculus §3.1](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-3-1-tests.html)

<Teorema titulo="Fermat's Theorem">

If <Eq>{`f`}</Eq> has a local extremum at <Eq>{`c`}</Eq> (interior to the domain) and <Eq>{`f`}</Eq> is differentiable at <Eq>{`c`}</Eq>, then <Eq display>{`f'(c) = 0.`}</Eq>

<strong>Caution — the converse is false.</strong> <Eq>{`f'(c) = 0`}</Eq> does not imply an extremum: the function <Eq>{`f(x) = x^3`}</Eq> has <Eq>{`f'(0) = 0`}</Eq>, but <Eq>{`c = 0`}</Eq> is an inflection point, not an extremum.

</Teorema>

<Insight>

Fermat's Theorem states: every local extremum (interior, differentiable) is a critical point. But not every critical point is an extremum. A critical point is a **candidate** — the tests below decide.

</Insight>

### First Derivative Test

<Definicao titulo="Sign Change Criterion">

Let <Eq>{`c`}</Eq> be a critical point of a continuous function <Eq>{`f`}</Eq>. For <Eq>{`x`}</Eq> near <Eq>{`c`}</Eq>:

- <Eq>{`f'`}</Eq> changes from <Eq>{`+`}</Eq> to <Eq>{`-`}</Eq> at <Eq>{`c`}</Eq> → **local maximum**.
- <Eq>{`f'`}</Eq> changes from <Eq>{`-`}</Eq> to <Eq>{`+`}</Eq> at <Eq>{`c`}</Eq> → **local minimum**.
- <Eq>{`f'`}</Eq> does not change sign at <Eq>{`c`}</Eq> → **neither maximum nor minimum** (inflection point).

</Definicao>

### Second Derivative Test

<Definicao titulo="Concavity Criterion">

Let <Eq>{`c`}</Eq> be a critical point with <Eq>{`f'(c) = 0`}</Eq> and <Eq>{`f''(c)`}</Eq> existing:

- <Eq>{`f''(c) > 0`}</Eq> → **local minimum** (parabola opens upward).
- <Eq>{`f''(c) < 0`}</Eq> → **local maximum** (parabola opens downward).
- <Eq>{`f''(c) = 0`}</Eq> → **inconclusive** (use the first derivative test).

</Definicao>

> "When the second derivative test is inconclusive, we resort to the first derivative test." — [OpenStax Calculus Vol. 1, §4.5](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/4-5-derivatives-and-the-shape-of-a-graph)

### Extreme Value Theorem (Weierstrass)

<Teorema titulo="Absolute Extrema on a Closed Interval">

If <Eq>{`f`}</Eq> is continuous on <Eq>{`[a, b]`}</Eq>, then <Eq>{`f`}</Eq> attains its absolute maximum value and its absolute minimum value at some point in <Eq>{`[a, b]`}</Eq>.

</Teorema>

### Algorithm for Absolute Extrema on [a, b]

<Definicao titulo="Closed Interval Method">

1. Find all critical points of <Eq>{`f`}</Eq> in the interior <Eq>{`(a, b)`}</Eq>.
2. Evaluate <Eq>{`f`}</Eq> at each critical point and at the endpoints <Eq>{`a`}</Eq> and <Eq>{`b`}</Eq>.
3. The **largest value** is the absolute maximum; the **smallest value** is the absolute minimum.

</Definicao>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

Imagine a **roller coaster**. It has places that are the **tops** (highest spots nearby) and places that are the **bottoms** (lowest spots nearby).

At the top of a hill, if you look around, you see everything else is lower. At the bottom of a valley, everything around is higher.

The special trick: **at the exact moment you are at the very top or bottom, the track is completely level**—no slope at all. This helps us find these special places!

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

When you draw the graph of a function, it goes up and down. The highest points are **maxima** and the lowest points are **minima**.

**How to find them?** At the top of a hill or the bottom of a valley, the slope of the graph is **zero**—the tangent line is horizontal. So:

1. Calculate <Eq>{`f'(x)`}</Eq>.
2. Solve <Eq>{`f'(x) = 0`}</Eq>.
3. See if the function goes up before and down after (maximum), or down before and up after (minimum).

Example: <Eq>{`f(x) = x^2`}</Eq>. Derivative: <Eq>{`f'(x) = 2x`}</Eq>. Setting to zero: <Eq>{`x = 0`}</Eq>. Since <Eq>{`f'`}</Eq> changes from negative to positive at <Eq>{`x = 0`}</Eq>, it's a **minimum**—and indeed, <Eq>{`x^2`}</Eq> has its lowest value at <Eq>{`x = 0`}</Eq>.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For the high school student">

**The central problem:** given a graph, where are the peaks and valleys?

### Step-by-step

1. **Differentiate:** calculate <Eq>{`f'(x)`}</Eq>.
2. **Critical points:** solve <Eq>{`f'(x) = 0`}</Eq> and mark where <Eq>{`f'`}</Eq> does not exist.
3. **Classify with the 1st test:** set up a sign table for <Eq>{`f'`}</Eq>.
   - <Eq>{`(+)`}</Eq> before, <Eq>{`(-)`}</Eq> after → local maximum.
   - <Eq>{`(-)`}</Eq> before, <Eq>{`(+)`}</Eq> after → local minimum.
4. (Quick alternative) **2nd test:** calculate <Eq>{`f''(c)`}</Eq> at the critical point.
   - <Eq>{`f''(c) < 0`}</Eq> → maximum. <Eq>{`f''(c) > 0`}</Eq> → minimum. <Eq>{`f''(c) = 0`}</Eq> → use the 1st test.

**Absolute extremum on** <Eq>{`[a, b]`}</Eq>**:** evaluate <Eq>{`f`}</Eq> at the critical points and the endpoints. The largest value wins; the smallest wins.

<strong>Caution:</strong> <Eq>{`f'(c) = 0`}</Eq> **does not guarantee** an extremum. For <Eq>{`f(x) = x^3`}</Eq>, we have <Eq>{`f'(0) = 0`}</Eq> but <Eq>{`x = 0`}</Eq> is an inflection point—the function doesn't change monotonicity there.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For the engineering student">

### Formal Foundations

<strong>Fermat's Theorem</strong> guarantees that interior local extrema are critical points when <Eq>{`f`}</Eq> is differentiable. The converse fails: <Eq>{`x^3`}</Eq> shows that <Eq>{`f'(c) = 0`}</Eq> can occur at an inflection point without a change in monotonicity.

The <strong>Extreme Value Theorem</strong> (Weierstrass) guarantees the existence of absolute extrema when <Eq>{`f`}</Eq> is continuous on <Eq>{`[a, b]`}</Eq>—but it doesn't provide their location. The algorithm for absolute extrema:

1. Interior critical points (where <Eq>{`f' = 0`}</Eq> or does not exist).
2. Endpoints <Eq>{`a, b`}</Eq>.
3. Evaluate <Eq>{`f`}</Eq> at all these points. The absolute maximum and minimum are the extreme values in the resulting set.

### Tests

<strong>1st derivative test:</strong> analyzes the sign change of <Eq>{`f'`}</Eq> around <Eq>{`c`}</Eq>. More general—works even when <Eq>{`f''(c)`}</Eq> does not exist or is zero.

<strong>2nd derivative test:</strong> at <Eq>{`c`}</Eq> where <Eq>{`f'(c) = 0`}</Eq>: if <Eq>{`f''(c) > 0`}</Eq>, the function has positive curvature (concave up) → local minimum. If <Eq>{`f''(c) < 0`}</Eq>, concave down → local maximum. The case <Eq>{`f''(c) = 0`}</Eq> is inconclusive: it could be an inflection point (<Eq>{`x^3`}</Eq>), a minimum (<Eq>{`x^4`}</Eq>), or a maximum (<Eq>{`-x^4`}</Eq>).

<strong>Connection to calculus of variations:</strong> the Euler-Lagrange condition, a cornerstone of analytical mechanics and optimal control, is the functional generalization of <Eq>{`f'(c) = 0`}</Eq>.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For the senior professional">

The problem of locating extrema is as old as calculus itself. Fermat, two decades before Newton and Leibniz formalized calculus, already used the method of "adequality" to find maxima and minima—substituting <Eq>{`x`}</Eq> with <Eq>{`x + e`}</Eq>, equating it to the original value, and letting <Eq>{`e \\to 0`}</Eq>. This is the intuition of the derivative before the derivative existed as a concept.

The Extreme Value Theorem, formalized by Weierstrass in the 19th century, required the completeness of the real numbers—a property Cantor would only axiomatize decades later. Without completeness, a continuum with holes might have a continuous function that never reaches its supremum.

In applied optimization: engineers, economists, and data scientists work with generalizations—Lagrange multipliers for constraints, KKT (Karush-Kuhn-Tucker) conditions for nonlinear programming, and zero gradient in high dimensions. The intuition from this lesson—"candidates are points where the gradient vanishes"—is universal. The challenge in high dimensions is distinguishing maxima, minima, and saddle points (where <Eq>{`\\nabla f = 0`}</Eq> but it's neither a maximum nor a minimum).

In machine learning, gradient descent avoids calculating critical points directly: it iterates in the direction of <Eq>{`-\\nabla f`}</Eq>, converging (we hope) to a local minimum. The theory behind it—learning rates, convergence—is a direct application of what is learned in this lesson.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Brazilian Practical Application">

<strong>Maximum profit for a bakery.</strong> A bakery produces <Eq>{`x`}</Eq> hundred units of bread per day. The daily profit (in reais) is modeled by:

<Eq display>{`L(x) = -2x^2 + 12x - 10, \quad x \in [0, 5]`}</Eq>

<strong>Step 1 — critical point:</strong> <Eq>{`L'(x) = -4x + 12 = 0 \\Rightarrow x = 3`}</Eq>.

<strong>Step 2 — compare:</strong> <Eq>{`L(0) = -10`}</Eq>, <Eq>{`L(3) = -18 + 36 - 10 = 8`}</Eq>, <Eq>{`L(5) = -50 + 60 - 10 = 0`}</Eq>.

<strong>Conclusion:</strong> maximum profit of R\$ 800/day producing 300 units. Below 100 units (x less than 1) or above 500 units (x greater than 5), the operation results in a loss.

<strong>Connection to ENEM:</strong> optimization problems appear in the ENEM with quadratic models. The algorithm here—differentiate, set to zero, compare with domain endpoints—is exactly the method required.

</Porta>

## Solved Examples

<Exemplo titulo="Critical Points of a Cubic Polynomial">

<strong>Problem.</strong> Find all critical points of <Eq>{`f(x) = x^3 - 3x^2 - 9x + 5`}</Eq> and classify them.

<strong>Strategy.</strong> Differentiate, set to zero, and use the second derivative test.

<strong>Solution.</strong>

<Eq display>{`f'(x) = 3x^2 - 6x - 9 = 3(x^2 - 2x - 3) = 3(x-3)(x+1)`}</Eq>

Critical points: <Eq>{`x = 3`}</Eq> and <Eq>{`x = -1`}</Eq>.

Second derivative: <Eq>{`f''(x) = 6x - 6`}</Eq>.

- <Eq>{`f''(-1) = -12 < 0`}</Eq> → **local maximum** at <Eq>{`x = -1`}</Eq>, with <Eq>{`f(-1) = -1 - 3 + 9 + 5 = 10`}</Eq>.
- <Eq>{`f''(3) = 12 > 0`}</Eq> → **local minimum** at <Eq>{`x = 3`}</Eq>, with <Eq>{`f(3) = 27 - 27 - 27 + 5 = -22`}</Eq>.

<strong>Verification.</strong> Sign table for <Eq>{`f'`}</Eq>: for <Eq>{`x < -1`}</Eq>, <Eq>{`f' > 0`}</Eq> (increasing). For <Eq>{`-1 < x < 3`}</Eq>, <Eq>{`f' < 0`}</Eq> (decreasing). For <Eq>{`x > 3`}</Eq>, <Eq>{`f' > 0`}</Eq> (increasing). Consistent with a maximum at <Eq>{`-1`}</Eq> and a minimum at <Eq>{`3`}</Eq>.

<strong>Source.</strong> [Active Calculus §3.1, Activity 3.1.2](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-3-1-tests.html) · CC-BY-NC-SA

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Absolute Extrema on a Closed Interval">

<strong>Problem.</strong> Determine the absolute extrema of <Eq>{`f(x) = x^3 - 6x^2 + 9x + 2`}</Eq> on <Eq>{`[0, 4]`}</Eq>.

<strong>Strategy.</strong> Algorithm for absolute extrema: interior critical points + endpoints.

<strong>Solution.</strong>

<Eq display>{`f'(x) = 3x^2 - 12x + 9 = 3(x^2 - 4x + 3) = 3(x-1)(x-3)`}</Eq>

Critical points in <Eq>{`(0,4)`}</Eq>: <Eq>{`x = 1`}</Eq> and <Eq>{`x = 3`}</Eq>.

Evaluation:

- <Eq>{`f(0) = 2`}</Eq>
- <Eq>{`f(1) = 1 - 6 + 9 + 2 = 6`}</Eq>
- <Eq>{`f(3) = 27 - 54 + 27 + 2 = 2`}</Eq>
- <Eq>{`f(4) = 64 - 96 + 36 + 2 = 6`}</Eq>

<strong>Absolute maximum:</strong> <Eq>{`f = 6`}</Eq>, attained at <Eq>{`x = 1`}</Eq> and <Eq>{`x = 4`}</Eq>. <strong>Absolute minimum:</strong> <Eq>{`f = 2`}</Eq>, attained at <Eq>{`x = 0`}</Eq> and <Eq>{`x = 3`}</Eq>.

<strong>Verification.</strong> Extrema at endpoints are valid—the algorithm necessarily includes <Eq>{`a`}</Eq> and <Eq>{`b`}</Eq>.

<strong>Source.</strong> [OpenStax Calculus Vol. 1, §4.3, Example 4.13](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/4-3-maxima-and-minima) · CC-BY-NC-SA

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Critical Point Without an Extremum (Inflection Point)">

<strong>Problem.</strong> Show that <Eq>{`f(x) = x^3`}</Eq> has a critical point at <Eq>{`x = 0`}</Eq>, but this point is not a local extremum.

<strong>Strategy.</strong> Apply the first derivative test to check for sign changes.

<strong>Solution.</strong>

<Eq display>{`f'(x) = 3x^2`}</Eq>

<Eq>{`f'(0) = 0`}</Eq> → <Eq>{`x = 0`}</Eq> is a critical point.

For <Eq>{`x < 0`}</Eq>: <Eq>{`3x^2 > 0`}</Eq>. For <Eq>{`x > 0`}</Eq>: <Eq>{`3x^2 > 0`}</Eq>. The sign of <Eq>{`f'`}</Eq> **does not change** → no local extremum.

<strong>Verification.</strong> <Eq>{`f(-1) = -1 < f(0) = 0 < f(1) = 1`}</Eq>: the function is strictly increasing in any neighborhood of <Eq>{`0`}</Eq>, confirming it is neither a maximum nor a minimum.

<strong>Source.</strong> [APEX Calculus §3.1, Example 3.1.3](https://www.apexcalculus.com/) · CC-BY-NC

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="First Derivative Test with Square Root">

<strong>Problem.</strong> Find the local extrema of <Eq>{`f(x) = x^{2/3}`}</Eq>.

<strong>Strategy.</strong> The derivative does not exist at <Eq>{`x = 0`}</Eq>; this creates a critical point even without <Eq>{`f' = 0`}</Eq>.

<strong>Solution.</strong>

<Eq display>{`f'(x) = \\frac{2}{3}x^{-1/3} = \\frac{2}{3\\sqrt[3]{x}}`}</Eq>

<Eq>{`f'(0)`}</Eq> does not exist (zero denominator), and <Eq>{`f(0) = 0`}</Eq> exists → <Eq>{`x = 0`}</Eq> is a critical point.

Sign table: for <Eq>{`x < 0`}</Eq>, <Eq>{`x^{1/3} < 0`}</Eq>, so <Eq>{`f' < 0`}</Eq>. For <Eq>{`x > 0`}</Eq>, <Eq>{`f' > 0`}</Eq>. Sign change <Eq>{`(-) \\to (+)`}</Eq> at <Eq>{`x = 0`}</Eq> → **local minimum**.

<strong>Verification.</strong> <Eq>{`f(x) = x^{2/3} \\geq 0`}</Eq> for all <Eq>{`x`}</Eq>, and <Eq>{`f(0) = 0`}</Eq> → also an absolute minimum.

<strong>Source.</strong> [Active Calculus §3.1, Exercise 3.1.3](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-3-1-tests.html) · CC-BY-NC-SA

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Extrema of Trigonometric Function on Closed Interval">

<strong>Problem.</strong> Determine the absolute extrema of <Eq>{`f(x) = 2\\sin x - x`}</Eq> on <Eq>{`[0, 2\\pi]`}</Eq>.

<strong>Strategy.</strong> Differentiate, find critical points (roots of cosine), apply the algorithm.

<strong>Solution.</strong>

<Eq display>{`f'(x) = 2\\cos x - 1 = 0 \\implies \\cos x = \\tfrac{1}{2} \\implies x = \\tfrac{\\pi}{3},\\; x = \\tfrac{5\\pi}{3}`}</Eq>

Evaluation at critical points and endpoints:

- <Eq>{`f(0) = 0`}</Eq>
- <Eq>{`f(\\pi/3) = 2 \\cdot (\\sqrt{3}/2) - \\pi/3 = \\sqrt{3} - \\pi/3 \\approx 0.685`}</Eq>
- <Eq>{`f(5\\pi/3) = 2 \\cdot (-\\sqrt{3}/2) - 5\\pi/3 = -\\sqrt{3} - 5\\pi/3 \\approx -6.97`}</Eq>
- <Eq>{`f(2\\pi) = 0 - 2\\pi \\approx -6.28`}</Eq>

<strong>Absolute maximum:</strong> <Eq>{`f(\\pi/3) = \\sqrt{3} - \\pi/3`}</Eq>. <strong>Absolute minimum:</strong> <Eq>{`f(5\\pi/3) = -\\sqrt{3} - 5\\pi/3`}</Eq>.

<strong>Verification.</strong> Sign of <Eq>{`f'`}</Eq>: positive on <Eq>{`(0, \\pi/3)`}</Eq>, negative on <Eq>{`(\\pi/3, 5\\pi/3)`}</Eq>, positive on <Eq>{`(5\\pi/3, 2\\pi)`}</Eq>. Maximum and minimum confirmed.

<strong>Source.</strong> [OpenStax Calculus Vol. 1, §4.3, Example 4.14](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/4-3-maxima-and-minima) · CC-BY-NC-SA

</Exemplo>
