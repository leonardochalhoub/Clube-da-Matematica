<Porta nivel="formal" titulo="Definitions, theorems, and algorithms">

### Local and absolute extrema

<Definicao titulo="Local maximum and minimum">

Let <Eq>{`f`}</Eq> be defined on a domain <Eq>{`D`}</Eq> and <Eq>{`c \\in D`}</Eq>.

- <Eq>{`c`}</Eq> is a **local maximum** of <Eq>{`f`}</Eq> if there exists <Eq>{`\\delta > 0`}</Eq> such that <Eq>{`f(c) \\geq f(x)`}</Eq> for all <Eq>{`x \\in D`}</Eq> with <Eq>{`|x - c| < \\delta`}</Eq>.
- <Eq>{`c`}</Eq> is a **local minimum** of <Eq>{`f`}</Eq> if there exists <Eq>{`\\delta > 0`}</Eq> such that <Eq>{`f(c) \\leq f(x)`}</Eq> for all <Eq>{`x \\in D`}</Eq> with <Eq>{`|x - c| < \\delta`}</Eq>.
- **Absolute (global) extremum**: <Eq>{`f(c) \\geq f(x)`}</Eq> (or <Eq>{`\\leq`}</Eq>) for **all** <Eq>{`x \\in D`}</Eq>.

</Definicao>

<Definicao titulo="Critical point">

<Eq>{`c`}</Eq> is a **critical point** of <Eq>{`f`}</Eq> if:
1. <Eq>{`c`}</Eq> is interior to the domain, **and**
2. <Eq>{`f'(c) = 0`}</Eq> or <Eq>{`f'(c)`}</Eq> does not exist.

</Definicao>

> "If f has a local maximum or minimum at c, then c is a critical point of f." — [Active Calculus §3.1](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-3-3-tests.html)

<Teorema titulo="Fermat's Theorem">

If <Eq>{`f`}</Eq> has a local extremum at <Eq>{`c`}</Eq> (interior to the domain) and <Eq>{`f`}</Eq> is differentiable at <Eq>{`c`}</Eq>, then <Eq display>{`f'(c) = 0.`}</Eq>

**Caution — the converse is false.** <Eq>{`f'(c) = 0`}</Eq> does not imply an extremum: the function <Eq>{`f(x) = x^3`}</Eq> has <Eq>{`f'(0) = 0`}</Eq>, but <Eq>{`c = 0`}</Eq> is an inflection point, not an extremum.

</Teorema>

<Insight>

Fermat says: every local extremum (interior, differentiable) is a critical point. But not every critical point is an extremum. The critical point is a **candidate** — the tests below decide.

</Insight>

### First derivative test

<Definicao titulo="Sign-change criterion">

Let <Eq>{`c`}</Eq> be a critical point of continuous <Eq>{`f`}</Eq>. For <Eq>{`x`}</Eq> near <Eq>{`c`}</Eq>:

- <Eq>{`f'`}</Eq> changes from <Eq>{`+`}</Eq> to <Eq>{`-`}</Eq> at <Eq>{`c`}</Eq> → **local maximum**.
- <Eq>{`f'`}</Eq> changes from <Eq>{`-`}</Eq> to <Eq>{`+`}</Eq> at <Eq>{`c`}</Eq> → **local minimum**.
- <Eq>{`f'`}</Eq> does not change sign at <Eq>{`c`}</Eq> → **neither maximum nor minimum** (inflection point).

</Definicao>

### Second derivative test

<Definicao titulo="Concavity criterion">

Let <Eq>{`c`}</Eq> be a critical point with <Eq>{`f'(c) = 0`}</Eq> and <Eq>{`f''(c)`}</Eq> existing:

- <Eq>{`f''(c) > 0`}</Eq> → **local minimum** (parabola opening upward).
- <Eq>{`f''(c) < 0`}</Eq> → **local maximum** (parabola opening downward).
- <Eq>{`f''(c) = 0`}</Eq> → **inconclusive** (use the first derivative test).

</Definicao>

> "When the second derivative test is inconclusive, we resort to the first derivative test." — [OpenStax Calculus Vol. 1, §4.5](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/4-5-derivatives-and-the-shape-of-a-graph)

### Extreme Value Theorem (Weierstrass)

<Teorema titulo="Absolute extremum on a closed interval">

If <Eq>{`f`}</Eq> is continuous on <Eq>{`[a, b]`}</Eq>, then <Eq>{`f`}</Eq> attains its absolute maximum and absolute minimum values at some point in <Eq>{`[a, b]`}</Eq>.

</Teorema>

### Algorithm for absolute extrema on [a, b]

<Definicao titulo="Closed interval method">

1. Find all critical points of <Eq>{`f`}</Eq> in the interior <Eq>{`(a, b)`}</Eq>.
2. Evaluate <Eq>{`f`}</Eq> at each critical point and at the endpoints <Eq>{`a`}</Eq> and <Eq>{`b`}</Eq>.
3. The **largest value** is the absolute maximum; the **smallest value** is the absolute minimum.

</Definicao>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a five-year-old">

Imagine a **roller coaster**. There are places that are the **peaks** (places that are highest nearby) and places that are the **valleys** (places that are lowest nearby).

At the peak of a mountain, if you look around, you see everything nearby is lower. In a valley, everything nearby is higher.

The special trick: **at the exact moment you're at the peak or valley, the track is completely level** — with no tilt at all. This helps us find those special places!

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a ten-year-old">

When you graph a function, it goes up and down. The highest points are **maxima** and the lowest points are **minima**.

**How to find them?** At the top of a hill or bottom of a valley, the slope of the graph is **zero** — the tangent line is horizontal. So:

1. Calculate <Eq>{`f'(x)`}</Eq>.
2. Solve <Eq>{`f'(x) = 0`}</Eq>.
3. Check whether the function goes up before and down after (maximum), or down before and up after (minimum).

Example: <Eq>{`f(x) = x^2`}</Eq>. Derivative: <Eq>{`f'(x) = 2x`}</Eq>. Setting to zero: <Eq>{`x = 0`}</Eq>. Since <Eq>{`f'`}</Eq> changes from negative to positive at <Eq>{`x = 0`}</Eq>, it's a **minimum** — and indeed, <Eq>{`x^2`}</Eq> has its smallest value at <Eq>{`x = 0`}</Eq>.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For the high school student">

**The central problem:** given a graph, where are the peaks and valleys?

### Step by step

1. **Differentiate:** calculate <Eq>{`f'(x)`}</Eq>.
2. **Critical points:** solve <Eq>{`f'(x) = 0`}</Eq> and mark where <Eq>{`f'`}</Eq> does not exist.
3. **Classify with the 1st test:** make a sign table of <Eq>{`f'`}</Eq>.
   - <Eq>{`(+)`}</Eq> before, <Eq>{`(-)`}</Eq> after → local maximum.
   - <Eq>{`(-)`}</Eq> before, <Eq>{`(+)`}</Eq> after → local minimum.
4. (Quick alternative) **2nd test:** calculate <Eq>{`f''(c)`}</Eq> at the critical point.
   - <Eq>{`f''(c) < 0`}</Eq> → maximum. <Eq>{`f''(c) > 0`}</Eq> → minimum. <Eq>{`f''(c) = 0`}</Eq> → use the 1st.

**Absolute extremum on** <Eq>{`[a, b]`}</Eq>**:** evaluate <Eq>{`f`}</Eq> at the critical points and endpoints. The largest value wins; the smallest wins.

**Caution:** <Eq>{`f'(c) = 0`}</Eq> **does not guarantee** an extremum. In <Eq>{`f(x) = x^3`}</Eq>, we have <Eq>{`f'(0) = 0`}</Eq> but <Eq>{`x = 0`}</Eq> is an inflection point — the function does not change monotonicity there.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For the engineering student">

### Formal foundations

**Fermat's Theorem** guarantees that interior local extrema are critical points when <Eq>{`f`}</Eq> is differentiable. The converse fails: <Eq>{`x^3`}</Eq> shows that <Eq>{`f'(c) = 0`}</Eq> can occur at an inflection point without change in monotonicity.

The **Extreme Value Theorem** (Weierstrass) guarantees existence of absolute extrema when <Eq>{`f`}</Eq> is continuous on <Eq>{`[a, b]`}</Eq> — but does not provide location. The absolute extrema algorithm:

1. Interior critical points (where <Eq>{`f' = 0`}</Eq> or does not exist).
2. Endpoints <Eq>{`a, b`}</Eq>.
3. Evaluate <Eq>{`f`}</Eq> at all. Absolute maximum and minimum are the extreme values of the set.

### Tests

**1st derivative:** analyzes sign change of <Eq>{`f'`}</Eq> around <Eq>{`c`}</Eq>. More general — works even when <Eq>{`f''(c)`}</Eq> does not exist or is zero.

**2nd derivative:** at <Eq>{`c`}</Eq> with <Eq>{`f'(c) = 0`}</Eq>: if <Eq>{`f''(c) > 0`}</Eq>, the function has positive curvature (concave up) → local minimum. If <Eq>{`f''(c) < 0`}</Eq>, concave down → local maximum. The case <Eq>{`f''(c) = 0`}</Eq> is inconclusive: could be inflection (<Eq>{`x^3`}</Eq>), minimum (<Eq>{`x^4`}</Eq>), or maximum (<Eq>{`-x^4`}</Eq>).

**Connection to calculus of variations:** the Euler-Lagrange equation, a pillar of analytical mechanics and optimal control, is the functional generalization of <Eq>{`f'(c) = 0`}</Eq>.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For the senior professional">

The problem of locating extrema is as old as calculus itself. Fermat, two decades before Newton and Leibniz formalized calculus, already used the method of "adequality" to find maxima and minima — by substituting <Eq>{`x`}</Eq> with <Eq>{`x + e`}</Eq>, equating to the original value, and taking <Eq>{`e \\to 0`}</Eq>. It is the intuition of the derivative before the derivative existed as a concept.

The Extreme Value Theorem, formalized by Weierstrass in the nineteenth century, required completeness of the reals — a property that Cantor would axiomatize decades later. Without completeness, a continuum with gaps can have a continuous function that never attains its supremum.

In applied optimization: engineers, economists, and data scientists work with generalizations — Lagrange multipliers for constraints, KKT conditions (Karush-Kuhn-Tucker) for nonlinear programming, and zero gradient in high dimensions. The intuition of this lesson — "candidates are points where the gradient vanishes" — is universal. The challenge in high dimensions is distinguishing maximum, minimum, and saddle point (where <Eq>{`\\nabla f = 0`}</Eq> but neither maximum nor minimum).

In machine learning, gradient descent avoids computing critical points directly: it iterates in the direction of <Eq>{`-\\nabla f`}</Eq>, converging (hopefully) to a local minimum. The theory behind it — learning rates, convergence — is direct application of what you learn in this lesson.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Brazilian practical application">

**Maximum profit from a bakery.** A bakery produces <Eq>{`x`}</Eq> units of bread per day (in hundreds). Daily profit (in reais) is modeled by:

<Eq display>{`L(x) = -2x^2 + 12x - 10, \\quad x \\in [0, 5]`}</Eq>

**Step 1 — critical point:** <Eq>{`L'(x) = -4x + 12 = 0 \\Rightarrow x = 3`}</Eq>.

**Step 2 — compare:** <Eq>{`L(0) = -10`}</Eq>, <Eq>{`L(3) = -18 + 36 - 10 = 8`}</Eq>, <Eq>{`L(5) = -50 + 60 - 10 = 0`}</Eq>.

**Conclusion:** maximum profit of R\$ 800/day by producing 300 units. Below 100 units (x less than 1) or above 500 units (x greater than 5), the operation loses money.

**Connection to ENEM:** optimization problems appear on ENEM with quadratic models. The algorithm here — differentiate, zero, compare with domain endpoints — is exactly the method required.

</Porta>

## Worked Examples

<Exemplo titulo="Critical points of a cubic polynomial">

**Problem.** Find all critical points of <Eq>{`f(x) = x^3 - 3x^2 - 9x + 5`}</Eq> and classify them.

**Strategy.** Differentiate, zero, and use the second derivative test.

**Solution.**

<Eq display>{`f'(x) = 3x^2 - 6x - 9 = 3(x^2 - 2x - 3) = 3(x-3)(x+1)`}</Eq>

Critical points: <Eq>{`x = 3`}</Eq> and <Eq>{`x = -1`}</Eq>.

Second derivative: <Eq>{`f''(x) = 6x - 6`}</Eq>.

- <Eq>{`f''(-1) = -12 < 0`}</Eq> → **local maximum** at <Eq>{`x = -1`}</Eq>, with <Eq>{`f(-1) = -1 - 3 + 9 + 5 = 10`}</Eq>.
- <Eq>{`f''(3) = 12 > 0`}</Eq> → **local minimum** at <Eq>{`x = 3`}</Eq>, with <Eq>{`f(3) = 27 - 27 - 27 + 5 = -22`}</Eq>.

**Verification.** Sign table of <Eq>{`f'`}</Eq>: for <Eq>{`x < -1`}</Eq>, <Eq>{`f' > 0`}</Eq> (increasing). For <Eq>{`-1 < x < 3`}</Eq>, <Eq>{`f' < 0`}</Eq> (decreasing). For <Eq>{`x > 3`}</Eq>, <Eq>{`f' > 0`}</Eq> (increasing). Consistent with maximum at <Eq>{`-1`}</Eq> and minimum at <Eq>{`3`}</Eq>.

**Source.** [Active Calculus §3.1, Activity 3.1.2](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-3-3-tests.html) · CC-BY-NC-SA

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Absolute extrema on a closed interval">

**Problem.** Determine the absolute extrema of <Eq>{`f(x) = x^3 - 6x^2 + 9x + 2`}</Eq> on <Eq>{`[0, 4]`}</Eq>.

**Strategy.** Absolute extrema algorithm: interior critical points + endpoints.

**Solution.**

<Eq display>{`f'(x) = 3x^2 - 12x + 9 = 3(x^2 - 4x + 3) = 3(x-1)(x-3)`}</Eq>

Critical points in <Eq>{`(0,4)`}</Eq>: <Eq>{`x = 1`}</Eq> and <Eq>{`x = 3`}</Eq>.

Evaluation:

- <Eq>{`f(0) = 2`}</Eq>
- <Eq>{`f(1) = 1 - 6 + 9 + 2 = 6`}</Eq>
- <Eq>{`f(3) = 27 - 54 + 27 + 2 = 2`}</Eq>
- <Eq>{`f(4) = 64 - 96 + 36 + 2 = 6`}</Eq>

**Absolute maximum:** <Eq>{`f = 6`}</Eq>, attained at <Eq>{`x = 1`}</Eq> and <Eq>{`x = 4`}</Eq>. **Absolute minimum:** <Eq>{`f = 2`}</Eq>, attained at <Eq>{`x = 0`}</Eq> and <Eq>{`x = 3`}</Eq>.

**Verification.** Endpoints give valid extrema — the algorithm includes <Eq>{`a`}</Eq> and <Eq>{`b`}</Eq> by necessity.

**Source.** [OpenStax Calculus Vol. 1, §4.3, Example 4.13](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/4-3-maxima-and-minima) · CC-BY-NC-SA

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Critical point without extremum (inflection point)">

**Problem.** Show that <Eq>{`f(x) = x^3`}</Eq> has a critical point at <Eq>{`x = 0`}</Eq>, but this point is not a local extremum.

**Strategy.** Apply the first derivative test to verify no sign change.

**Solution.**

<Eq display>{`f'(x) = 3x^2`}</Eq>

<Eq>{`f'(0) = 0`}</Eq> → <Eq>{`x = 0`}</Eq> is a critical point.

For <Eq>{`x < 0`}</Eq>: <Eq>{`3x^2 > 0`}</Eq>. For <Eq>{`x > 0`}</Eq>: <Eq>{`3x^2 > 0`}</Eq>. Sign of <Eq>{`f'`}</Eq> **does not change** → no local extremum.

**Verification.** <Eq>{`f(-1) = -1 < f(0) = 0 < f(1) = 1`}</Eq>: the function is strictly increasing on every neighborhood of <Eq>{`0`}</Eq>, confirming it is neither maximum nor minimum.

**Source.** [APEX Calculus §3.1, Example 3.1.3](https://www.apexcalculus.com/) · CC-BY-NC

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="First derivative test with a fractional exponent">

**Problem.** Find the local extrema of <Eq>{`f(x) = x^{2/3}`}</Eq>.

**Strategy.** The derivative does not exist at <Eq>{`x = 0`}</Eq>; this creates a critical point even without <Eq>{`f' = 0`}</Eq>.

**Solution.**

<Eq display>{`f'(x) = \\frac{2}{3}x^{-1/3} = \\frac{2}{3\\sqrt[3]{x}}`}</Eq>

<Eq>{`f'(0)`}</Eq> does not exist (denominator zero), yet <Eq>{`f(0) = 0`}</Eq> exists → <Eq>{`x = 0`}</Eq> is a critical point.

Sign table: for <Eq>{`x < 0`}</Eq>, <Eq>{`x^{1/3} < 0`}</Eq>, so <Eq>{`f' < 0`}</Eq>. For <Eq>{`x > 0`}</Eq>, <Eq>{`f' > 0`}</Eq>. Sign change <Eq>{`(-) \\to (+)`}</Eq> at <Eq>{`x = 0`}</Eq> → **local minimum**.

**Verification.** <Eq>{`f(x) = x^{2/3} \\geq 0`}</Eq> for all <Eq>{`x`}</Eq>, and <Eq>{`f(0) = 0`}</Eq> → also absolute minimum.

**Source.** [Active Calculus §3.1, Exercise 3.1.3](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-3-3-tests.html) · CC-BY-NC-SA

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Extrema of a trigonometric function on a closed interval">

**Problem.** Determine the absolute extrema of <Eq>{`f(x) = 2\\sin x - x`}</Eq> on <Eq>{`[0, 2\\pi]`}</Eq>.

**Strategy.** Differentiate, find critical points (cosine zeros), apply the algorithm.

**Solution.**

<Eq display>{`f'(x) = 2\\cos x - 1 = 0 \\implies \\cos x = \\tfrac{1}{2} \\implies x = \\tfrac{\\pi}{3},\\; x = \\tfrac{5\\pi}{3}`}</Eq>

Evaluation at critical points and endpoints:

- <Eq>{`f(0) = 0`}</Eq>
- <Eq>{`f(\\pi/3) = 2 \\cdot (\\sqrt{3}/2) - \\pi/3 = \\sqrt{3} - \\pi/3 \\approx 0.685`}</Eq>
- <Eq>{`f(5\\pi/3) = 2 \\cdot (-\\sqrt{3}/2) - 5\\pi/3 = -\\sqrt{3} - 5\\pi/3 \\approx -6.97`}</Eq>
- <Eq>{`f(2\\pi) = 0 - 2\\pi \\approx -6.28`}</Eq>

**Absolute maximum:** <Eq>{`f(\\pi/3) = \\sqrt{3} - \\pi/3`}</Eq>. **Absolute minimum:** <Eq>{`f(5\\pi/3) = -\\sqrt{3} - 5\\pi/3`}</Eq>.

**Verification.** Sign of <Eq>{`f'`}</Eq>: positive on <Eq>{`(0, \\pi/3)`}</Eq>, negative on <Eq>{`(\\pi/3, 5\\pi/3)`}</Eq>, positive on <Eq>{`(5\\pi/3, 2\\pi)`}</Eq>. Maximum and minimum confirmed.

**Source.** [OpenStax Calculus Vol. 1, §4.3, Example 4.14](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/4-3-maxima-and-minima) · CC-BY-NC-SA

</Exemplo>
