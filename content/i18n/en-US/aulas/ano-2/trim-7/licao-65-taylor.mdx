### Taylor Polynomial

<Definicao titulo="Taylor Polynomial of Order n">
Let $f: I \to \mathbb{R}$ have $n$ derivatives at $a \in I$. The **Taylor polynomial** of order $n$ centered at $a$ is
$$P_n(x) = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!} (x - a)^k = f(a) + f'(a)(x-a) + \frac{f''(a)}{2!}(x-a)^2 + \cdots + \frac{f^{(n)}(a)}{n!}(x-a)^n.$$

When $a = 0$, $P_n$ is called the **Maclaurin polynomial** of $f$.
</Definicao>

> "If $f$ has $n$ derivatives at $x = a$, then the $n$th-order Taylor polynomial of $f$ centered at $a$ is $p_n(x) = \sum_{k=0}^n \frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}(x-a)^k$." — [APEX Calculus §8.6](https://www.apexcalculus.com/downloads)

<Teorema titulo="Uniqueness of the Taylor Polynomial">
$P_n$ is the unique polynomial of degree $\leq n$ such that $P_n^{(k)}(a) = f^{(k)}(a)$ for $k = 0, 1, \ldots, n$.
</Teorema>

### Lagrange Remainder

<Definicao titulo="Taylor Remainder (Lagrange Form)">
If $f$ has $n+1$ continuous derivatives in a neighborhood of $a$, the **remainder** $R_n(x) = f(x) - P_n(x)$ satisfies
$$R_n(x) = \frac{f^{(n+1)}(\xi)}{(n+1)!}(x-a)^{n+1}$$
for some $\xi$ strictly between $a$ and $x$.
</Definicao>

> "Let $f$ have $n + 1$ derivatives on an open interval $I$ and let $a \in I$. For each $x \in I$ there exists a value $c$ between $a$ and $x$ such that $R_n(x) = \frac{f^{(n+1)}(c)}{(n+1)!}(x-a)^{n+1}$." — [OpenStax Calculus Vol. 2 §6.3](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-2/pages/6-3-taylor-and-maclaurin-series)

<Insight titulo="Euler's Identity via Taylor">
Substituting $x \to ix$ into the series for $e^x$:
$$e^{ix} = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{(ix)^k}{k!} = \underbrace{\sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{(-1)^k x^{2k}}{(2k)!}}_{\cos x} + i\underbrace{\sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{(-1)^k x^{2k+1}}{(2k+1)!}}_{\sin x}.$$
Thus $e^{ix} = \cos x + i\sin x$. At $x = \pi$: $e^{i\pi} + 1 = 0$ — Euler's identity.
</Insight>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

Imagine a curve that's **all wiggly**. You want to **copy** it near a point, but you only have a ruler and a pencil.

You place the ruler to match the curve's slope at that point. It looks similar already!

If you also use a **circular arc** to adjust the curvature, it looks even more alike.

Each **extra thing** you add (slope, curvature, the next thing...) makes the copy more accurate. The Taylor polynomial is the **recipe for a perfect copy**—the more terms, the better.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

The idea is simple: any "nice" function can be **imitated by a polynomial** near a point.

Maclaurin's formula (when the point is $0$) is:

$$f(x) \approx f(0) + f'(0)\cdot x + \frac{f''(0)}{2}\cdot x^2 + \frac{f'''(0)}{6}\cdot x^3 + \cdots$$

Each new term uses a higher derivative. More terms = better imitation.

**Example with sine:** At $x = 0$: $\sin 0 = 0$, $\cos 0 = 1$, $-\sin 0 = 0$, $-\cos 0 = -1$, ...

So $\sin x \approx x - x^3/6 + x^5/120 - \cdots$

For small $x$, $\sin x \approx x$ already works—error less than $0.2\%$ for $x < 0.1$ radians!

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old">

Taylor is the **bridge between arbitrary functions and polynomials**—the easiest ones to calculate.

**Recipe:**
1. Choose a point $a$ (almost always $a = 0$ — Maclaurin series).
2. Calculate $f(a),\, f'(a),\, f''(a),\, \ldots,\, f^{(n)}(a)$.
3. Assemble $P_n(x) = \sum_{k=0}^n \frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}(x-a)^k$.

**Series every student should memorize:**

- $e^x = 1 + x + x^2/2 + x^3/6 + \cdots$
- $\sin x = x - x^3/6 + x^5/120 - \cdots$
- $\cos x = 1 - x^2/2 + x^4/24 - \cdots$
- $\ln(1+x) = x - x^2/2 + x^3/3 - \cdots$ for $|x| < 1$

**Three immediate applications:**

- **Approximating values:** $\sin(0.1) \approx 0.1 - 0.001/6 \approx 0.09983$.
- **Solving limits:** $\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{e^x - 1 - x}{x^2} = \lim_{x\to 0}\frac{x^2/2 + O(x^3)}{x^2} = \frac{1}{2}$.
- **Engineering:** $\sin\theta \approx \theta$ for small-angle pendulum—simplifies the differential equation.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

Taylor is the **engineer's surgical tool**: without it, there's no linearization, no small-signal analysis, no Newton-Raphson, no Runge-Kutta.

### Forms of the Remainder

$$f(x) = P_n(x) + R_n(x)$$

**Lagrange:** $R_n = \frac{f^{(n+1)}(\xi)}{(n+1)!}(x-a)^{n+1}$, some $\xi$ between $a$ and $x$.

**Integral:** $R_n = \int_a^x \frac{(x-t)^n}{n!} f^{(n+1)}(t)\, dt$.

**Big-O:** $R_n = O((x-a)^{n+1}$ — quick read of error order.

### Practical Applications

- **Linearization:** $f(x) \approx f(x_0) + f'(x_0)(x - x_0)$ — basis of small-signal analysis in electronics and control.
- **Newton-Raphson:** uses Taylor of order 1: $f(x) \approx f(x_n) + f'(x_n)(x - x_n) = 0$ gives $x_{n+1} = x_n - f(x_n)/f'(x_n)$.
- **Runge-Kutta:** derived via multivariate Taylor in $t$ and $y$; RK4 has global error $O(h^4)$.
- **Dispersion relations:** $\omega(k) \approx \omega_0 + v_g(k - k_0) + \cdots$ — group velocity as a Taylor coefficient.

### Multivariate Taylor (Order 2)

$$f(\vec x) \approx f(\vec a) + \nabla f(\vec a)^T (\vec x - \vec a) + \tfrac{1}{2}(\vec x - \vec a)^T H(\vec a)(\vec x - \vec a)$$

where $H$ is the **Hessian**. Critical for numerical optimization.

### Convergence: Not Always Guaranteed

$f(x) = e^{-1/x^2}$ (with $f(0) = 0$) has all derivatives zero at $0$, so $P_n = 0$ for all $n$. But $f \neq 0$ — a smooth but **non-analytic** function. Taylor converges, but does NOT converge to $f$.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a Senior Professional">

Taylor is the seed of complex analysis, perturbation theory, mathematical physics, and second-order optimization.

### Complex Analysis: Holomorphic Functions are Analytic

Every **holomorphic** function $f: \Omega \to \mathbb{C}$ equals its Taylor series on any open disk where it is defined (Cauchy, 1820s). This is radically stronger than in $\mathbb{R}$: real smoothness ($C^\infty$) does not guarantee analyticity; holomorphicity does. The beauty lies in this rigidity.

### Quantum Perturbation Theory

$H = H_0 + \lambda V$: eigenenergy expanded as a Taylor series in $\lambda$: $E_n(\lambda) = E_n^{(0)} + \lambda E_n^{(1)} + \lambda^2 E_n^{(2)} + \cdots$ Coefficients via Rayleigh-Schrödinger. It's Taylor applied in operator space.

### Asymptotic vs. Convergent Series

Stirling: $\ln(n!) \sim n\ln n - n + \tfrac{1}{2}\ln(2\pi n) + \sum_k B_{2k}/(2k(2k-1)n^{2k-1})$. This series **diverges** for every fixed $n$, but truncated it gives excellent numerical approximation. Philosophical difference: a series can be useful even if divergent—the error decreases before exploding.

### Padé Approximants

Rational approximation $f(x) \approx P_m(x)/Q_n(x)$ outperforms Taylor in effective radius for functions with singularities. Used in quantum mechanics (Padé-Borel method) and series convergence acceleration.

### Historical Context

Brook Taylor (1715) publishes *Methodus Incrementorum* without proof of convergence. Maclaurin (1742) popularizes $a = 0$. Lagrange (1797) formalizes the remainder. Cauchy (1820s) rigorously proves it via contour integral. This whole machinery arrived ready for Maxwell to build electromagnetism and for Einstein to linearize the geodesic around a flat metric.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practical Application">

**4-Step Recipe:**

1. **Identify the expansion point** $a$ (usually $0$).
2. **Calculate derivatives** $f(a), f'(a), f''(a), \ldots$ to the desired order.
3. **Assemble** $P_n(x) = \sum_{k=0}^n \frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}(x-a)^k$.
4. **Estimate the error**: for a decreasing alternating series, $|R_n| < $ absolute value of the first omitted term.

### Operational Tricks

- **Substitute:** $\sin(x^2) = x^2 - (x^2)^3/6 + \cdots = x^2 - x^6/6 + \cdots$ — straight from the sin series.
- **Multiply:** $e^x \sin x = (1 + x + x^2/2 + \cdots)(x - x^3/6 + \cdots)$, expand to the desired order.
- **Integrate/Differentiate:** $\arctan x = \int_0^x \frac{1}{1+t^2}\, dt = \int_0^x (1 - t^2 + t^4 - \cdots)\, dt = x - x^3/3 + x^5/5 - \cdots$

### Brazilian Context — Interest Rates

In finance, $(1 + r)^n \approx 1 + nr$ for small rate $r$ (1st-order Taylor). Relative error: approximately $\binom{n}{2} r^2$. For $r = 1\%$ per month and $n = 12$ months: error $\approx 66 \times 0.0001 = 0.66\%$ — negligible for quick annual return estimates.

### Python/SymPy Verification

```python
import sympy as sp
x = sp.symbols('x')
sp.series(sp.sin(x), x, 0, 8)       # Maclaurin of sin x up to x^7
sp.series(sp.exp(x)*sp.cos(x), x, 0, 5)
```

## Solved Examples

<Exemplo titulo="Maclaurin of e^x up to order 4 (direct application)" numero="1">

**Problem.** Find the Maclaurin polynomial of $f(x) = e^x$ of order 4 and estimate the error at $x = 0.5$.

**Strategy.** Since $\frac{d^k}{dx^k}e^x = e^x$, all derivatives at $a = 0$ are $1$. Assemble $P_4$ directly. Use Lagrange remainder with $f^{(5)} = e^x$.

**Solution.**

1. **Derivatives at $0$:** $f^{(k)}(0) = e^0 = 1$ for all $k$.
2. **Polynomial:** $P_4(x) = 1 + x + \dfrac{x^2}{2} + \dfrac{x^3}{6} + \dfrac{x^4}{24}$.
3. **Error at $x = 0.5$:** $|R_4(0.5)| \leq \dfrac{e^\xi}{5!}(0.5)^5 \leq \dfrac{e^{0.5}}{120}\cdot\dfrac{1}{32} \approx \dfrac{1.649}{120 \cdot 32} \approx 0.000430$.
4. **Approximate value:** $P_4(0.5) = 1 + 0.5 + 0.125 + 0.02083 + 0.002604 \approx 1.6484$. Exact value: $e^{0.5} \approx 1.6487$ — error less than $3 \times 10^{-4}$. $\checkmark$

**Verification.** The estimated error $4.3 \times 10^{-4}$ is greater than the actual error $3 \times 10^{-4}$: the Lagrange remainder is an upper bound. $\checkmark$

**Source.** [OpenStax Calculus Vol. 2, §6.3, Example 6.17](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-2/pages/6-3-taylor-and-maclaurin-series) — CC-BY-NC-SA.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Taylor of ln x around a = 1 (intermediate)" numero="2">

**Problem.** Obtain the Taylor polynomial of order 3 for $f(x) = \ln x$ centered at $a = 1$.

**Strategy.** Calculate $f, f', f'', f'''$ at $x = 1$. Assemble $P_3$ using $(x - 1)$ as the variable.

**Solution.**

1. **Derivatives:** $f(x) = \ln x$, $f'(x) = 1/x$, $f''(x) = -1/x^2$, $f'''(x) = 2/x^3$.
2. **At $a = 1$:** $f(1) = 0$, $f'(1) = 1$, $f''(1) = -1$, $f'''(1) = 2$.
3. **Polynomial:**
$$P_3(x) = 0 + 1\cdot(x-1) + \frac{-1}{2!}(x-1)^2 + \frac{2}{3!}(x-1)^3 = (x-1) - \frac{(x-1)^2}{2} + \frac{(x-1)^3}{3}.$$
4. **Test at $x = 1.1$:** $P_3(1.1) = 0.1 - 0.005 + 0.000333 \approx 0.09533$. Exact value: $\ln(1.1) \approx 0.09531$. $\checkmark$

**Verification.** The series for $\ln(1+u) = u - u^2/2 + u^3/3 - \cdots$ with $u = x - 1$ matches $P_3$. $\checkmark$

**Source.** [Active Calculus §8.5, Activity 8.5.3](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-8-5-taylor.html) — CC-BY-NC-SA.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Limit via Taylor: lim (sin x - x)/x^3 (intermediate)" numero="3">

**Problem.** Calculate $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin x - x}{x^3}$.

**Strategy.** Substitute the Maclaurin series for $\sin x$ into the numerator and simplify. Avoids iterated L'Hôpital's Rule.

**Solution.**

1. **Series:** $\sin x = x - \dfrac{x^3}{6} + \dfrac{x^5}{120} - \cdots$
2. **Numerator:** $\sin x - x = -\dfrac{x^3}{6} + \dfrac{x^5}{120} - \cdots$
3. **Division by $x^3$:** $\dfrac{\sin x - x}{x^3} = -\dfrac{1}{6} + \dfrac{x^2}{120} - \cdots$
4. **Limit:** as $x \to 0$, all terms with $x$ go to zero. The limit is $\mathbf{-1/6}$.

**Verification.** Applying L'Hôpital's Rule three times would also yield $-1/6$, but the Taylor method is faster. $\checkmark$

**Source.** [OpenStax Calculus Vol. 2, §6.3, Example 6.20](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-2/pages/6-3-taylor-and-maclaurin-series) — CC-BY-NC-SA.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Approximation error sin x ≈ x for pendulum (modeling)" numero="4">

**Problem.** A pendulum uses the approximation $\sin\theta \approx \theta$. For $\theta = 15° = \pi/12$ radians, what is the percent relative error?

**Strategy.** Calculate $\sin(\pi/12)$ exactly and compare with $\pi/12$. Estimate the error via the next term in the series: $-\theta^3/6$.

**Solution.**

1. **Angle in radians:** $\theta = \pi/12 \approx 0.2618$ radians.
2. **Exact value:** $\sin(\pi/12) = \sin(15°) = (\sqrt{6} - \sqrt{2})/4 \approx 0.2588$.
3. **Approximation:** $\theta = 0.2618$.
4. **Absolute error:** $|0.2618 - 0.2588| = 0.0030$.
5. **Relative error:** $0.0030 / 0.2588 \approx 1.16\%$.
6. **Via Taylor:** error $\approx \theta^3/6 = (0.2618)^3/6 \approx 0.00298$ — very close to the calculated error. $\checkmark$

**Verification.** For $\theta < 0.1$ radians ($\approx 5.7°$), the error drops below $0.017\%$ — justifying its use in engineering. $\checkmark$

**Source.** [APEX Calculus §8.6, Exercise 8.6.13](https://www.apexcalculus.com/downloads) — CC-BY-NC.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Maclaurin by composition: e^(sin x) up to order 3 (advanced)" numero="5">

**Problem.** Obtain the Maclaurin polynomial of $f(x) = e^{\sin x}$ up to order 3.

**Strategy.** Substitute the series for $\sin x$ into the series for $e^u$, grouping terms up to $x^3$.

**Solution.**

1. **Sine series:** $\sin x = x - x^3/6 + O(x^5)$.
2. **$u = \sin x$:** then $e^u = 1 + u + u^2/2 + u^3/6 + O(u^4)$.
3. **Substituting:** $u = x - x^3/6 + \cdots$, so:
   - $u = x - x^3/6$
   - $u^2 = x^2 - 2x\cdot x^3/6 + \cdots = x^2 + O(x^4)$
   - $u^3 = x^3 + O(x^5)$
4. **Assembly:**
$$e^{\sin x} = 1 + \left(x - \tfrac{x^3}{6}\right) + \frac{x^2}{2} + \frac{x^3}{6} + O(x^4) = 1 + x + \frac{x^2}{2} + 0 \cdot x^3 + O(x^4).$$
5. **Result:** $P_3(x) = 1 + x + \dfrac{x^2}{2}$.

**Verification.** At $x = 0$: $f(0) = e^0 = 1$ $\checkmark$. $f'(0) = e^{\sin 0}\cos 0 = 1$ $\checkmark$. $f''(0) = e^{\sin 0}(\cos^2 0 - \sin 0) = 1$ $\checkmark$.

**Source.** [APEX Calculus §8.6, Exercises 8.6.17–8.6.20](https://www.apexcalculus.com/downloads) — CC-BY-NC.
</Exemplo>
