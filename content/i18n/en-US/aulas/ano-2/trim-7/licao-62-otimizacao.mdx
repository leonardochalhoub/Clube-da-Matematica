### Optimization Problem with Constraint

<Definicao titulo="General Structure">

An **optimization problem** has three ingredients:

1.  An **objective function** <Eq>{`Q`}</Eq> that we want to maximize or minimize.
2.  One or more **decision variables** and a **physical domain** <Eq>{`[a, b]`}</Eq> imposed by the context.
3.  One or more **constraints** that relate the variables — used to reduce to a single variable.

</Definicao>

### Optimization Algorithm (One Variable)

<Definicao titulo="Standard Roadmap">

1.  **Define** the quantity to optimize and name the variables with physical meaning.
2.  **Write** the equation <Eq>{`Q = f(x, y, \ldots)`}</Eq> for the objective function.
3.  **Use the constraint** to eliminate secondary variables, obtaining <Eq>{`Q = f(x)`}</Eq> with domain <Eq>{`x \in [a, b]`}</Eq>.
4.  **Apply the absolute extrema algorithm**: critical points in the interior + evaluation at the endpoints.
5.  **Interpret** the result in the context of the problem (units, physical feasibility).

</Definicao>

> "Suppose we wish to find the value(s) of x for which a given function Q is maximized or minimized. We use derivatives to find critical points and then evaluate Q at those points and at the endpoints of the domain to determine the absolute maximum or minimum." — [Active Calculus §3.3](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-3-3-optimization.html)

### Canonical Example: Fixed-Volume Cylindrical Can

<Definicao titulo="The Can Problem">

Given: fixed volume <Eq>{`V`}</Eq> (parameter). Minimize total surface area <Eq>{`A = 2\pi r^2 + 2\pi r h`}</Eq>.

**Constraint:** <Eq>{`\pi r^2 h = V \Rightarrow h = V/(\pi r^2)`}</Eq>.

**Reduced Objective Function:**
<Eq display>{`A(r) = 2\pi r^2 + \frac{2V}{r}, \quad r > 0`}</Eq>

**Critical Point:**
<Eq display>{`A'(r) = 4\pi r - \frac{2V}{r^2} = 0 \implies r^* = \sqrt[3]{\frac{V}{2\pi}}`}</Eq>

**Optimal Dimension:** substituting, <Eq>{`h^* = 2r^*`}</Eq>. The can using the least material has **height equal to its diameter**.

</Definicao>

<Insight>

The optimal can (<Eq>{`h = 2r`}</Eq>) minimizes the area-to-volume ratio. Real-world soda cans deviate from this for reasons of stacking and structural integrity, not pure mathematical optimization.

</Insight>

> "The optimal cylinder has height equal to diameter — this is a consequence of the symmetry of the problem and appears frequently in packaging design." — [OpenStax Calculus Vol. 1, §4.7](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/4-7-applied-optimization-problems)

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-Year-Old">

Imagine you have a piece of string and want to make the **biggest possible playpen** for your puppy.

If you make the playpen **very long and skinny**, the puppy can barely run from one side to the other.

If you make it **square**, the puppy has much more room to play!

The trick is: with the same string, different shapes have different sizes. **The right shape holds more space.** Math helps us find the best shape!

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-Year-Old">

You have 40 meters of fencing and want to make a rectangular pen. What's the largest pen you can make?

Let the width be <Eq>{`x`}</Eq>. The length will be <Eq>{`20 - x`}</Eq> (because the perimeter must be 40 m).

Area: <Eq>{`A(x) = x \cdot (20 - x) = 20x - x^2`}</Eq>.

Derivative: <Eq>{`A'(x) = 20 - 2x = 0 \Rightarrow x = 10`}</Eq>.

The largest pen is <Eq>{`10 \times 10 = 100`}</Eq> m² — it's a **square**!

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For the High School Student">

Applied optimization has a four-step recipe. Practice the steps until they become automatic.

**Step 1 — Model:** read the problem and write the objective function <Eq>{`Q`}</Eq> (what you want to maximize/minimize).

**Step 2 — Reduce:** use the constraint to eliminate extra variables. You need <Eq>{`Q = f(x)`}</Eq> with **only one** variable.

**Step 3 — Differentiate:** calculate <Eq>{`f'(x)`}</Eq>, set it to zero, find <Eq>{`x^*`}</Eq>.

**Step 4 — Compare:** evaluate <Eq>{`f`}</Eq> at <Eq>{`x^*`}</Eq> and at the boundaries of the physical domain. The largest (or smallest) value wins.

**Tip for Step 2:** when the problem mentions "fixed perimeter," "fixed volume," "fixed budget," that's the constraint. Isolate one variable and substitute.

**Watch out for the physical domain:** lengths must be positive; <Eq>{`0 < x < L`}</Eq>. Never ignore this.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For the Engineering Student">

Optimization with one variable is the simplest case of a general mathematical programming problem. Here the technique is straightforward: the Extreme Value Theorem guarantees that if the domain is compact and <Eq>{`f`}</Eq> is continuous, the optimum exists — and the absolute extrema algorithm finds it.

**When the domain is open or unbounded:** the optimum might be the unique critical point. The standard argument is: if <Eq>{`f(x) \to +\infty`}</Eq> as <Eq>{`x \to 0^+`}</Eq> and <Eq>{`x \to +\infty`}</Eq>, and there's a unique interior critical point with <Eq>{`f'' > 0`}</Eq>, then that point is the global minimum (not just local). This argument appears explicitly in the can problem.

**Second-Order Economic Modeling:** in economics, marginal cost <Eq>{`C'(x)`}</Eq> and marginal revenue <Eq>{`R'(x)`}</Eq> determine the maximum profit point: <Eq>{`L'(x) = R'(x) - C'(x) = 0`}</Eq>, i.e., marginal revenue equals marginal cost. This is the central principle of neoclassical microeconomics.

**Extension:** in two variables, <Eq>{`\nabla f = 0`}</Eq> with constraint <Eq>{`g = 0`}</Eq> leads to the method of Lagrange multipliers — the next natural step after this lesson.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For the Senior Professional">

The constrained optimization problem has roots in 17th-century calculus of variations. Euler and Lagrange generalized it to functionals — "which function <Eq>{`y(x)`}</Eq> minimizes <Eq>{`\int F(x,y,y') dx`}</Eq>?" The Euler-Lagrange equation is the generalization of <Eq>{`f'(x^*) = 0`}</Eq> to infinite-dimensional spaces.

In optimal control (Pontryagin, 1950s), the question is "which control function <Eq>{`u(t)`}</Eq> minimizes a cost over a trajectory?" Pontryagin's Maximum Principle generalizes this lesson's method to dynamic systems. Direct applications: rocket trajectories, resource management, algorithmic trading.

In contemporary machine learning, training neural networks is optimization in extremely high dimensions (billions of parameters). The intuition from this lesson — "the gradient points where the function grows; zero gradient is a candidate for optimum" — is the foundation of gradient descent (SGD, Adam, etc.).

The economics of the cylindrical can reappears in packaging design: aluminum has a cost per area, not per volume. The <Eq>{`h = 2r`}</Eq> proportion (mathematically optimal) yields to transportation constraints (stacking: shorter cans) and merchandising (larger label area). Real engineering is optimization with multiple, conflicting constraints and objectives.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Brazilian Practical Application">

**Problem:** A small packaging factory in São Paulo receives an order for open-top boxes made from a 12 cm × 8 cm rectangular sheet. To make the box, squares of side <Eq>{`x`}</Eq> cm are cut from the four corners, and the flaps are folded up.

Base dimensions: <Eq>{`(12 - 2x) \times (8 - 2x)`}</Eq>. Height: <Eq>{`x`}</Eq>. Domain: <Eq>{`0 < x < 4`}</Eq>.

<Eq display>{`V(x) = x(12-2x)(8-2x) = 4x^3 - 40x^2 + 96x`}</Eq>

<Eq display>{`V'(x) = 12x^2 - 80x + 96 = 4(3x^2 - 20x + 24)`}</Eq>

Roots: <Eq>{`x = \frac{20 \pm \sqrt{400 - 288}}{6} = \frac{20 \pm \sqrt{112}}{6}`}</Eq>.

<Eq>{`x_1 \approx 1.57`}</Eq> cm (feasible) and <Eq>{`x_2 \approx 5.10`}</Eq> cm (outside the domain).

<Eq>{`V(1.57) \approx 67.6`}</Eq> cm³. Therefore, cutting squares of approximately **1.57 cm** maximizes the box volume — a direct result applicable on the factory floor.

**In Brazil:** this type of problem appears in packaging optimization to reduce material waste, relevant for companies seeking ISO 14001 certification (environmental management).

</Porta>

## Solved Examples

<Exemplo titulo="Rectangular Fence with One Fixed Side">

**Problem.** A farmer has 120 m of fencing to build a rectangular pasture, using an existing wall as one of the long sides. What are the dimensions of the largest possible area?

**Strategy.** Identify the objective function (area) and the constraint (perimeter with three free sides = 120 m). Eliminate one variable and maximize.

**Solution.**

Let <Eq>{`x`}</Eq> = width (two sides) and <Eq>{`y`}</Eq> = length (side opposite the wall). Constraint: <Eq>{`2x + y = 120 \Rightarrow y = 120 - 2x`}</Eq>.

Area: <Eq>{`A(x) = xy = x(120 - 2x) = 120x - 2x^2`}</Eq>. Domain: <Eq>{`0 < x < 60`}</Eq>.

<Eq display>{`A'(x) = 120 - 4x = 0 \implies x = 30 \text{ m}`}</Eq>

<Eq>{`y = 120 - 60 = 60`}</Eq> m. Maximum area: <Eq>{`30 \times 60 = 1800`}</Eq> m².

**Verification.** <Eq>{`A''(30) = -4 < 0`}</Eq> → maximum confirmed. At the endpoints: <Eq>{`A(0) = A(60) = 0`}</Eq>.

**Source.** [OpenStax Calculus Vol. 1, §4.7, Example 4.33](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/4-7-applied-optimization-problems) · CC-BY-NC-SA

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Open-Top Box of Maximum Volume">

**Problem.** From a square sheet of side 36 cm, equal squares are cut from the corners and the flaps are folded to form an open-top box. What size cut maximizes the volume?

**Strategy.** Express the volume as a function of the side <Eq>{`x`}</Eq> of the cut square, differentiate, find the critical point.

**Solution.**

Base: <Eq>{`(36 - 2x)^2`}</Eq>. Height: <Eq>{`x`}</Eq>. Domain: <Eq>{`0 < x < 18`}</Eq>.

<Eq display>{`V(x) = x(36-2x)^2 = x(1296 - 144x + 4x^2) = 4x^3 - 144x^2 + 1296x`}</Eq>

<Eq display>{`V'(x) = 12x^2 - 288x + 1296 = 12(x^2 - 24x + 108) = 12(x-6)(x-18)`}</Eq>

Roots: <Eq>{`x = 6`}</Eq> (feasible) and <Eq>{`x = 18`}</Eq> (endpoint, <Eq>{`V = 0`}</Eq>).

<Eq>{`V(6) = 6 \cdot 24^2 = 6 \cdot 576 = 3456`}</Eq> cm³.

**Verification.** <Eq>{`V''(6) = 24 \cdot 6 - 288 = -144 < 0`}</Eq> → maximum.

**Source.** [OpenStax Calculus Vol. 1, §4.7, Example 4.32](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/4-7-applied-optimization-problems) · CC-BY-NC-SA

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Minimum Area Cylindrical Can">

**Problem.** A cylindrical can must hold <Eq>{`V = 1000`}</Eq> cm³. Determine the radius and height that minimize the total material area.

**Strategy.** Express the area as a function of the radius using <Eq>{`h = V/(\pi r^2)`}</Eq>, differentiate.

**Solution.**

<Eq display>{`A(r) = 2\pi r^2 + \frac{2V}{r} = 2\pi r^2 + \frac{2000}{r}`}</Eq>

<Eq display>{`A'(r) = 4\pi r - \frac{2000}{r^2} = 0 \implies r^3 = \frac{2000}{4\pi} = \frac{500}{\pi}`}</Eq>

<Eq display>{`r^* = \sqrt[3]{\frac{500}{\pi}} \approx 5.42 \text{ cm}`}</Eq>

<Eq>{`h^* = 1000/(\pi \cdot r^{*2}) \approx 10.84`}</Eq> cm <Eq>{`= 2r^*`}</Eq> — height equals diameter.

**Verification.** <Eq>{`A''(r) = 4\pi + 4000/r^3 > 0`}</Eq> for all <Eq>{`r > 0`}</Eq> → global minimum.

**Source.** [Active Calculus §3.3, Activity 3.3.2](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-3-3-optimization.html) · CC-BY-NC-SA

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Minimum Distance from Point to Line">

**Problem.** Determine the point on the line <Eq>{`y = 2x + 3`}</Eq> closest to the origin.

**Strategy.** Minimize <Eq>{`d^2 = x^2 + y^2`}</Eq> (equivalent to minimizing <Eq>{`d`}</Eq>) by substituting <Eq>{`y = 2x + 3`}</Eq>.

**Solution.**

<Eq display>{`D(x) = x^2 + (2x+3)^2 = 5x^2 + 12x + 9`}</Eq>

<Eq display>{`D'(x) = 10x + 12 = 0 \implies x^* = -\frac{6}{5}`}</Eq>

<Eq>{`y^* = 2(-6/5) + 3 = 3/5`}</Eq>. Distance: <Eq>{`d = \sqrt{(-6/5)^2 + (3/5)^2} = \sqrt{45/25} = 3\sqrt{5}/5`}</Eq>.

**Verification.** <Eq>{`D''(x) = 10 > 0`}</Eq> → minimum. The analytic point-to-line distance formula yields the same result: <Eq>{`d = |0 - 0 + 3|/\sqrt{5} = 3/\sqrt{5} = 3\sqrt{5}/5`}</Eq>.

**Source.** [APEX Calculus §4.3, Example 4.3.2](https://www.apexcalculus.com/) · CC-BY-NC

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Maximum Profit with Non-linear Cost">

**Problem.** A company has revenue <Eq>{`R(x) = 200x - x^2`}</Eq> and cost <Eq>{`C(x) = x^3/3 - 7x^2 + 111x + 50`}</Eq> for <Eq>{`x \in [0, 14]`}</Eq> units. Maximize profit.

**Strategy.** Profit <Eq>{`L = R - C`}</Eq>. Differentiate, find critical point, compare with endpoints.

**Solution.**

<Eq display>{`L(x) = 200x - x^2 - \frac{x^3}{3} + 7x^2 - 111x - 50 = -\frac{x^3}{3} + 6x^2 + 89x - 50`}</Eq>

<Eq display>{`L'(x) = -x^2 + 12x + 89`}</Eq>

Discriminant: <Eq>{`144 + 356 = 500`}</Eq>. Roots: <Eq>{`x = \frac{-12 \pm \sqrt{500}}{-2}`}</Eq>. Positive root in <Eq>{`[0,14]`}</Eq>: <Eq>{`x^* \approx 17.18`}</Eq> (outside). Smaller root: <Eq>{`x \approx -5.18`}</Eq> (outside). Thus <Eq>{`L'(x) > 0`}</Eq> on <Eq>{`[0,14]`}</Eq> — <Eq>{`L`}</Eq> is increasing: maximum at endpoint <Eq>{`x = 14`}</Eq>.

<Eq>{`L(14) = -2744/3 + 6(196) + 89(14) - 50 = -914.67 + 1176 + 1246 - 50 \approx 1457`}</Eq>.

**Verification.** Checking <Eq>{`L(0) = -50`}</Eq> confirms the absolute maximum is at the endpoint <Eq>{`x = 14`}</Eq>.

**Source.** [Active Calculus §3.3, Exercise 3.3.4](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-3-3-optimization.html) · CC-BY-NC-SA

</Exemplo>
