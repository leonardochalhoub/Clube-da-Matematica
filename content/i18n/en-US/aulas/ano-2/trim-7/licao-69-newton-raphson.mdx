### The Newton-Raphson Iteration

<Definicao titulo="Newton-Raphson Iteration">
Let $f: I \to \mathbb{R}$ be differentiable on an interval $I$, and let $x_0 \in I$ be an initial estimate of a root. The **Newton-Raphson iteration** is the sequence defined by:

$$x_{n+1} = x_n - \frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)}, \quad n = 0, 1, 2, \ldots$$

Geometrically, $x_{n+1}$ is the intersection of the tangent line to the graph of $f$ at $(x_n, f(x_n))$ with the x-axis.
</Definicao>

> "Newton's Method is a technique to approximate the solution of $f(x) = 0$. It works when one can perform repeated evaluations of $f$ and $f'$, making it ideal for functions like polynomials, exponentials, and trigonometric functions." — [APEX Calculus, §4.4](https://www.apexcalculus.com/)

### Derivation via Linear Approximation (1st Order Taylor)

If $r$ is a root of $f$ and $x_n$ is close to $r$, by the Taylor expansion:

$$0 = f(r) \approx f(x_n) + f'(x_n)(r - x_n).$$

Solving for $r$: $r \approx x_n - f(x_n)/f'(x_n) = x_{n+1}$. The iteration *defines* the next estimate as the zero of the linear approximation.

### Theorem of Local Convergence

<Teorema titulo="Quadratic Convergence of Newton-Raphson">
If $f \in C^2$ on an open neighborhood of $r$ with $f(r) = 0$ and $f'(r) \neq 0$. Then there exists $\delta > 0$ such that, for all $x_0$ with $|x_0 - r| < \delta$:

1. The sequence $\{x_n\}$ is well-defined and converges to $r$.
2. There is **quadratic convergence**: $|x_{n+1} - r| \leq C\, |x_n - r|^2$, where $C = \dfrac{|f''(r)|}{2|f'(r)|}$.
</Teorema>

**Proof (sketch).** Let $e_n = x_n - r$. Taylor expansion of $f$ around $r$:

$$0 = f(r) = f(x_n) + f'(x_n)(r - x_n) + \frac{f''(\xi_n)}{2}(r - x_n)^2$$

for some $\xi_n$ between $x_n$ and $r$. From the iteration, $x_{n+1} - r = x_n - f(x_n)/f'(x_n) - r$. Substituting and simplifying:

$$e_{n+1} = -\frac{f''(\xi_n)}{2 f'(x_n)}\, e_n^2.$$

As $x_n \to r$, $\xi_n \to r$ and $f'(x_n) \to f'(r) \neq 0$, hence $|e_{n+1}|/|e_n|^2 \to |f''(r)|/(2|f'(r)|) = C$. $\square$

### Pathologies and Failures

<Definicao titulo="When Newton-Raphson Fails">
- **Zero derivative at an iterate**: $f'(x_n) = 0$ — division by zero, iteration undefined.
- **Distant guess**: sequence may diverge or jump to another root.
- **Multiple root** (multiplicity $m \geq 2$): convergence is only **linear**, $|e_{n+1}| \approx (1 - 1/m)|e_n|$. Correction: use $x_{n+1} = x_n - m\, f(x_n)/f'(x_n)$.
- **Cycling**: sequence enters a periodic orbit (e.g., $f(x) = x^3 - 2x + 2$, $x_0 = 0$).
</Definicao>

<Insight>
**Newton's Basin.** For a complex function $f$, the set $\{x_0 \in \mathbb{C} : x_n \to r_k\}$ is the **attraction basin** of the root $r_k$. The boundaries between distinct basins are **fractals** — self-similar structures discovered by Cayley (1879), still studied today in dynamical systems.
</Insight>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

Imagine you want to **find where a drawn snake** crosses the floor. You look at **a small piece** of it and draw a **straight line** showing where it's going. Then you see where **that straight line** crosses the floor.

That point is **closer** to the real crossing. You repeat the trick from there, and get even closer. With a few tries, you're there!

The snake is the function $f$. The floor is the x-axis. The straight line is the tangent. And "finding where it crosses" is what we call Newton-Raphson.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10"titulo="For a 10-year-old">

To find where $f(x) = 0$ (where the curve crosses the x-axis):

1. Pick a guess $x_0$ near the root.
2. Calculate $f(x_0)$ (the height of the curve there) and $f'(x_0)$ (the slope of the curve there).
3. Draw the tangent line. Where this line crosses the x-axis is your new guess $x_1$.
4. Repeat.

The formula is $x_{n+1} = x_n - f(x_n)/f'(x_n)$.

**Example: finding $\sqrt{2}$.** We want $x^2 = 2$, which means $f(x) = x^2 - 2 = 0$. Since $f'(x) = 2x$, the iteration becomes $x_{n+1} = (x_n + 2/x_n)/2$.

- $x_0 = 1$
- $x_1 = (1 + 2)/2 = 1.5$
- $x_2 = (1.5 + 2/1.5)/2 \approx 1.4167$
- $x_3 \approx 1.41421568...$

That's already 5 correct digits! Each step, the number of correct digits doubles.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15"titulo="For a 15-year-old">

Newton-Raphson is the root-finding algorithm that scientific calculators use internally to compute $\sqrt{a}$, $\ln a$, $\sin^{-1} a$, and many others.

### The Idea: Linearization

Replace $f$ by its tangent at $x_n$: $L(x) = f(x_n) + f'(x_n)(x - x_n)$. Solve $L(x) = 0$:

$$x = x_n - \frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)}.$$

This is $x_{n+1}$. In the next iteration, you use $x_{n+1}$ as the new point of tangency.

### Why Is It So Fast?

The error $e_n = x_n - r$ satisfies $e_{n+1} \approx C\, e_n^2$. This means:
- $e_0 = 10^{-1}$ → $e_1 \approx 10^{-2}$ → $e_2 \approx 10^{-4}$ → $e_3 \approx 10^{-8}$ → $e_4 \approx 10^{-16}$.

In 4 steps, you have machine precision (double). This is **quadratic convergence**.

### When It Fails

- **$f'(x_n) \approx 0$**: division by near-zero, huge jump.
- **Guess is far away**: may diverge or converge to another root.
- **Double root**: convergence degrades to linear.

### Reasonable Stopping Criterion

Combine two tests: $|f(x_n)| < \varepsilon_f$ **and** $|x_{n+1} - x_n| < \varepsilon_x$. Using only the first can fail near multiple roots.

### Direct Applications

- **$\sqrt{a}$**: $f(x) = x^2 - a$ → Heron/Babylonian iteration.
- **$1/a$ without division**: $f(x) = 1/x - a$ → $x_{n+1} = x_n(2 - ax_n)$.
- **IRR** (Internal Rate of Return): root of a polynomial in $(1+r)^{-1}$.
- **Equilibrium point**: where supply = demand in an economic model.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25"titulo="For an engineering student">

Newton-Raphson is the cornerstone of numerical calculus. Almost every non-linear solver—in MATLAB, Python/SciPy, Julia—uses Newton or one of its variants as its engine.

### Rigorous Error Analysis

Error $e_n = x_n - r$. Taylor expansion of order 2 around $r$:

$$0 = f(r) = f(x_n) + f'(x_n)(r - x_n) + \frac{f''(\xi_n)}{2}(r - x_n)^2.$$

Isolating $e_{n+1} = x_{n+1} - r$:

$$e_{n+1} = -\frac{f''(\xi_n)}{2 f'(x_n)}\, e_n^2.$$

As $x_n \to r$: $|e_{n+1}|/|e_n|^2 \to C = |f''(r)|/(2|f'(r)|)$. Convergence is exactly quadratic.

### Global vs. Local Convergence

Newton converges **locally** (near the root). For global guarantee:
- **$f$ convex and increasing** (or concave and decreasing) with $f(x_0) > 0$: Newton converges globally.
- **Kantorovich conditions**: $|f(x_0) f''(x_0)| \leq |f'(x_0)|^2 / 2$ — a quantitative condition guaranteeing convergence even without knowing where the root is.

### Quasi-Newton and Secant Method

Calculating $f'$ analytically can be expensive or difficult. The **secant method** approximates $f'(x_n) \approx (f(x_n) - f(x_{n-1}))/(x_n - x_{n-1})$: **superlinear** convergence with exponent $\phi \approx 1.618$ (golden ratio).

In multivariate optimization: BFGS and L-BFGS build Hessian approximations via rank-updates. They are the standard in `scipy.optimize`, `sklearn`, PyTorch (Adam is related).

### Newton in $\mathbb{R}^n$ (Systems)

For $\vec f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^n$:

$$\vec x_{n+1} = \vec x_n - J(\vec x_n)^{-1} \vec f(\vec x_n),$$

where $J$ is the Jacobian. In practice, $J$ is not inverted—the linear system $J \Delta x = -f$ is solved by LU factorization. Applications: power flow in electrical grids, non-linear structural analysis, solving PDEs.

### Implied Volatility in Black-Scholes

Given market price $V_{\text{mkt}}$ of an option, find $\sigma$ such that $V_{BS}(\sigma) = V_{\text{mkt}}$. Define $g(\sigma) = V_{BS}(\sigma) - V_{\text{mkt}}$ and apply Newton. The derivative $g'(\sigma) = \partial V_{BS}/\partial \sigma$ is **vega**—available in closed form. Convergence in 3-5 iterations for 5-6 decimal places.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40"titulo="For a senior professional">

Newton-Raphson has theoretical extensions influencing computational complexity, functional analysis, and even number theory.

### Kantorovich's Theorem (1948)

Given $f: B \to B$ in a Banach space, Kantorovich provided quantitative conditions on $f(x_0)$, $f'(x_0)$, and $f''$ that guarantee the existence of a root and quadratic convergence of Newton—all inferred from **a single starting point** $x_0$. Foundation of modern functional numerical analysis.

### Newton on Riemannian Manifolds

In geodesic optimization, Newton generalizes via the Levi-Civita connection:

$$x_{n+1} = \exp_{x_n}\!\!\left(-H_n^{-1}\,\text{grad}\, f(x_n)\right),$$

where $\exp$ is the exponential map and $H_n$ is the Riemann Hessian operator. Appears in optimization on matrix manifolds (Stiefel, Grassmann), in geometric deep learning, and in optimal control on SO(3).

### Smale's alpha-theory

Stephen Smale (1981) gave a criterion $\alpha(f, x_0) < 3 - 2\sqrt{2}$ based solely on derivatives at $x_0$ that guarantees quadratic convergence of Newton. Links to the complexity theory of algorithms over $\mathbb{R}$—part of Smale's "18 problems for the 21st century."

### Newton Fractal and Complex Dynamics

For $f(z) = z^3 - 1$ in the complex plane, the boundaries between the three Newton basins are **Julia sets**—fractals with Hausdorff dimension $> 1$, uncountable, with infinite self-similar structure. Cayley (1879) posed the problem; Blanchard (1984) rigorously analyzed it.

### Historical Connection

Newton (1669, *De Analysi*) used the method for polynomial roots. Raphson (1690, *Analysis Aequationum*) published a simplified version. Simpson (1740) generalized to arbitrary functions. Cauchy (19th century) formalized with derivatives. Kantorovich (1948) gave rigorous analysis in Banach spaces. Today: every calculator, every physics simulator, every scientific library uses Newton at some level.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica"titulo="Practice — Recipe and Real-World Scenarios">

### 5-Step Recipe

1. Put the problem in the form $f(x) = 0$.
2. Calculate $f'$ analytically (or use automatic differentiation).
3. Choose $x_0$ by inspecting the graph or a few bisection steps.
4. Iterate: $x_{n+1} = x_n - f(x_n)/f'(x_n)$.
5. Stop when $|f(x_n)| < \varepsilon$ **and** $|x_{n+1} - x_n| < \delta$. Typically $\varepsilon = \delta = 10^{-8}$ is safe.

### Scenario 1: IRR for Solar Project

Solar project: initial investment R\$ 50,000, annual returns R\$ 12,000 for 6 years. The IRR $r$ solves:

$$f(r) = -50000 + \sum_{k=1}^{6} \frac{12000}{(1+r)^k} = 0.$$

With $x_0 = 0.10$ (10%): $f(0.10) > 0$, $f(0.30) < 0$. Newton converges in 5 iterations to $r \approx 0.1120$ (11.20% p.a.).

### Scenario 2: Calibration of Resistance via Non-linear Ohm's Law

Thermistor with $R(T) = R_0 \exp[B(1/T - 1/T_0)]$. Measurement gives $R = 4.7\,\text{k}\Omega$. Find $T$ given $R_0 = 10\,\text{k}\Omega$, $B = 3950$, $T_0 = 298$ K. Define $f(T) = R_0 e^{B(1/T - 1/T_0)} - 4700$. Newton in $T$ converges from $T_0 = 300$ K to the solution in 4 iterations.

### Scenario 3: Square Root in Hardware (FPGA/CPU)

Heron's algorithm—iteration $x_{n+1} = (x_n + a/x_n)/2$—is Newton applied to $f(x) = x^2 - a$. Modern processors (Intel, ARM) implement exactly this in microcode for the `sqrt` instruction. Starts with an approximation from an 8-bit lookup table and refines with 2-3 Newton steps for double precision (64 bits).

## Solved Examples

<Exemplo titulo="Basic Iteration: Estimating the Cube Root of 10 (Introduction)" numero="1">

**Problem.** Apply 3 iterations of Newton-Raphson to estimate $\sqrt[3]{10}$, starting from $x_0 = 2$.

**Strategy.** Define $f(x) = x^3 - 10$, so that $f(x) = 0 \Leftrightarrow x = \sqrt[3]{10}$. Calculate $f'(x) = 3x^2$ and apply the iteration $x_{n+1} = x_n - (x_n^3 - 10)/(3x_n^2)$.

**Solution.**

$x_0 = 2$

$x_1 = 2 - (8 - 10)/(12) = 2 + 2/12 = 2.166...$

$x_2 = 2.1667 - (2.1667^3 - 10)/(3 \cdot 2.1667^2) \approx 2.1544$

$x_3 \approx 2.15443...$

The exact value is $\sqrt[3]{10} = 2.154435...$ Already in the 3rd iteration, we have 5 correct digits.

**Verification.** $2.15443^3 \approx 9.9997 \approx 10$. Consistent.

**Source.** [APEX Calculus §4.4, Example 4.4.1](https://www.apexcalculus.com/) — CC-BY-NC.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Cycle and Failure: f(x) = x^3 - 2x + 2, x_0 = 0 (Understanding)" numero="2">

**Problem.** Show that Newton-Raphson with $f(x) = x^3 - 2x + 2$ and $x_0 = 0$ **does not converge**—it cycles.

**Strategy.** Calculate $f'(x) = 3x^2 - 2$ and observe what happens when iterating from $x_0 = 0$.

**Solution.**

$x_1 = 0 - f(0)/f'(0) = 0 - 2/(-2) = 1$

$x_2 = 1 - f(1)/f'(1) = 1 - (1 - 2 + 2)/(3 - 2) = 1 - 1/1 = 0$

$x_3 = 0$ (returned to the start).

The sequence oscillates indefinitely between $0$ and $1$. The only real root of $f$ is $x \approx -1.769$ — the guess $x_0 = 0$ is too far and on the wrong side of the geometry.

**Verification.** $f(0) = 2 > 0$, $f(1) = 1 > 0$, $f(-2) = -8 + 4 + 2 < 0$ — the root is in $(-2, -1)$, not near $0$.

**Source.** [OpenStax Calculus Vol. 1 §4.9, Example 4.9.4](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/4-9-newtons-method) — CC-BY-NC-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Multiple Root: Linear Convergence in (x-1)^2 (Intermediate)" numero="3">

**Problem.** Compare the convergence of Newton-Raphson with the standard formula and the modified formula $x_{n+1} = x_n - 2f(x_n)/f'(x_n)$ for $f(x) = (x-1)^2$, starting from $x_0 = 2$.

**Strategy.** $f(x) = (x-1)^2$, $f'(x) = 2(x-1)$. Standard iteration: $x_{n+1} = x_n - (x_n-1)^2/(2(x_n-1)) = x_n - (x_n-1)/2 = (x_n+1)/2$. Modified iteration with $m = 2$: $x_{n+1} = x_n - 2(x_n-1)^2/(2(x_n-1)) = x_n - (x_n - 1) = 1$ (converges in **1 step**).

**Solution.**

Standard formula: $x_0 = 2$, $x_1 = 1.5$, $x_2 = 1.25$, $x_3 = 1.125$, $x_4 = 1.0625$. The error halves at each step: **linear convergence** with ratio $1/2$.

Modified formula: $x_1 = 2 - 2 \cdot 1/2 = 1$. Converged in 1 iteration.

**Verification.** Error in the standard iteration after $k$ steps: $e_k = (1/2)^k$. In 10 steps, $e_{10} = 2^{-10} \approx 10^{-3}$. With the modified formula, $e_1 = 0$ exactly.

**Source.** [APEX Calculus §4.4, Discussion after Theorem 4.4.1](https://www.apexcalculus.com/) — CC-BY-NC.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Investment IRR: Root of a Polynomial in Rate (Modeling)" numero="4">

**Problem.** An investment costs R\$ 1000 and returns R\$ 400 at the end of year 1, R\$ 400 at the end of year 2, and R\$ 300 at the end of year 3. Find the IRR using Newton-Raphson.

**Strategy.** The IRR $r$ is the root of $f(r) = -1000 + 400/(1+r) + 400/(1+r)^2 + 300/(1+r)^3$. Calculate $f'(r)$ by differentiation and apply Newton with $x_0 = 0.10$.

**Solution.** Let $u = 1/(1+r)$. Then $f = -1000 + 400u + 400u^2 + 300u^3$.

$f'(r) = -400u^2 - 800u^3 - 900u^4$ (deriving $u$ wrt $r$: $du/dr = -u^2$).

Iteration with $r_0 = 0.10$ ($u_0 \approx 0.9091$):

$f(0.10) = -1000 + 363.6 + 330.6 + 225.4 = -80.4$

$f'(0.10) \approx -330 - 657 - 676 = -1663$

$r_1 = 0.10 - (-80.4)/(-1663) \approx 0.1517$

After 3 iterations: $r \approx 0.1493$ (14.93% p.a.).

**Verification.** $f(0.1493) = -1000 + 400/1.1493 + 400/1.1493^2 + 300/1.1493^3 \approx 0.01$ (practically zero).

**Source.** [OpenStax Calculus Vol. 1 §4.9, Exercises 4.9](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/4-9-newtons-method) — CC-BY-NC-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="2x2 System Newton: Non-linear Market Equilibrium (Challenge)" numero="5">

**Problem.** Non-linear supply and demand: $Q_d = 100 - p^2$ and $Q_s = 10 p^{0.5}$. Find the equilibrium $(p^*, Q^*)$ via Newton for systems.

**Strategy.** Define $f_1(p, Q) = Q - 100 + p^2 = 0$ and $f_2(p, Q) = Q - 10\sqrt{p} = 0$. The Newton system is $J \Delta \vec{x} = -\vec{f}$, with Jacobian $J = \partial(f_1, f_2)/\partial(p, Q)$.

**Solution.**

$$J = \begin{pmatrix} 2p & 1 \\ -5/\sqrt{p} & 1 \end{pmatrix}$$

Initial guess: $p_0 = 5$, $Q_0 = 75$.

Step 1: $f_1(5, 75) = 75 - 100 + 25 = 0$; $f_2(5, 75) = 75 - 10\sqrt{5} \approx 52.6$.

Step 2: Solve $J \Delta \vec x = -(0, 52.6)^T$.

$J = \begin{pmatrix} 10 & 1 \\ -2.24 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, $\det J \approx 12.24$.

$\Delta p = -52.6/12.24 \approx -4.3$, $\Delta Q \approx 43$.
