<Porta nivel="formal" titulo="Rigorous definition">

### Density and parameters

<Definicao titulo="Normal distribution N(mu, sigma^2)">

$X \sim \mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ if $X$ has density:

$$f(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\,\exp\!\left(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right), \quad x \in \mathbb{R}$$

- $\mu \in \mathbb{R}$: **location** parameter (mean = median = mode).
- $\sigma > 0$: **scale** parameter (standard deviation).
- $\sigma^2$: variance.

The normalization $\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} f(x)\,dx = 1$ follows from the classic Gaussian result: $\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} e^{-x^2/2}\,dx = \sqrt{2\pi}$ (proof via polar coordinates).

</Definicao>

> "If $X$ is a random variable and $X$ has a normal distribution with mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$, we write $X \sim N(\mu, \sigma)$. The mean $\mu$ is the center of the symmetric curve, and the standard deviation $\sigma$ gives the spread." — [OpenStax Statistics §6.1](https://openstax.org/books/statistics/pages/6-1-the-standard-normal-distribution)

<Definicao titulo="Standard normal and standardization">

$Z \sim \mathcal N(0, 1)$. CDF:

$$\Phi(z) = P(Z \leq z) = \int_{-\infty}^z \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-t^2/2}\,dt$$

No elementary closed-form — tabulated or calculated numerically.

**Standardization**: if $X \sim \mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2)$, then $Z = (X - \mu)/\sigma \sim \mathcal N(0,1)$.

**Symmetry**: $\Phi(-z) = 1 - \Phi(z)$.

**Notable quantiles**:
- $z_{0{,}975} = 1{,}96$ (95% bilateral CI).
- $z_{0{,}95} = 1{,}645$ (95% unilateral CI).
- $z_{0{,}995} = 2{,}576$ (99% bilateral CI).

</Definicao>

> "Normal distributions are symmetric around their mean... The area under a normal distribution curve within one standard deviation of the mean is approximately 68%, within two standard deviations is approximately 95%, and within three standard deviations is approximately 99.7%." — [OpenIntro Statistics §3.5](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/)

<Teorema titulo="68-95-99.7 rule (sigma rule)">

For $X \sim \mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2)$:

$$P(\mu - \sigma \leq X \leq \mu + \sigma) \approx 0{,}6827$$

$$P(\mu - 2\sigma \leq X \leq \mu + 2\sigma) \approx 0{,}9545$$

$$P(\mu - 3\sigma \leq X \leq \mu + 3\sigma) \approx 0{,}9973$$

</Teorema>

<Teorema titulo="Closure properties">

1. **Linear combination**: $aX + b \sim \mathcal N(a\mu + b,\; a^2\sigma^2)$.
2. **Independent sum**: if $X \sim \mathcal N(\mu_1, \sigma_1^2)$ and $Y \sim \mathcal N(\mu_2, \sigma_2^2)$ are independent, then $X + Y \sim \mathcal N(\mu_1+\mu_2,\; \sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2)$.
3. **Sample mean**: $\bar X = (1/n)\sum X_i$ with $X_i$ iid from $\mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ satisfies $\bar X \sim \mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2/n)$.

</Teorema>

<Insight>

The normal distribution maximizes **differential entropy** among all distributions with fixed mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$. This provides a Bayesian justification: assuming normality is assuming the least amount of additional information given the mean and variance constraints — the "most agnostic" distribution compatible with what is known.

</Insight>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

Imagine measuring the height of **one thousand children** your age. Most are close to the average height. Very few are very short, and very few are very tall.

If you make a graph, a **symmetric mountain** will appear — the famous **bell**. This mountain is the normal distribution.

The closer to the center, the more children there are. At the edges, there is almost no one.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

The normal distribution is the most common in nature:

- Human heights
- Laboratory measurement errors
- Grades on a well-designed test
- Sizes of leaves on a tree

Two numbers completely describe the curve:

- $\mu$ (the **mean**): where the peak is.
- $\sigma$ (the **standard deviation**): how wide the curve is.

**Magic rule 68-95-99.7**:
- 68% of the data falls between $\mu - \sigma$ and $\mu + \sigma$.
- 95% between $\mu - 2\sigma$ and $\mu + 2\sigma$.
- 99.7% between $\mu - 3\sigma$ and $\mu + 3\sigma$.

Example: heights of Brazilian adults: $\mu = 170$ cm, $\sigma = 8$ cm. So 95% fall between 154 and 186 cm.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old">

The normal distribution is so important for three reasons:

1. **It arises naturally**: when many small, independent factors add up, the result tends toward normal (Central Limit Theorem — next lesson).
2. **Simple algebra**: sums and averages of independent normal variables are normal.
3. **It underpins inferential statistics**: CIs, hypothesis tests, regression.

### How to use the Z-table

To calculate $P(X \leq c)$ in any $\mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2)$:

1. Standardize: $z = (c - \mu)/\sigma$.
2. Consult the $\Phi(z)$ table (or use `NORM.S.DIST(z, TRUE)`).
3. For $P(X \geq c)$: use symmetry $\Phi(-z) = 1 - \Phi(z)$.

**Example**: test with $\mu = 70$, $\sigma = 10$. $P(X \geq 85)$?
$z = (85-70)/10 = 1{,}5$. $P(Z \geq 1{,}5) = 1 - \Phi(1{,}5) \approx 1 - 0{,}933 = 0{,}067$ (6.7%).

### History

Carl Friedrich Gauss (1809) used the normal distribution to model astronomical observation errors ("normal errors"). Abraham de Moivre (1733) had already derived the curve as a limit of the binomial. That is why "Gaussian" and "normal" are synonyms.

### When the normal distribution does not apply

- Income and real estate prices (long-tail distribution).
- Rare counts (Poisson).
- Waiting times (exponential).
- Financial returns (heavier tail than the normal — "black swans").

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

### Confidence interval for the mean

For $X_1, \ldots, X_n$ iid from $\mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ with known $\sigma$:

$$\bar X \sim \mathcal N\!\left(\mu, \frac{\sigma^2}{n}\right) \implies \bar X \pm 1{,}96\,\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n} \text{ is a 95\% CI for } \mu$$

With unknown $\sigma$: replace with $s$ and use the **Student's t-distribution** (lessons 79+).

### Z-test for the mean

$H_0: \mu = \mu_0$. Test statistic:

$$z = \frac{\bar X - \mu_0}{\sigma/\sqrt n}$$

Reject $H_0$ (bilateral, $\alpha = 5\%$) if $|z| > 1{,}96$.

### Tolerance engineering (Six Sigma)

Part with diameter $\mathcal N(10{,}00;\; 0{,}02^2)$ mm. Tolerance $10{,}00 \pm 0{,}05$ mm.

$z = 0{,}05/0{,}02 = 2{,}5$. $P(|Z| > 2{,}5) = 2\Phi(-2{,}5) \approx 1{,}24\%$.

For 1000 parts: $\sim 12$ rejects. For Six Sigma (3.4 ppm): $z \approx 4{,}5$.

### Gaussian Mixture Models (GMM)

$$f(x) = \sum_k \pi_k\,\mathcal N(x;\, \mu_k, \sigma_k^2)$$

Models heterogeneous populations (e.g., heights of mixed populations, cluster scores). Fitted by the EM algorithm. Basis of Gaussian Mixture Models in ML.

### Q-Q plot (normality diagnostic)

Plot sample quantiles vs. theoretical quantiles of $\mathcal N(0,1)$. The line $y = x$ indicates normality. Curves indicate skewness or heavy tails (kurtosis). Complemented by the Shapiro-Wilk test ($n < 50$) or Anderson-Darling.

### Python implementation

```python
from scipy.stats import norm
norm.cdf(1.96)                       # 0.975
norm.ppf(0.975)                      # 1.96
norm.pdf(0)                          # 0.3989 (peak of N(0,1))
norm.cdf(85, loc=70, scale=10)       # P(X <= 85) with mu=70, sigma=10
norm.rvs(loc=0, scale=1, size=1000)  # samples
```

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

### Characterization by characteristic function

The characteristic function of $\mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ is:

$$\varphi(t) = E[e^{itX}] = \exp\!\left(i\mu t - \frac{\sigma^2 t^2}{2}\right)$$

Unique among distributions with finite variance with a quadratic $\log \varphi$. Allows proving the CLT via Lévy's continuity theorem: if $\varphi_{X_n}(t) \to e^{-t^2/2}$ pointwise, then $X_n \to Z \sim \mathcal N(0,1)$ in distribution.

### Multivariate normal

$\mathbf X \sim \mathcal N(\boldsymbol\mu, \Sigma)$ with mean vector $\boldsymbol\mu$ and positive semi-definite covariance matrix $\Sigma$. Density:

$$f(\mathbf x) = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{p/2}|\Sigma|^{1/2}} \exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf x - \boldsymbol\mu)^\top \Sigma^{-1} (\mathbf x - \boldsymbol\mu)\right)$$

Marginals and conditionals remain normal. Basis of PCA, multivariate linear regression, Kalman filter, Gaussian processes, and Bayesian neural networks.

### Light tail vs. heavy tail

The normal tail decays as $e^{-x^2/2}$ — super-exponentially fast. Events at $5\sigma$ should occur $< 10^{-6}$ times per million observations. In finance: LTCM (1998) and the 2008 crisis involved "$10\sigma$" events. The lesson: distributions with **heavy tails** (Student's t, Pareto, stable Lévy) model extreme risks better.

### Maximum entropy

Among all distributions on $\mathbb{R}$ with fixed $E[X] = \mu$ and $E[X^2] = \sigma^2 + \mu^2$, the normal distribution maximizes differential entropy $h(X) = -\int f \log f\,dx$. Justification for the maximum entropy principle in Bayesian statistical modeling.

### Brownian motion and Black-Scholes

The Wiener process $W_t$ has increments $W_{t+\Delta} - W_t \sim \mathcal N(0, \Delta)$, which are independent. It is the continuous foundation of the CLT: limit of sums of iid jumps. The Black-Scholes formula (lesson 119) explicitly uses $W_t$ and the standard normal $\Phi$ to price options. See [1997 Nobel Prize in Economics](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/1997/summary/).

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practical application">

### 4-step recipe

1. **Identify $\mu$ and $\sigma$** of the population.
2. **Standardize**: $z = (x - \mu)/\sigma$.
3. **Consult table** $\Phi(z)$ or use spreadsheet/Python.
4. **Use symmetry**: $P(X \geq c) = 1 - P(X \leq c) = \Phi(-z)$.

### In spreadsheets (Excel / LibreOffice)

| Function | Result |
|---|---|
| `NORM.S.DIST(z, TRUE)` | $\Phi(z)$ |
| `NORM.DIST(x, mu, sigma, TRUE)` | $P(X \leq x)$ |
| `NORM.S.INV(prob)` | Quantile $z$ |
| `NORM.INV(prob, mu, sigma)` | Quantile of $\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)$ |

### When NOT to use the normal distribution

- Strictly positive data with long tails: **log-normal** (salaries, prices).
- Rare counts: **Poisson**.
- Time until event: **exponential** or **Weibull**.
- Daily financial returns: **Student's t** (heavier tail).
- Proportions: **binomial** or **beta**.

### Quick normality check

1. Histogram symmetric and bell-shaped?
2. 68% of data in $[\bar x - s;\; \bar x + s]$?
3. Straight Q-Q plot?
4. Shapiro-Wilk test ($n < 50$) with $p$-value $> 0{,}05$.

## Solved examples

<Exemplo numero="76.1" titulo="Standardization and Z-table" dificuldade="basico">

**Problem.** $X \sim \mathcal N(70, 10^2)$. Calculate $P(X \leq 85)$.

**Strategy.** Standardize and consult the $\Phi$ table.

**Resolution.**

$$z = \frac{85 - 70}{10} = 1{,}5$$

$$P(X \leq 85) = P(Z \leq 1{,}5) = \Phi(1{,}5) \approx 0{,}9332$$

**Verification.** 85 is $1{,}5\sigma$ above the mean. By the 68-95-99.7 rule, $\approx 84\%$ are below $\mu + \sigma = 80$, and even more below $\mu + 1{,}5\sigma = 85$. The result $\approx 93\%$ is plausible.

**Source.** [OpenIntro Statistics §3.5](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/) — CC-BY-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="76.2" titulo="68-95-99.7 rule applied" dificuldade="basico">

**Problem.** IQ follows $\mathcal N(100, 15^2)$. What % of the population has an IQ between 85 and 115? And above 130?

**Strategy.** Identify positions in $\sigma$ and apply the rule.

**Resolution.**

- 85 = $\mu - \sigma$ and 115 = $\mu + \sigma$ ($\pm 1\sigma$ interval): $P(85 \leq X \leq 115) \approx 68\%$.
- 130 = $\mu + 2\sigma$: $P(X > 130) = (1 - 0{,}9545)/2 \approx 2{,}28\%$.

**Verification.** The 68% between $\pm 1\sigma$ and 2.28% above $+2\sigma$ are tabulated results of the standard normal. The IQ scale was deliberately calibrated to have $\sigma = 15$ and make these percentiles meaningful.

**Source.** [OpenStax Statistics §6.1](https://openstax.org/books/statistics/pages/6-1-the-standard-normal-distribution) — CC-BY.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="76.3" titulo="Engineering tolerances" dificuldade="intermediario">

**Problem.** Parts with diameter $\mathcal N(10{,}00;\; 0{,}02^2)$ mm. Specification $10{,}00 \pm 0{,}05$ mm. What fraction is rejected?

**Strategy.** Calculate $P(|X - 10| > 0{,}05)$ via standardization.

**Resolution.**

$$z = \frac{0{,}05}{0{,}02} = 2{,}5$$

$$P(|X - 10| > 0{,}05) = P(|Z| > 2{,}5) = 2\Phi(-2{,}5) = 2 \times 0{,}0062 = 0{,}0124$$

About $1{,}24\%$ of the parts are rejected — approximately **12 per 1000**.

**Verification.** $z = 2{,}5$ is between $2\sigma$ (2.28% out) and $3\sigma$ (0.27% out). The result 1.24% is consistent with this interval. For Six Sigma (3.4 ppm), $z \approx 4{,}5$ would be required.

**Source.** [OpenStax Statistics §6.3](https://openstax.org/books/statistics/pages/6-3-estimating-the-binomial-with-the-normal-distribution) — CC-BY.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="76.4" titulo="Confidence interval for the mean" dificuldade="intermediario">

**Problem.** Sample of $n = 25$ parts, $\bar X = 105$ mm, $\sigma = 10$ mm (known). Construct a 95% CI for $\mu$.

**Strategy.** Use $\bar X \sim \mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2/n)$ and $z_{0{,}975} = 1{,}96$.

**Resolution.**

$$SE = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n} = \frac{10}{\sqrt{25}} = 2$$

$$IC_{95\%} = \left[\bar X - 1{,}96 \cdot SE;\; \bar X + 1{,}96 \cdot SE\right] = [105 - 3{,}92;\; 105 + 3{,}92] = [101{,}08;\; 108{,}92]$$

**Verification.** The total width of the CI is $2 \times 3{,}92 = 7{,}84$ mm. Since $\sigma = 10$ and $n = 25$, the standard error is 2 — half the population deviation, reduced by $\sqrt{25}$. The interval covers the true mean in 95% of samples.

**Source.** [OpenIntro Statistics §4.2](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/) — CC-BY-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="76.5" titulo="Derivation: Gaussian integral" dificuldade="avancado">

**Problem.** Demonstrate that $\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} e^{-x^2/2}\,dx = \sqrt{2\pi}$ using the polar coordinates trick.

**Strategy.** Calculate $I^2$ where $I = \int e^{-x^2/2}\,dx$ and convert to polar.

**Resolution.** Let $I = \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} e^{-x^2/2}\,dx$.

$$I^2 = \left(\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} e^{-x^2/2}\,dx\right)\!\left(\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} e^{-y^2/2}\,dy\right) = \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\!\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} e^{-(x^2+y^2)/2}\,dx\,dy$$

Polar: $x = r\cos\theta$, $y = r\sin\theta$, Jacobian $r$:

$$I^2 = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\int_0^{+\infty} e^{-r^2/2}\,r\,dr\,d\theta = 2\pi \int_0^{+\infty} r\,e^{-r^2/2}\,dr$$

Substitution $u = r^2/2$: $\int_0^\infty r e^{-r^2/2}\,dr = [-e^{-r^2/2}]_0^\infty = 1$.

Thus $I^2 = 2\pi$, therefore $I = \sqrt{2\pi}$.

**Verification.** The density $f(x) = e^{-x^2/2}/\sqrt{2\pi}$ integrates to 1 over $\mathbb R$ — consistent with the normality of $Z$.

**Source.** [Introduction to Probability — Grinstead, Snell §5.2](https://chance.dartmouth.edu/teaching_aids/books_articles/probability_book/book.html) — GNU FDL.

</Exemplo>
