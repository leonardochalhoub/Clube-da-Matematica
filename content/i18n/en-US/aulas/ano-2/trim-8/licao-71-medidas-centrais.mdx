### Descriptive statistics: the summarization problem

Given a set of $n$ observations $x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n$, we want a single number that represents the "center" of the distribution. There is no single answer — there are three different questions, three different answers.

<Definicao titulo="Arithmetic mean">

For data $x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n$:

<Equation explicacao="Mean: sum of all values divided by the number of observations n." latex="\bar{x} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i" />

For **grouped data** with values $x_i$ and absolute frequencies $f_i$:

<Equation explicacao="Weighted mean by frequencies: sum of each value multiplied by its frequency, divided by the total number of observations." latex="\bar{x} = \frac{\sum_{i} f_i \, x_i}{\sum_{i} f_i}" />

</Definicao>

> "The sample mean can be calculated for any quantitative variable. For a discrete distribution, the mean is the sum of each value multiplied by its probability; for a continuous distribution, the corresponding integral." — [OpenIntro Statistics, §1.6](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/)

<Definicao titulo="Median">

Order the data: $x_{(1)} \leq x_{(2)} \leq \cdots \leq x_{(n)}$.

- If $n$ is **odd**: $\mathrm{Med} = x_{((n+1)/2)}$
- If $n$ is **even**: $\mathrm{Med} = \dfrac{x_{(n/2)} + x_{(n/2 + 1)}}{2}$

The median is the 50th percentile — half the observations fall below, half above.

</Definicao>

<Definicao titulo="Mode">

Value (or values) with the greatest absolute frequency. A distribution can be:

- **Unimodal**: one mode only.
- **Bimodal**: two modes (equal frequencies in both, higher than the others).
- **Multimodal**: more than two modes.
- **Amodal** (or no mode): all frequencies equal.

</Definicao>

### Algebraic properties of the mean

<Teorema titulo="Deviations from the mean sum to zero">

For any dataset $x_1, \ldots, x_n$ with mean $\bar{x}$:

<Equation explicacao="The sum of deviations around the mean is always zero: positive and negative deviations cancel exactly. This is the sense in which the mean is the center of gravity of the data." latex="\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - \bar{x}) = 0" />

**Proof.** $\sum(x_i - \bar{x}) = \sum x_i - n\bar{x} = n\bar{x} - n\bar{x} = 0$. $\square$

</Teorema>

<Teorema titulo="Mean minimizes squared error">

Among all constants $c \in \mathbb{R}$, the function $f(c) = \sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - c)^2$ is minimized at $c = \bar{x}$.

**Proof.** Expand: $f(c) = \sum(x_i - \bar{x} + \bar{x} - c)^2 = \sum(x_i-\bar{x})^2 + n(\bar{x}-c)^2$. The first term is constant; the second is $\geq 0$ and vanishes at $c = \bar{x}$. $\square$

</Teorema>

> "The mean minimizes the sum of squared deviations (L^2 error). The median minimizes the sum of absolute deviations (L^1 error). This distinction has profound consequences in regression and machine learning." — [OpenIntro Statistics, §2.1](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/)

<Insight>

The order Mode–Median–Mean in skewed distributions is an **empirical rule**, not a theorem. In highly skewed or multimodal distributions, the relationship may reverse. Always use the histogram as a visual check.

</Insight>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

Imagine you and your friends have bags of candy:

- Your bag: 3 candies
- Friend 1: 5 candies
- Friend 2: 5 candies
- Friend 3: 7 candies
- Friend 4: 10 candies

**Mean**: put all the candies in a pile (3 + 5 + 5 + 7 + 10 = 30) and divide equally among 5 bags. Each one gets 6 candies. That's the mean: 6.

**Median**: line up the bags from smallest to largest: 3, 5, **5**, 7, 10. The one in the middle is 5. That's the median.

**Mode**: which number appears most often? The 5 appears twice, all the others once. So the mode is 5.

Each one tells a **different story** about the bags.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

When you have several numbers and want to **summarize in one**, you use measures of central tendency.

**Mean** $\bar{x}$: add them all up and divide by the total.

Class grades: 6, 7, 8, 8, 9. Sum = 38. Total = 5 students. Mean = 38 / 5 = 7.6.

**Median**: order and pick the middle one.

Ordered grades: 6, 7, **8**, 8, 9. There are 5 grades, the middle is the 3rd: median = 8.

If you have an even number of values — for example, 6, 7, 8, 9 — pick the two middle ones (7 and 8) and average them: (7 + 8) / 2 = 7.5.

**Mode**: what appears most often. In the list 6, 7, 8, 8, 9, the number 8 appears twice. Mode = 8.

**When to use which?**

- Salaries at a company where the owner earns US\$ 500k and everyone else earns US\$ 3k: the **mean** comes out huge and doesn't represent anyone. Use **median**.
- Most popular car color: you can't take the "mean of colors." Use **mode**.
- Class grades with no extreme values: **mean**.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old teen">

**Descriptive** statistics starts here. The three central measures answer distinct questions about the same dataset.

### Mean

$$\bar{x} = \frac{x_1 + x_2 + \cdots + x_n}{n} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i$$

**Strength:** uses all the information; has elegant algebraic properties (linearity: $\overline{ax + b} = a\bar{x} + b$).

**Weakness:** sensitive to **outliers**. Salary mean at a company with 10 workers earning US\$ 5,000 and 1 CEO earning US\$ 1,000,000: $\bar{x} \approx$ US\$ 95,000. Represents no one.

### Median

Middle value after ordering. **Robust**: replacing the largest value with an even larger one doesn't change the median.

In the salary example: median = US\$ 5,000. Represents the typical worker.

### Mode

Most frequent value. Useful for **categorical** variables (color, brand, party) and for revealing peaks in **multimodal** distributions (ex.: distribution of height combining men and women is bimodal, with two peaks).

### The journalist's rule

The press almost always uses **mean** (it's "the statistic"). But:

- In **income, real-estate price, wait time**: prefer **median**.
- In **categorical variable**: use **mode**.
- In symmetric data with no outliers: **mean** is a good choice.

### Skewness and order of measures

Long tail to the **right** (ex.: family income): **mode less than median less than mean** — the tail pulls the mean upward.

Long tail to the **left**: **mean less than median less than mode**.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

Central measures are the basic vocabulary of all quantitative analysis.

### Variational characterization (the key)

| Measure | Minimizes | Norm |
|---|---|---|
| Mean | $\displaystyle\sum_{i}(x_i - c)^2$ | $L^2$ |
| Median | $\displaystyle\sum_{i}\lvert x_i - c \rvert$ | $L^1$ |
| Mode | $\displaystyle\sum_{i} \mathbf{1}_{x_i \neq c}$ | $L^0$ (approx.) |

**The choice of central measure is the choice of norm.** In ML: least-squares regression (MSE) presupposes $L^2$ and the optimal estimator is the mean; least-absolute-deviations regression (MAE) presupposes $L^1$ and the optimal estimator is the median; Huber loss combines the two.

### Robustness: breakdown point

The **breakdown point** of an estimator is the fraction of data that can be corrupted before the estimator diverges.

- Mean: breakdown point = $1/n \to 0$. A single sufficiently extreme outlier destroys the mean.
- Median: breakdown point = $1/2$. Resists up to 50% contamination.

**Trimmed mean**: discard the outer $k\%$ before calculating. Compromise between efficiency (close to the mean) and robustness (close to the median).

### Pathological cases

**Cauchy distribution** $f(x) = 1/[\pi(1+x^2)]$: median = 0, but the mean **does not exist** — the integral $\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} x f(x)\, dx$ diverges. The median is the only meaningful measure of central tendency.

**Log-normal distribution** $\ln X \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)$: mode $= e^{\mu - \sigma^2}$, median $= e^\mu$, mean $= e^{\mu + \sigma^2/2}$. The order mode less than median less than mean is guaranteed for $\sigma > 0$.

### Law of Large Numbers

For $X_i$ i.i.d. with $E[X] = \mu$ finite: $\bar{X}_n \to \mu$ a.s. (Kolmogorov strong LLN). Therefore the sample mean is a **consistent** estimator of $\mu$.

The sample median also converges to the population median, at rate $\sqrt{n}$ with asymptotic variance $1/(4f(m)^2)$ where $f(m)$ is the density at the median point. When $f(m)$ is large (sharp peak), the median is more efficient than the mean.

### Computation

- Mean: $O(n)$ — one pass.
- Median: $O(n)$ via QuickSelect (median of medians guarantees worst case); $O(n \log n)$ by sorting.
- Mode: $O(n)$ with hash; $O(n \log n)$ by sorting.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

### M-estimators — the unification

Huber (1964) proposes minimizing $\sum \rho(x_i - c)$ for convex function $\rho$:

- $\rho(t) = t^2/2$ → mean.
- $\rho(t) = \lvert t \rvert$ → median.
- $\rho(t) = t^2/2$ for $\lvert t \rvert \leq k$, $k\lvert t\rvert - k^2/2$ otherwise → **Huber**, robust.
- $\rho$ by Tukey biweight → even greater resistance to extreme outliers.

This family (M-estimators) is the foundation of modern **robust statistics**. In regression: ridge ($L^2$ + regularization), lasso ($L^1$ → sparsity), Huber loss (robust regression).

### Fréchet median on manifolds

In $\mathbb{R}^n$: spatial median = $\arg\min_c \sum \lvert\lvert x_i - c \rvert\rvert_2$. On Riemannian manifolds: $\arg\min_c \sum d_g(x_i, c)^p$ for $p = 1$ (Fréchet median) or $p = 2$ (Fréchet mean). The Fréchet mean appears in computing means on Lie groups (rotations, poses), computer vision, signal processing on manifolds.

### Federated learning and robustness

In federated learning, the usual aggregation is the mean of client gradients. A malicious client can send arbitrarily large gradients and corrupt the model. Replacing the mean with **coordinate-wise median** or **Krum** (selection of the gradient closest to the others) eliminates this attack vector, at the cost of slower convergence.

### Simpson's paradox

Trends reverse when aggregating groups. Classic: success rate of medical treatment appears lower at hospital A, but when disaggregating by case severity, A is better in each category. The median does not resolve the paradox — the problem is in variable confusion, not in the measure chosen.

### Historical timeline

- Galton (1880s): introduces median, percentiles, correlation — statistics as science.
- Pearson (1894): defines moment, skewness, kurtosis. Frames the mean as central.
- Fisher (1922): consistency, sufficiency, efficiency — theory of estimators.
- Tukey (1962): Exploratory Data Analysis. Boxplot, median return to center stage.
- Huber (1964): M-estimators. Robustness formalized.
- Lugosi & Mendelson (2019): median-of-means for heavy-tailed distributions — optimal convergence at $O(1/\sqrt{n})$ even without normality assumption.

[Nobel Prize in Economic Sciences 2000 — Heckman and McFadden](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/2000/summary/) — microeconometric methods that rely on robust estimation of central location for heterogeneous populations.

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practical application">

### Quick selection guide

| Situation | Measure |
|---|---|
| Symmetric data, no outliers | Mean |
| Skewed data (income, price, wait time) | Median |
| Categorical variable (color, brand, party) | Mode |
| Outliers present and not removable | Median |
| Multimodal distribution | Report all modes |

**Operational rule:**

1. Make a histogram. Always.
2. Identify skewness (long tail to right or left).
3. Identify outliers (points beyond $\pm 3$ standard deviations or beyond $1.5 \times \text{IQR}$ from boxplot).
4. Choose per the table above.
5. Report both (mean + median) when relevant — in income, the IBGE does exactly that.

### Real-world examples

**IBGE — Per capita household income (2022):** median = US\$ 264; mean = US\$ 325. The difference of US\$ 61 reveals the skewness: a minority of very high incomes pulls the mean upward, while the median better represents the typical resident.

**ENEM 2023 — Mathematics:** average score ≈ 527 points, median ≈ 513. Distribution with mild right skew — INEP's report publishes both.

**E-commerce delivery time and server latency have heavy tails (1% of requests very slow). The median of the 99th percentile (p99) is the standard SLA metric in industry — not the mean.

### Grouped data (frequency table)

When data come in intervals:

- **Mean:** $\bar{x} = \sum f_i x_i^* / \sum f_i$ where $x_i^*$ is the midpoint of the interval.
- **Median:** locate the median class (where cumulative frequency crosses $n/2$), then interpolate linearly within it.
- **Mode:** interval of highest frequency. The Czuber formula estimates the exact value within the modal interval using the frequencies of neighboring intervals.

## Worked examples

<Exemplo titulo="Direct calculation of the three measures (introduction)" numero="1">

**Problem:** The time (in minutes) it took seven students to solve an ENEM question was: 8, 12, 7, 15, 12, 10, 9. Calculate the mean, median, and mode.

**Strategy:** For the mean, add and divide. For the median, we must order first. For the mode, identify the most frequent value.

**Solution:**

Ordered data: 7, 8, 9, **10**, 12, 12, 15.

**Mean:**
$$\bar{x} = \frac{7 + 8 + 9 + 10 + 12 + 12 + 15}{7} = \frac{73}{7} \approx 10.43 \text{ min}$$

**Median:** $n = 7$ (odd), middle position = $(7+1)/2 = 4$. The 4th value in the ordered list is **10**. Median = 10 min.

**Mode:** the value 12 appears twice; all others, once. Mode = 12 min.

**Verification:** The mean (10.43) lies between the median (10) and the mode (12). The distribution has mild right skew driven by the value 15 — consistent with mode less than or equal to median less than or equal to mean.

**Source.** [OpenIntro Statistics §1.6](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/) — CC-BY-SA 4.0. Exercise adapted from the introductory descriptive statistics section.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Mean for grouped data in frequency table (intermediate)" numero="2">

**Problem:** A survey of 40 students about weekly hours spent on extracurricular study produced the table:

| Hours | Frequency ($f_i$) |
|---|---|
| 0 to 4 | 5 |
| 4 to 8 | 12 |
| 8 to 12 | 15 |
| 12 to 16 | 6 |
| 16 to 20 | 2 |

Calculate the arithmetic mean using the midpoints of the intervals.

**Strategy:** For grouped data, replace each interval with its midpoint and apply the weighted mean formula.

**Solution:**

Midpoints: 2, 6, 10, 14, 18.

$$\bar{x} = \frac{5 \cdot 2 + 12 \cdot 6 + 15 \cdot 10 + 6 \cdot 14 + 2 \cdot 18}{40} = \frac{10 + 72 + 150 + 84 + 36}{40} = \frac{352}{40} = 8.8 \text{ hours}$$

**Verification:** The value 8.8 falls within the interval of highest frequency (8 to 12 hours), which contains 15 of 40 students. Consistent. The sum of frequencies is $5 + 12 + 15 + 6 + 2 = 40$. Correct.

**Source.** [OpenStax Introductory Statistics 2e §2.5](https://openstax.org/books/introductory-statistics-2e/pages/2-5-measures-of-the-center-of-the-data) — CC-BY 4.0. Adapted from the grouped data example in classes.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Median with n even and outlier impact (intermediate-advanced)" numero="3">

**Problem:** The monthly salaries (in USD thousands) of 8 startup employees are: 4, 4, 5, 5, 6, 7, 8, 60.

(a) Calculate mean and median.
(b) The CEO (USD 60k) leaves and is replaced by someone earning USD 7k. Recalculate.
(c) Which measure is more appropriate to describe the salary of the "typical employee" in both cases?

**Strategy:** Calculate both measures before and after the swap, and compare the sensitivity of each to the outlier.

**Solution:**

**(a) With the CEO (USD 60k):**

Ordered data: 4, 4, 5, **5, 6**, 7, 8, 60.

Mean: $(4 + 4 + 5 + 5 + 6 + 7 + 8 + 60)/8 = 99/8 = 12.375$ thousand.

Median ($n = 8$ even): average of 4th and 5th values = $(5 + 6)/2 = 5.5$ thousand.

**(b) Without the CEO, with USD 7k:**

Ordered data: 4, 4, 5, **5, 6**, 7, 7, 8.

Mean: $(4 + 4 + 5 + 5 + 6 + 7 + 7 + 8)/8 = 46/8 = 5.75$ thousand.

Median: $(5 + 6)/2 = 5.5$ thousand.

**(c) Comparison:** The mean jumped from 5.75 to 12.375 — a change of +115% — with the arrival of a single outlier. The median remained 5.5 thousand in both cases. To represent the salary of the typical employee, the **median is more appropriate** in the presence of extreme outliers.

**Verification:** In (a), only 1 of 8 employees (12.5%) earns above the mean. The median better reflects the bulk of salaries.

**Source.** [OpenIntro Statistics §1.6](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/) — CC-BY-SA 4.0. Context adapted to startup salary data.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Choice of central measure: income, wait time, color (modeling)" numero="4">

**Problem:** For each situation below, identify the most appropriate measure of central tendency and justify:

(a) Per capita income in Brazilian municipalities for comparison between states.
(b) Wait time (in minutes) at the emergency room of a public hospital.
(c) Most installed operating system in public school computer labs.

**Strategy:** For each variable, identify: is it quantitative or categorical? If quantitative, are there expected skewness or outliers?

**Solution:**

**(a) Per capita income:** quantitative variable with expected right skew (very wealthy municipalities like São Paulo and Brasília pull the mean upward, but most municipalities have low income). **Median** is more appropriate — the IBGE uses median for exactly this reason.

**(b) Wait time at the emergency room:** quantitative variable with heavy right tail (most patients are seen in 1 to 3 hours, but critical cases may wait 12 or more hours, distorting the mean). **Median** or the 90th percentile are standard metrics in hospital management.

**(c) Operating system:** **categorical** variable — no natural ordering among Windows, Linux, macOS. It only makes sense to ask which is most common. **Mode** is the only measure of central tendency that applies.

**Verification:** The three situations cover the three typical application cases: (a) quantitative skewed → median; (b) quantitative with outliers → median; (c) categorical → mode. The mean would be appropriate only in variables that are symmetric with no outliers.

**Source.** [OpenIntro Statistics §1.6–§2.1](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/) — CC-BY-SA 4.0. Classification based on the measure-choice criteria from the variables and measures section.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Demonstration: mean minimizes squared error (challenge)" numero="5">

**Problem:** Prove that for any constant $c \in \mathbb{R}$, the sum $S(c) = \sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - c)^2$ is minimized at $c = \bar{x}$.

**Strategy:** Expand $S(c)$ by adding and subtracting $\bar{x}$, completing the square. The algebraic structure will reveal that $S(c) \geq S(\bar{x})$ for all $c$.

**Solution:**

Write $x_i - c = (x_i - \bar{x}) + (\bar{x} - c)$. Then:

$$S(c) = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigl[(x_i - \bar{x}) + (\bar{x} - c)\bigr]^2$$

Expanding the square:

$$S(c) = \sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - \bar{x})^2 + 2(\bar{x} - c)\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - \bar{x}) + n(\bar{x} - c)^2$$

By Theorem 1 of this lesson, $\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - \bar{x}) = 0$. Thus the middle term is zero:

$$S(c) = \underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - \bar{x})^2}_{\text{constant (independent of } c)} + \underbrace{n(\bar{x} - c)^2}_{\geq\; 0,\text{ vanishes at } c = \bar{x}}$$

The second term is non-negative and vanishes if and only if $c = \bar{x}$. Therefore $S(c) \geq S(\bar{x})$ for all $c$, with equality at $c = \bar{x}$. $\square$

**Verification:** The proof uses only: (i) algebraic expansion of the square; (ii) the fact that $\sum(x_i - \bar{x}) = 0$. No assumption about the distribution of $x_i$ was needed — the result holds for any finite set of real numbers.

**Source.** [OpenIntro Statistics §2.1](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/) — CC-BY-SA 4.0. Demonstration based on the variational characterization of the mean.

</Exemplo>
