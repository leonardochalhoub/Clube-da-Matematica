### Descriptive statistics: the summary problem

Given a set of $n$ observations $x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n$, we want a single number that represents the "center" of the distribution. There is no single answer — there are three different questions, three different answers.

<Definicao titulo="Arithmetic mean">

For data $x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n$:

<Equation explicacao="Mean: sum of all values divided by the number of observations n." latex="\bar{x} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i" />

For **grouped data** with values $x_i$ and absolute frequencies $f_i$:

<Equation explicacao="Mean weighted by frequencies: sum of each value multiplied by its frequency, divided by the total number of observations." latex="\bar{x} = \frac{\sum_{i} f_i \, x_i}{\sum_{i} f_i}" />

</Definicao>

> "The sample mean can be calculated for any quantitative variable. For a discrete distribution, the mean is the sum of each value multiplied by its probability; for a continuous distribution, the corresponding integral." — [OpenIntro Statistics, §1.6](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/)

<Definicao titulo="Median">

Sort the data: $x_{(1)} \leq x_{(2)} \leq \cdots \leq x_{(n)}$.

- If $n$ is **odd**: $\mathrm{Med} = x_{((n+1)/2)}$
- If $n$ is **even**: $\mathrm{Med} = \dfrac{x_{(n/2)} + x_{(n/2 + 1)}}{2}$

The median is the 50% quantile — half of the observations are below, half are above.

</Definicao>

<Definicao titulo="Mode">

Value (or values) with the highest absolute frequency. A distribution can be:

- **Unimodal**: a single mode.
- **Bimodal**: two modes (equal frequencies in both, higher than the others).
- **Multimodal**: more than two modes.
- **Amodal** (or no mode): all frequencies are equal.

</Definicao>

### Algebraic properties of the mean

<Teorema titulo="Deviations from the mean sum to zero">

For any dataset $x_1, \ldots, x_n$ with mean $\bar{x}$:

<Equation explicacao="The sum of deviations around the mean is always zero: positive and negative deviations cancel out exactly. This is the sense in which the mean is the center of gravity of the data." latex="\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - \bar{x}) = 0" />

**Proof.** $\sum(x_i - \bar{x}) = \sum x_i - n\bar{x} = n\bar{x} - n\bar{x} = 0$. $\square$

</Teorema>

<Teorema titulo="Mean minimizes squared error">

Among all constants $c \in \mathbb{R}$, the function $f(c) = \sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - c)^2$ is minimized at $c = \bar{x}$.

**Proof.** Expand: $f(c) = \sum(x_i - \bar{x} + \bar{x} - c)^2 = \sum(x_i-\bar{x})^2 + n(\bar{x}-c)^2$. The first term is constant; the second is $\geq 0$ and vanishes at $c = \bar{x}$. $\square$

</Teorema>

> "The mean minimizes the sum of squared deviations ($L^2$ error). The median minimizes the sum of absolute deviations ($L^1$ error). This distinction has profound consequences in regression and machine learning." — [OpenIntro Statistics, §2.1](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/)

<Insight>

The Mode–Median–Mean order in skewed distributions is an **empirical rule**, not a theorem. In highly skewed or multimodal distributions, the relationship may invert. Always use the histogram as a visual check.

</Insight>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

Imagine you and your friends have bags of candy:

- Your bag: 3 candies
- Friend 1: 5 candies
- Friend 2: 5 candies
- Friend 3: 7 candies
- Friend 4: 10 candies

**Mean**: put all candies in a pile (3 + 5 + 5 + 7 + 10 = 30) and divide equally among the 5 bags. Each would have 6 candies. That is the mean: 6.

**Median**: line up the bags from smallest to largest: 3, 5, **5**, 7, 10. The one in the middle is 5. That is the median.

**Mode**: which number appears most often? 5 appears twice, all others only once. So the mode is 5.

Each tells a **different story** about the bags.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

When you have several numbers and want to **summarize them into one**, use measures of central tendency.

**Mean** $\bar{x}$: sum everything and divide by the total.

Class grades: 6, 7, 8, 8, 9. Sum = 38. Total = 5 students. Mean = 38 / 5 = 7.6.

**Median**: sort and take the middle one.

Sorted grades: 6, 7, **8**, 8, 9. There are 5 grades, the middle one is the 3rd: median = 8.

If you have an even number of values — for example, 6, 7, 8, 9 — take the two in the middle (7 and 8) and calculate their mean: (7 + 8) / 2 = 7.5.

**Mode**: what appears most often. In the list 6, 7, 8, 8, 9, the number 8 appears twice. Mode = 8.

**When to use which?**

- Salaries in a company where the owner earns \$500k and others earn \$3k: the **mean** comes out huge and represents no one. Use **median**.
- Best-selling car color: you can't calculate the "mean of colors". Use **mode**.
- Class grades without extreme values: **mean**.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old">

**Descriptive** statistics starts here. The three central measures answer distinct questions about the same dataset.

### Mean

$$\bar{x} = \frac{x_1 + x_2 + \cdots + x_n}{n} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i$$

**Strength:** uses all information; has elegant algebraic properties (linearity: $\overline{a x + b} = a\bar{x} + b$).

**Weakness:** sensitive to **outliers**. Average salary in a company with 10 employees earning \$5,000 and 1 CEO earning \$1,000,000: $\bar{x} \approx$ \$95,000. Represents no one.

### Median

Middle value after sorting. **Robust**: changing the largest value to an even larger one does not change the median.

In the salary example: median = \$5,000. Represents the typical worker.

### Mode

Most frequent value. Useful for **categorical** variables (color, brand, party) and to reveal peaks in **multimodal** distributions (e.g., height distribution grouping men and women is bimodal, with two peaks).

### The journalist's rule

The press almost always uses **mean** (it is "the statistic"). But:

- For **income, property price, waiting time**: prefer **median**.
- For **categorical variables**: use **mode**.
- For symmetric data, without outliers: **mean** is a good choice.

### Skewness and order of measures

Long tail to the **right** (e.g., family income): **mode less than median less than mean** — the tail pulls the mean up.

Long tail to the **left**: **mean less than median less than mode**.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

Central measures are the basic vocabulary of all quantitative analysis.

### Variational characterization (the key)

| Measure | Minimizes | Norm |
|---|---|---|
| Mean | $\displaystyle\sum_{i}(x_i - c)^2$ | $L^2$ |
| Median | $\displaystyle\sum_{i}\lvert x_i - c \rvert$ | $L^1$ |
| Mode | $\displaystyle\sum_{i} \mathbf{1}_{x_i \neq c}$ | $L^0$ (approx.) |

**The choice of central measure is the choice of norm.** In ML: least squares regression (MSE) assumes $L^2$ and the optimal estimator is the mean; least absolute deviations regression (MAE) assumes $L^1$ and the optimal estimator is the median; Huber loss combines both.

### Robustness: breakdown point

The **breakdown point** of an estimator is the fraction of data that can be corrupted before the estimator diverges.

- Mean: breakdown point = $1/n \to 0$. A single sufficiently extreme outlier destroys the mean.
- Median: breakdown point = $1/2$. Resists up to 50% contamination.

**Trimmed mean**: discards the $k\%$ extremes before calculating. Compromise between efficiency (close to mean) and robustness (close to median).

### Pathological cases

**Cauchy distribution** $f(x) = 1/[\pi(1+x^2)]$: median = 0, but the mean **does not exist** — the integral $\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} x f(x)\, dx$ diverges. The median is the only significant measure of central tendency.

**Log-normal distribution** $\ln X \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)$: mode $= e^{\mu - \sigma^2}$, median $= e^\mu$, mean $= e^{\mu + \sigma^2/2}$. The order mode less than median less than mean is guaranteed for $\sigma > 0$.

### Law of Large Numbers

For $X_i$ i.i.d. with $E[X] = \mu$ finite: $\bar{X}_n \to \mu$ a.s. (Kolmogorov's strong LLN). Thus the sample mean is a **consistent** estimator of $\mu$.

The sample median also converges to the population median, with rate $\sqrt{n}$ and asymptotic variance $1/(4f(m)^2)$ where $f(m)$ is the density at the median point. When $f(m)$ is large (sharp peak), the median is more efficient than the mean.

### Computation

- Mean: $O(n)$ — one pass.
- Median: $O(n)$ via QuickSelect (median of medians guarantees worst case); $O(n \log n)$ by sorting.
- Mode: $O(n)$ with hash; $O(n \log n)$ by sorting.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

### M-estimators — the unification

Huber (1964) proposes minimizing $\sum \rho(x_i - c)$ for a convex function $\rho$:

- $\rho(t) = t^2/2$ → mean.
- $\rho(t) = \lvert t \rvert$ → median.
- $\rho(t) = t^2/2$ for $\lvert t \rvert \leq k$, $k\lvert t\rvert - k^2/2$ otherwise → **Huber**, robust.
- Tukey biweight $\rho$ → even higher resistance to extreme outliers.

This family (M-estimators) is the foundation of modern **robust statistics**. In regression: ridge ($L^2$ + regularization), lasso ($L^1$ → sparsity), Huber loss (robust regression).

### Fréchet median on manifolds

In $\mathbb{R}^n$: spatial median = $\arg\min_c \sum \lvert\lvert x_i - c \rvert\rvert_2$. On Riemannian manifolds: $\arg\min_c \sum d_g(x_i, c)^p$ for $p = 1$ (Fréchet median) or $p = 2$ (Fréchet mean). The Fréchet mean appears in computing means on Lie groups (rotations, poses), computer vision, signal processing on manifolds.

### Federated learning and robustness

In federated learning, the usual aggregation is the mean of client gradients. A malicious client can send an arbitrarily large gradient and contaminate the model. Replacing mean with **coordinate-wise median** or **Krum** (selection of the gradient closest to others) eliminates this attack vector, at the cost of slower convergence.

### Simpson's paradox

Trends reverse when aggregating groups. Classic: success rate of medical treatment seems lower in hospital A, but when disaggregating by case severity, A is better in each category. The median does not solve the paradox — the problem lies in confounding variables, not in the chosen measure.

### Historical timeline

- Galton (1880s): introduces median, percentiles, correlation — statistics as a science.
- Pearson (1894): defines moment, skewness, kurtosis. Formulates the mean as central.
- Fisher (1922): consistency, sufficiency, efficiency — theory of estimators.
- Tukey (1962): Exploratory Data Analysis. Boxplot, median return to the center.
- Huber (1964): M-estimators. Robustness formalized.
- Lugosi & Mendelson (2019): median-of-means for heavy-tailed distributions — optimal convergence in $O(1/\sqrt{n})$ even without normality assumption.

[2000 Nobel Prize in Economics — Heckman and McFadden](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/2000/summary/) — microeconometric methods that depend on robust estimation of central location for heterogeneous populations.

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practical application">

### Quick choice guide

| Situation | Measure |
|---|---|
| Symmetric data, no outliers | Mean |
| Asymmetric data (income, price, wait time) | Median |
| Categorical variable (color, brand, party) | Mode |
| Outliers present and non-removable | Median |
| Multimodal distribution | Report all modes |

**Operational rule:**

1. Make the histogram. Always.
2. Identify skewness (long tail to the right or left).
3. Identify outliers (points beyond $\pm 3$ standard deviations or beyond $1.5 \times \text{IQR}$ of the boxplot).
4. Choose according to the table above.
5. Report both (mean + median) when relevant — for income, IBGE does exactly this.

### Brazilian examples

**IBGE — Per capita household income (2022):** median = R\$ 1,320; mean = R\$ 1,625. The difference of R\$ 305 reveals skewness: a minority of very high incomes pulls the mean up, while the median better represents the typical resident.

**ENEM 2023 — Mathematics:** mean score ≈ 527 points, median ≈ 513. Distribution with slight right skew — the INEP report includes both.

**E-commerce delivery time:** server latency has a heavy tail (1% of requests are very slow). The p99 median is the standard SLA metric in the industry — not the mean.

### Grouped data (frequency table)

When data comes in intervals:

- **Mean:** $\bar{x} = \sum f_i x_i^* / \sum f_i$ where $x_i^*$ is the interval midpoint.
- **Median:** locate the median class (where cumulative frequency crosses $n/2$), then interpolate linearly within it.
- **Mode:** interval with highest frequency. The Czuber formula estimates the exact value within the modal interval using frequencies of neighboring intervals.

## Solved examples

<Exemplo titulo="Direct calculation of the three measures (initiation)" numero="1">

**Problem:** The time (in minutes) that seven students took to solve an ENEM question was: 8, 12, 7, 15, 12, 10, 9. Calculate the mean, median, and mode.

**Strategy:** For the mean, we sum and divide. For the median, we need to sort first. For the mode, we identify the most frequent value.

**Resolution:**

Sorted data: 7, 8, 9, **10**, 12, 12, 15.

**Mean:**
$$\bar{x} = \frac{7 + 8 + 9 + 10 + 12 + 12 + 15}{7} = \frac{73}{7} \approx 10.43 \text{ min}$$

**Median:** $n = 7$ (odd), middle position = $(7+1)/2 = 4$. The 4th value in the sorted list is **10**. Median = 10 min.

**Mode:** the value 12 appears twice; all others, once. Mode = 12 min.

**Verification:** The mean (10.43) is between the median (10) and the mode (12). The distribution has slight right skew pulled by the value 15 — consistent with mode less than or equal to median less than or equal to mean.

**Source.** [OpenIntro Statistics §1.6](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/) — CC-BY-SA 4.0. Exercise adapted from the introductory descriptive statistics section.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Mean for grouped data in frequency table (intermediate)" numero="2">

**Problem:** A survey of 40 students on weekly hours dedicated to extracurricular study produced the table:

| Hours | Frequency ($f_i$) |
|---|---|
| 0 to 4 | 5 |
| 4 to 8 | 12 |
| 8 to 12 | 15 |
| 12 to 16 | 6 |
| 16 to 20 | 2 |

Calculate the arithmetic mean using the midpoints of the intervals.

**Strategy:** For grouped data, we replace each interval with its midpoint and apply the weighted mean formula.

**Resolution:**

Midpoints: 2, 6, 10, 14, 18.

$$\bar{x} = \frac{5 \cdot 2 + 12 \cdot 6 + 15 \cdot 10 + 6 \cdot 14 + 2 \cdot 18}{40} = \frac{10 + 72 + 150 + 84 + 36}{40} = \frac{352}{40} = 8.8 \text{ hours}$$

**Verification:** The value 8.8 falls within the interval of highest frequency (8 to 12 hours), which concentrates 15 of the 40 students. Consistent. The sum of frequencies is $5 + 12 + 15 + 6 + 2 = 40$. Correct.

**Source.** [OpenStax Introductory Statistics 2e §2.5](https://openstax.org/books/introductory-statistics-2e/pages/2-5-measures-of-the-center-of-the-data) — CC-BY 4.0. Adapted from the grouped data example.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Median with even n and outlier impact (intermediate-advanced)" numero="3">

**Problem:** The monthly salaries (in \$k) of 8 employees at a startup are: 4, 4, 5, 5, 6, 7, 8, 60.

(a) Calculate mean and median.
(b) The CEO (\$60k) leaves and is replaced by someone with \$7k. Recalculate.
(c) Which measure is more adequate to describe the salary of the "typical employee" in both cases?

**Strategy:** Calculate both measures before and after the swap, and compare the sensitivity of each to the outlier.

**Resolution:**

**(a) With the CEO (\$60k):**

Sorted data: 4, 4, 5, **5, 6**, 7, 8, 60.

Mean: $(4 + 4 + 5 + 5 + 6 + 7 + 8 + 60)/8 = 99/8 = 12.375$k.

Median ($n = 8$ even): mean of 4th and 5th values = $(5 + 6)/2 = 5.5$k.

**(b) Without the CEO, with \$7k:**

Sorted data: 4, 4, 5, **5, 6**, 7, 7, 8.

Mean: $(4 + 4 + 5 + 5 + 6 + 7 + 7 + 8)/8 = 46/8 = 5.75$k.

Median: $(5 + 6)/2 = 5.5$k.

**(c) Comparison:** The mean jumped from 5.75 to 12.375 — a +115% variation — with the entry of a single outlier. The median remained 5.5k in both cases. To represent the typical employee's salary, the **median is more adequate** in the presence of extreme outliers.

**Verification:** In (a), only 1 of the 8 employees (12.5%) earns above the mean. The median better reflects the salary mass.

**Source.** [OpenIntro Statistics §1.6](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/) — CC-BY-SA 4.0. Context adapted for Brazilian startup data.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Choice of central measure: income, wait time, color (modeling)" numero="4">

**Problem:** For each situation below, identify the most adequate measure of central tendency and justify:

(a) Per capita income of Brazilian municipalities for comparison between states.
(b) Waiting time (in minutes) at a public hospital emergency room.
(c) Most installed operating system in public school computer labs.

**Strategy:** For each variable, identify: is it quantitative or categorical? If quantitative, is skewness or outliers expected?

**Resolution:**

**(a) Per capita income:** quantitative variable with expected right skew (very rich municipalities like São Paulo and Brasília pull the mean up, but most municipalities have low income). **Median** is more adequate — IBGE uses the median for this very reason.

**(b) Emergency room waiting time:** quantitative variable with heavy right tail (most patients are seen in 1 to 3 hours, but critical cases may wait 12 or more hours, distorting the mean). **Median** or 90th percentile are standard metrics in hospital management.

**(c) Operating system:** **categorical** variable — there is no natural ordering between Windows, Linux, macOS. It only makes sense to ask which is most frequent. **Mode** is the only applicable measure of central tendency.

**Verification:** The three situations cover the three typical application cases: (a) asymmetric quantitative → median; (b) quantitative with outliers → median; (c) categorical → mode. The mean would only be adequate for symmetric variables without outliers.

**Source.** [OpenIntro Statistics §1.6–§2.1](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/) — CC-BY-SA 4.0. Classification based on the selection criteria of the variables and measures section.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Proof: mean minimizes squared error (challenge)" numero="5">

**Problem:** Prove that for any constant $c \in \mathbb{R}$, the sum $S(c) = \sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - c)^2$ is minimized at $c = \bar{x}$.

**Strategy:** Expand $S(c)$ by adding and subtracting $\bar{x}$, completing the square. The algebraic structure will reveal that $S(c) \geq S(\bar{x})$ for all $c$.

**Resolution:**

Write $x_i - c = (x_i - \bar{x}) + (\bar{x} - c)$. Then:

$$S(c) = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigl[(x_i - \bar{x}) + (\bar{x} - c)\bigr]^2$$

Expanding the square:

$$S(c) = \sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - \bar{x})^2 + 2(\bar{x} - c)\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - \bar{x}) + n(\bar{x} - c)^2$$

By Theorem 1 of this lesson, $\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - \bar{x}) = 0$. Thus the middle term is zero:

$$S(c) = \underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - \bar{x})^2}_{\text{constant (does not depend on } c)} + \underbrace{n(\bar{x} - c)^2}_{\geq\; 0,\text{ vanishes at } c = \bar{x}}$$

The second term is non-negative and vanishes if and only if $c = \bar{x}$. Therefore $S(c) \geq S(\bar{x})$ for all $c$, with equality at $c = \bar{x}$. $\square$

**Verification:** The proof uses only: (i) algebraic expansion of the square; (ii) the fact that $\sum(x_i - \bar{x}) = 0$. No assumption about the distribution of $x_i$ was necessary — the result holds for any finite set of real numbers.

**Source.** [OpenIntro Statistics §2.1](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/) — CC-BY-SA 4.0. Proof based on the variational characterization of the mean.

</Exemplo>
