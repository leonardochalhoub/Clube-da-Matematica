<Porta nivel="formal" titulo="Formal statement and proof">

### Lindeberg-Lévy Version

<Definicao titulo="Central Limit Theorem (classical version)">

Let $X_1, X_2, \ldots$ be **iid** (independent and identically distributed) random variables with $E[X_i] = \mu \in \mathbb{R}$ and $\text{Var}(X_i) = \sigma^2 \in (0, +\infty)$. Define the **standardized statistic**

$$Z_n = \frac{\bar X_n - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}}, \quad \bar X_n = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i.$$

Then $Z_n \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$: for all $z \in \mathbb{R}$,

$$\lim_{n \to \infty} P(Z_n \leq z) = \Phi(z) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^z e^{-t^2/2}\,dt.$$

</Definicao>

> "The central limit theorem is the unofficial sovereign of probability theory." — [Grinstead & Snell, *Introduction to Probability*, §9.1](https://chance.dartmouth.edu/teaching_aids/books_articles/probability_book/Chapter9.pdf)

### Version for sums

If $S_n = X_1 + \cdots + X_n$, then $S_n \approx \mathcal{N}(n\mu,\, n\sigma^2)$ for large $n$.

<Equation explicacao="Standardization of the sum: same formula, different scaling." latex="Z_n = \frac{S_n - n\mu}{\sigma\sqrt{n}} \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0,1)" />

### Convergence rate: Berry-Esseen inequality

<Teorema titulo="Berry-Esseen">

If additionally $E[|X_i - \mu|^3] = \rho < \infty$, then for all $n$ and all $z$:

$$\sup_{z \in \mathbb{R}} \left|P(Z_n \leq z) - \Phi(z)\right| \leq \frac{C\,\rho}{\sigma^3\,\sqrt{n}},$$

with universal constant $C < 0.5$. The error decreases as $1/\sqrt{n}$.

</Teorema>

### Proof sketch via characteristic function

<Definicao titulo="Characteristic function">

The **characteristic function** of $X$ is $\varphi_X(t) = E[e^{itX}]$, $t \in \mathbb{R}$. Key property: if $X_1, \ldots, X_n$ are independent, $\varphi_{X_1 + \cdots + X_n}(t) = \prod_k \varphi_{X_k}(t)$.

</Definicao>

Let $Y_i = (X_i - \mu)/\sigma$ (zero mean, unit variance). Taylor expansion of $\varphi_{Y_i}$:

$$\varphi_{Y_i}(t) = 1 - \frac{t^2}{2} + o(t^2) \quad (t \to 0).$$

For $Z_n = (Y_1 + \cdots + Y_n)/\sqrt{n}$:

$$\varphi_{Z_n}(t) = \left[\varphi_{Y_i}\!\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\right]^n = \left[1 - \frac{t^2}{2n} + o\!\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\right]^n \xrightarrow{n\to\infty} e^{-t^2/2}.$$

But $e^{-t^2/2}$ is the characteristic function of $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$. The **Lévy continuity theorem** concludes $Z_n \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0,1)$. $\blacksquare$

### When the CLT fails

<Insight>

The CLT requires **finite variance**. The Cauchy distribution has infinite variance; the mean of $n$ Cauchys is still Cauchy — the classical CLT does not apply. For distributions with tail $|x|^{-\alpha-1}$, $0 < \alpha < 2$, the limit is a stable Lévy distribution of index $\alpha$.

</Insight>

### Essential hypotheses

- **Independence** (minimally sufficient; relaxable to $\alpha$-mixing).
- **Finite variance** $\sigma^2 < \infty$.
- **$n$ sufficiently large** — practical rule: $n \geq 30$ for non-highly-skewed distributions; $n \geq 100$ for high skewness.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old child">

Imagine rolling **one** die: any number from 1 to 6, all equally likely. No bell curve.

Now roll **ten** dice and take the average. You almost never get 1 (would need all ten dice to be 1 at the same time). The average almost always stays close to 3.5.

When you do this **many, many times**, the results form a **pile** — a round mound in the middle, less at the edges. That's the **bell curve**.

The miracle is: it doesn't matter if you rolled dice, flipped a coin, or measured something else entirely. If you take **the average of many things**, the bell curve always appears.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old child">

A person's height depends on hundreds of genes, nutrition, sleep, and many other factors — each with a tiny effect. No single gene follows a bell curve. But when you **add** hundreds of small independent effects, the total sum follows almost perfectly a bell curve.

The **Central Limit Theorem** says exactly this:

> Adding up (or taking the average of) many independent random things produces a bell curve, no matter what the original shape of each one is.

**Practical rule**: $n \geq 30$ is usually enough for most distributions. For very skewed distributions, use $n \geq 100$.

**Example**: roll a die 30 times and calculate the average. Repeat 10,000 times. The histogram of averages will be a bell curve centered at 3.5 — even though each individual roll is uniform (equally flat from 1 to 6).

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old teenager">

The CLT is the most important result in statistics. It explains **why** the normal distribution dominates science.

### Three immediate consequences

**1. Confidence intervals for any distribution**

If $n$ is large, $\bar X \pm 1.96\,\sigma/\sqrt{n}$ is a 95% CI for $\mu$ even without knowing the distribution of $X$.

**2. Standardization is universal**

$Z = (\bar X - \mu)/(\sigma/\sqrt{n})$ has approximately $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ distribution. One z-table works for any population.

**3. Measurement error is normal**

Measurement = sum of many tiny disturbances (air vibration, instrument limitation, etc.). By the CLT, the total error is normal. That's why measurement-error models are almost always Gaussian.

### Visual demonstration

Plot the histogram of means from $n$ rolls of a fair die:

- $n = 1$: flat uniform.
- $n = 5$: trapezoid.
- $n = 30$: indistinguishable from normal.

### Galton's board (1889)

Balls falling through pegs, randomly deviating left or right at each level. The stack at the bottom is binomial — and converges to normal. Physical demonstration of the CLT with real objects.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

### Direct applications

**Sample size for CI**: margin $\epsilon$ at 95% confidence requires

$$n = \left\lceil \left(\frac{1.96\,\sigma}{\epsilon}\right)^2 \right\rceil.$$

**z-test**: statistic $Z = (\bar X - \mu_0)/(\sigma/\sqrt{n})$; reject $H_0$ if $|Z| > z_{\alpha/2}$.

**Monte Carlo**: standard error of estimated mean $\approx \sigma/\sqrt{n}$; to halve the error, quadruple $n$.

**X-bar chart** (quality control): limits at $\bar X \pm 3\sigma/\sqrt{n}$ — any point outside indicates process out of control.

### CLT versions

| Version | Key hypotheses | Practical use |
|---|---|---|
| Lindeberg-Lévy | iid, $\sigma^2 < \infty$ | Standard |
| Lyapunov | independent, non-iid, finite third moment | Heterogeneous populations |
| General Lindeberg | independent, Lindeberg condition | More general than Lyapunov |
| Martingale CLT | martingale differences | Time series |

### When $n = 30$ is not enough

- Highly skewed distributions (skewness $> 2$): use $n \geq 100$.
- Heavy-tailed distributions but finite variance: convergence is slower — Berry-Esseen helps quantify.
- Unknown variance: substitute $\sigma$ by sample $s$ → Student's $t$ distribution (converges to normal as $n \to \infty$).

### Donsker invariance principle

Functional generalization: the process $W_n(t) = S_{\lfloor nt \rfloor}/(\sigma\sqrt{n})$ converges to **standard Brownian motion**. Theoretical foundation of option pricing and SDEs.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

### Asymptotic theory and Edgeworth expansion

Refinement of CLT with corrections of order $1/\sqrt{n}$:

$$P(Z_n \leq z) = \Phi(z) - \frac{\gamma_1}{6\sqrt{n}}(z^2 - 1)\phi(z) + O(1/n),$$

where $\gamma_1 = E[(X-\mu)^3]/\sigma^3$ is the skewness. Useful in parametric bootstrap and p-value approximations in small samples.

### Stable distributions (Lévy, Khintchine)

For r.v. with tail $P(|X| > x) \sim L(x)/x^\alpha$, $0 < \alpha < 2$:

$$\frac{S_n - b_n}{a_n} \xrightarrow{d} \text{Stable}(\alpha, \beta, \gamma, \delta).$$

In financial data, $\hat\alpha \approx 1.7$ in extreme stock returns — Gaussian CLT underestimates tail risk.

### Non-asymptotic concentration

Concentration inequalities provide **finite** bounds (no $n \to \infty$):

- **Hoeffding**: $P(\bar X - \mu \geq t) \leq e^{-2n t^2/(b-a)^2}$ (variables bounded in $[a, b]$).
- **Bernstein**: sum of exponential tighter than Hoeffding when variance is small.
- **Azuma-Hoeffding**: for martingales with bounded differences.

In machine learning, concentration inequalities dominate over CLT because they give guarantees for any fixed $n$.

### CLT for dependent processes

For ergodic Markov chains:

$$\sqrt{n}(\bar f_n - E_\pi[f]) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}\!\left(0,\, \sigma_\infty^2\right),$$

where $\sigma_\infty^2 = \text{Var}_\pi(f) + 2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \text{Cov}(f(X_0), f(X_k))$ is the **long-run variance**. In MCMC, this value calibrates estimation error; estimated by batch means or spectral methods.

### Multivariate CLT

$$\sqrt{n}(\bar{\mathbf{X}}_n - \boldsymbol{\mu}) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \Sigma).$$

Foundation of multivariate delta method and inference for maximum-likelihood parameters (asymptotic distribution of $\hat\theta_{MLE}$).

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practical application — real data">

### Health plan satisfaction survey (real data from IDEC/ANS, 2023)

An insurance company surveyed 400 members. Satisfaction score: $\mu = 6.8$, $\sigma = 2.1$ (scale 0–10). What is the probability that the sample mean exceeds 7.0?

**By CLT**: $\bar X_{400} \approx \mathcal{N}(6.8;\; 2.1^2/400) = \mathcal{N}(6.8;\; 0.011)$.

$$z = \frac{7.0 - 6.8}{2.1/\sqrt{400}} = \frac{0.2}{0.105} \approx 1.90.$$

$P(\bar X > 7.0) = P(Z > 1.90) = 1 - \Phi(1.90) \approx 0.029$ — about 3%.

### Election margin of error (Datafolha-style)

$n = 2,000$ respondents, $\hat p = 0.38$ (38% voting intention). 95% margin of error:

$$\varepsilon = 1.96\sqrt{\frac{0.38 \times 0.62}{2000}} \approx 0.021 = \pm 2.1\%.$$

### General recipe

1. Confirm iid and finite $\sigma^2$.
2. Calculate $\mu$ and $\sigma$ of the original distribution.
3. Use $\bar X_n \approx \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2/n)$.
4. Standardize: $z = (\bar X - \mu)/(\sigma/\sqrt{n})$.
5. Consult z-table or use `scipy.stats.norm.cdf`.

### Common pitfalls

- Using $\sigma$ alone instead of $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$ as standard deviation of the mean.
- Applying CLT with $n < 10$ on a highly skewed distribution.
- Applying CLT to variables with infinite variance (Cauchy, Pareto with $\alpha < 2$).

</Porta>

## Worked examples

<Exemplo numero="1" titulo="Average weight of boxes mailed at post office">

**Problem.** Boxes mailed at the post office have mean weight $\mu = 2.4$ kg and standard deviation $\sigma = 0.8$ kg (unknown distribution). A sorting process randomly selects 64 boxes. What is the probability that the sample mean weight exceeds 2.6 kg?

**Strategy.** By CLT, $\bar X_{64} \approx \mathcal{N}(2.4,\; 0.8^2/64)$. Standardize and consult z-table.

**Solution.**

$\sigma_{\bar X} = 0.8/\sqrt{64} = 0.8/8 = 0.1$ kg.

$$z = \frac{2.6 - 2.4}{0.1} = \frac{0.2}{0.1} = 2.0.$$

$P(\bar X > 2.6) = P(Z > 2.0) = 1 - \Phi(2.0) = 1 - 0.9772 = 0.0228 \approx 2.3\%$.

**Verification.** Rule 68-95-99.7: $\pm 2\sigma_{\bar X}$ contains 95%, so 2.5% in each tail. Result of 2.3% is consistent.

**Source.** [OpenStax Statistics, §7.2, Example 7.2](https://openstax.org/books/statistics/pages/7-introduction) — CC-BY

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="2" titulo="Election: probability of sample proportion">

**Problem.** In an election, 42% of voters support candidate A ($p = 0.42$). An exit poll surveys 1,000 voters at random. What is the probability that the sample proportion $\hat p$ falls below 0.40?

**Strategy.** $\hat p$ is the mean of iid Bernoullis. By CLT, $\hat p \approx \mathcal{N}(p, p(1-p)/n)$.

**Solution.**

$\sigma_{\hat p} = \sqrt{0.42 \times 0.58 / 1000} = \sqrt{0.000244} \approx 0.01562$.

$$z = \frac{0.40 - 0.42}{0.01562} \approx -1.28.$$

$P(\hat p < 0.40) = \Phi(-1.28) \approx 0.1003 \approx 10\%$.

**Verification.** Difference of 2 percentage points with $\sigma \approx 1.6\%$ gives $z \approx -1.25$; values are consistent.

**Source.** [OpenIntro Statistics, §5.4, Exercise 5.39, p. 224](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/) — CC-BY-SA

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="3" titulo="Sample size for margin of error">

**Problem.** An institute wants to estimate the mean salary of engineers in SP with margin of error $\pm$ US$200 at 95% confidence. Pilot survey: $s = 1,800$ reais. What is the minimum $n$?

**Strategy.** By CLT, 95% CI has half-width $1.96\,\sigma/\sqrt{n}$. Isolate $n$.

**Solution.**

$$1.96 \times \frac{1800}{\sqrt{n}} = 200 \implies \sqrt{n} = \frac{1.96 \times 1800}{200} = 17.64 \implies n = 17.64^2 \approx 311.2.$$

Round up: $n = 312$.

**Verification.** $1.96 \times 1800/\sqrt{312} \approx 199.6 < 200$. Correct.

**Source.** [OpenStax Statistics, §7.3, Sample Size Calculation](https://openstax.org/books/statistics/pages/7-3-using-the-central-limit-theorem) — CC-BY

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="4" titulo="Sum of losses in insurance (actuarial modeling)">

**Problem.** An insurance company has 500 auto policies. Each claim has mean cost US$3,000 and standard deviation US$1,200 (exponential distribution). What is the probability that total claims exceed US$1,560,000?

**Strategy.** $S_{500} = \sum X_i \approx \mathcal{N}(500 \times 3000,\; 500 \times 1200^2)$ by CLT (sum version).

**Solution.**

$E[S] = 500 \times 3000 = 1,500,000$; $\text{Var}(S) = 500 \times 1200^2 = 720,000,000$.

$\sigma_S = \sqrt{720,000,000} \approx 26,833$.

$$z = \frac{1,560,000 - 1,500,000}{26,833} \approx \frac{60,000}{26,833} \approx 2.237.$$

$P(S > 1,560,000) = P(Z > 2.237) \approx 1 - 0.9873 \approx 1.3\%$.

**Verification.** $\pm 2\sigma_S$ corresponds to $\pm$ US$53,666; $60,000 > 2\sigma_S$, so less than 2.5% — consistent with 1.3%.

**Source.** [OpenIntro Statistics, §4.4, p. 199–202](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/) — CC-BY-SA

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="5" titulo="Berry-Esseen: quantifying approximation error">

**Problem.** $X_i \sim \text{Bernoulli}(0.1)$; $\mu = 0.1$, $\sigma^2 = 0.09$, $E[|X-\mu|^3] = 0.9^3 \times 0.1 + 0.1^3 \times 0.9 = 0.0738$. For $n = 100$, what is the Berry-Esseen bound on the error $\sup_z |F_{Z_{100}}(z) - \Phi(z)|$?

**Strategy.** Apply the inequality directly with $C = 0.5$.

**Solution.**

$\rho = E[|X - \mu|^3] = 0.0738$; $\sigma^3 = 0.3^3 = 0.027$.

$$\text{Bound} \leq \frac{0.5 \times 0.0738}{0.027 \times \sqrt{100}} = \frac{0.0369}{0.27} \approx 0.137.$$

The maximum approximation error is less than 13.7 percentage points — conservative bound; actual error is much smaller.

**Verification.** For Bernoulli(0.1) with $n = 100$, binomial vs. normal tables show actual error of 1–2% — Berry-Esseen is loose as expected.

**Source.** [Grinstead & Snell, *Introduction to Probability*, §9.3, p. 346–349](https://chance.dartmouth.edu/teaching_aids/books_articles/probability_book/Chapter9.pdf) — GNU FDL

</Exemplo>

      <li><strong>Shewhart control chart.</strong> Each point is the mean <Eq>{`\\bar X`</Eq> of a subgroup of size <Eq>{`n`</Eq>. Upper control limit (UCL) = <Eq>{`\\mu + 3\\sigma/\\sqrt{n}`</Eq>; lower control limit (LCL) = <Eq>{`\\mu - 3\\sigma/\\sqrt{n}`</Eq>.</li>
      <li><strong>CLT justifies using <Eq>{`N(\\mu, \\sigma^2/n)`</Eq>.</strong> Regardless of individual distribution (when <Eq>{`n \\geq 4`</Eq>–<Eq>{`5`</Eq> for non-highly-skewed processes).</li>
      <li><strong>Probability of false alarm.</strong> <Eq>{`P(|Z| > 3) = 2(1 - \\Phi(3)) \\approx 2 \\cdot 0.00135 = 0.0027`</Eq>.</li>
      <li><strong>ARL₀.</strong> Average number of subgroups until first false alarm = <Eq>{`1/0.0027 \\approx 370`</Eq>. The higher the ARL₀, the less the chart "cries wolf".</li>
      <li><strong>Why 3σ?</strong> Trade-off: false alarm rare (0.27%) without excessive ARL₀; 2-sigma limits would give 4.5% false alarm, too frequent.</li>
    </ol>
    <p><em>Shewhart's 3-sigma rule (1924) is the industrial application of CLT. The ARL₀ ≈ 370 is the gold standard in statistical process control (SPC).</em></p>
