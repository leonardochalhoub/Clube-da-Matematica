<Porta nivel="formal" titulo="Definitions and theorems">

### Conditional probability

<Definicao titulo="Conditional probability">

Given events $A$ and $B$ with $P(B) > 0$, the **conditional probability of $A$ given $B$** is:

$$P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}$$

Intuitively: we restrict the sample space to event $B$ and measure the proportion that also satisfies $A$.

</Definicao>

> "The conditional probability $P(E \mid F)$, the probability of $E$ given $F$, expresses the probability of $E$ when we know that $F$ has occurred. It can be computed using the formula $P(E \mid F) = P(EF)/P(F)$, assuming $P(F) > 0$." — [Grinstead & Snell, Introduction to Probability, §4.1](https://chance.dartmouth.edu/teaching_aids/books_articles/probability_book/book.html)

<Definicao titulo="Independence">

$A$ and $B$ are **independent** when $P(A \cap B) = P(A)\,P(B)$, or equivalently when $P(A \mid B) = P(A)$ (knowing $B$ does not change the probability of $A$).

</Definicao>

### Law of total probability

<Teorema titulo="Law of total probability">

Let $\{H_1, H_2, \ldots, H_n\}$ be a **partition** of the sample space $\Omega$ (mutually exclusive and exhaustive events). For any event $E$:

$$P(E) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(E \mid H_i)\,P(H_i)$$

**Proof (sketch).** Since $H_i$ partition $\Omega$, we have $E = \bigcup_i (E \cap H_i)$ with disjoint terms. By additivity: $P(E) = \sum_i P(E \cap H_i) = \sum_i P(E \mid H_i)P(H_i)$. $\square$

</Teorema>

### Bayes' theorem

<Teorema titulo="Bayes' theorem">

Under the conditions of the law of total probability:

$$P(H_i \mid E) = \frac{P(E \mid H_i)\,P(H_i)}{\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n} P(E \mid H_j)\,P(H_j)}$$

**Proof.** By the definition of conditional probability: $P(H_i \mid E) = P(H_i \cap E)/P(E)$. The numerator is $P(E \mid H_i)P(H_i)$ by the product rule. The denominator is $P(E)$ expanded by the law of total probability. $\square$

</Teorema>

> "Bayes' Theorem is just a formula that comes from the definition of conditional probability. Yet it is extremely powerful, and is the key to understanding what it means to rationally revise your beliefs in light of new evidence." — [OpenIntro Statistics 4e, §3.2](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/)

### Odds form

<Definicao titulo="Odds form">

The **odds form** rewrites Bayes as a multiplication of ratios:

$$\underbrace{\frac{P(H \mid E)}{P(\neg H \mid E)}}_{\text{posterior odds}} = \underbrace{\frac{P(E \mid H)}{P(E \mid \neg H)}}_{\text{likelihood ratio (LR)}} \times \underbrace{\frac{P(H)}{P(\neg H)}}_{\text{prior odds}}$$

The **positive likelihood ratio** $\text{LR}^+ = \text{sensitivity}/(1 - \text{specificity})$ quantifies how much a positive test result favors the hypothesis.

</Definicao>

### Sequential updating

<Definicao titulo="Sequential updating (independent evidence)">

If $E_1$ and $E_2$ are **conditionally independent** given $H$ (i.e., $P(E_1 \cap E_2 \mid H) = P(E_1 \mid H)P(E_2 \mid H)$), then:

$$P(H \mid E_1, E_2) \propto P(E_1 \mid H)\,P(E_2 \mid H)\,P(H)$$

In practice: the **posterior** after $E_1$ becomes the **prior** for the update with $E_2$.

</Definicao>

<Insight>

The conditional independence condition is the heart of **Naive Bayes**: even if $E_1$ and $E_2$ are correlated in the population, the classifier treats them as independent given the class. Surprisingly, even when the assumption is violated in reality, the model often works well in practice because dependencies tend to cancel out on average.

</Insight>

### Beta-binomial conjugate prior

<Definicao titulo="Conjugate prior">

The prior $\pi(\theta)$ is **conjugate** to the likelihood $L(\theta \mid \mathbf{x})$ if the posterior $\pi(\theta \mid \mathbf{x})$ belongs to the same parametric family as the prior.

For the Bernoulli model: if $X_1, \ldots, X_n \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \text{Bernoulli}(\theta)$ and $k = \sum X_i$, with prior $\theta \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)$:

$$\theta \mid k \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha + k,\; \beta + n - k)$$

Prior Beta(1,1) = uniform on $[0,1]$ (non-informative prior). Posterior mean: $(\alpha + k)/(\alpha + \beta + n)$.

</Definicao>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

You hear a bark outside. It could be a big dog or a small dog.

How do you decide which one it is?

First, you think: **on my street, there are more small dogs** — let's say out of 10 dogs, 7 are small and only 3 are big. This is what you **already knew before** hearing the bark.

Then you hear the bark: it was **very deep and loud**. Big dogs bark deeply more often than small ones.

Bayes is exactly the way to **mix** what you already knew (more small dogs on the street) with what you just heard (deep bark) to make a better guess.

If the mix says "probably big," you get more scared. If it says "probably still small," you relax.

Every time you update what you think about something based on a new clue, you are doing Bayes, even without knowing the name.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

**The medical test riddle**

A rare disease affects 1 in 1000 people. There is a very good test:
- It gets 99% of sick people right (99% sensitivity).
- It gets 99% of healthy people right (99% specificity).

You take the test. It came back **positive**. What is the chance you are sick?

Most people guess: "99%!" But that is **completely wrong**.

Let's count with concrete numbers. Imagine 10,000 people:

- How many are sick? $10,000 \times 0.001 = 10$.
- Of the 10 sick people, the test gets 99% right: $10 \times 0.99 \approx 10$ true positives.
- How many are healthy? $10,000 - 10 = 9,990$.
- Of the 9,990 healthy people, the test gets 1% wrong: $9,990 \times 0.01 \approx 100$ false positives.

Total positives: $10 + 100 = 110$.

Sick people among the positives: $10$.

Real chance: $10/110 \approx 9\%$.

The answer isn't 99% because the disease is **very rare**. Having 100 false positives among 9,990 healthy people is normal for a test with a 1% error rate. But since the disease is so rare, those 100 false positives swallow the 10 true ones.

This is called the **base rate fallacy** — ignoring how rare the thing you are testing for is.

Bayes gives us the tool to calculate the correct number.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old">

**Bayes in 3 steps**

1. **Prior** $P(H)$: what you believe before seeing any evidence.
2. **Likelihood** $P(E \mid H)$: what is the probability of observing $E$ if $H$ is true?
3. **Posterior** $P(H \mid E)$: updated belief after observing $E$.

$$P(H \mid E) = \frac{P(E \mid H)\,P(H)}{P(E)}$$

**Error table for diagnostic tests**

|  | Sick | Healthy |
|---|---|---|
| Test + | True Positive (TP) | False Positive (FP) |
| Test − | False Negative (FN) | True Negative (TN) |

- **Sensitivity** $= P(+\mid D) = \text{TP}/(\text{TP}+\text{FN})$: ability to detect the sick.
- **Specificity** $= P(-\mid \bar D) = \text{TN}/(\text{TN}+\text{FP})$: ability to rule out the healthy.
- **Positive Predictive Value (PPV)** $= P(D\mid +)$: this is the Bayesian posterior.

The PPV **depends on prevalence**. A test with 99% sensitivity and 99% specificity for a disease with 0.1% prevalence has a PPV of only 9%. The same test for a disease with 50% prevalence has a PPV of 99%.

**Sequential updating**

If you take two consecutive tests (independent given the disease), the posterior of the first test becomes the prior of the second:

1. Prior: prevalence $p_0$.
2. After 1st positive test: posterior $p_1 = \text{PPV}_1(p_0)$.
3. After 2nd positive test: posterior $p_2 = \text{PPV}_2(p_1)$.

Each new piece of evidence refines the belief.

<Insight>

The **odds form** turns Bayes into a multiplication:

$$\text{posterior odds} = \text{LR}^+ \times \text{prior odds}$$

where $\text{LR}^+ = \text{sens}/(1-\text{spec})$. For two tests with $\text{LR}^+$ equal to $r_1$ and $r_2$, the posterior odds is $r_1 \times r_2 \times \text{prior odds}$. Multiplying LRs is much faster than applying Bayes twice in full.

</Insight>

</Porta>

### Frequentist vs. Bayesian

- **Frequentist**: parameter $\theta$ is fixed but unknown. Inference via confidence intervals: "if I repeat the experiment many times, 95% of the CIs constructed this way will contain $\theta$".
- **Bayesian**: $\theta$ is a random variable. Uncertainty about $\theta$ is described by a distribution. Posterior $\pi(\theta \mid \mathbf{x})$ summarizes everything we know about $\theta$ after observing $\mathbf{x}$. Credible interval: "$P(a \leq \theta \leq b \mid \mathbf{x}) = 0.95$" — 95% posterior probability.

For large $n$, both approaches converge (under regularity conditions).

### Beta-binomial conjugate prior

Flip a coin $n$ times, observe $k$ heads. Model: $k \mid \theta \sim \text{Binomial}(n, \theta)$.

Prior $\theta \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)$. Posterior:

$$\theta \mid k \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha + k,\; \beta + n - k)$$

Posterior mean: $\hat\theta_{\text{Bayes}} = (\alpha + k)/(\alpha + \beta + n)$.

Interpretation: $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are "previous virtual flips" ($\alpha$ heads, $\beta$ tails).

### Naive Bayes Classifier

For classes $C_1, \ldots, C_K$ and features $\mathbf{x} = (x_1, \ldots, x_p)$:

$$\hat C = \arg\max_{C_k} P(C_k) \prod_{j=1}^{p} P(x_j \mid C_k)$$

Assumption of **conditional independence** of features given the class. Training $O(np)$, prediction $O(p)$. Excellent baseline in text classification (spam, sentiment, categorization).

### Likelihood ratio and diagnosis

$\text{LR}^+ = P(+\mid D)/P(+\mid \bar D) = \text{sens}/(1-\text{spec})$.

In odds scale: $\text{post}O = \text{LR}^+ \times \text{prior}O$. For two independent tests: $\text{LR}_{\text{combined}}^+ = \text{LR}_1^+ \times \text{LR}_2^+$.

Rule of thumb (Fagan nomogram): $\text{LR}^+ > 10$ produces clinically relevant change; $\text{LR}^+ > 100$ is diagnostic on its own.

### Engineering applications

- **Kalman filter**: Bayes in linear-Gaussian dynamic systems. The prior of the next iteration is the posterior of the previous one.
- **Particle filter**: Bayes in non-linear systems via samples (Sequential Monte Carlo).
- **Bayesian optimization**: prior on objective function (Gaussian process), updates with evaluations, maximizes acquisition function. BoTorch, Optuna TPE.
- **Reliability engineering**: prior + observed failures → posterior of failure rate $\lambda$.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

### Cox-Jaynes coherence

Cox (1946) and Jaynes (2003): any system of "plausibility" satisfying consistency, dependence only on relevant information, and transitivity is isomorphic to Kolmogorov probability. Bayes' theorem emerges as an inevitable consequence — it is not a methodological choice, it is the only coherent update rule.

### Subjectivity of priors and frequentist critique

The most common critique of Bayesianism is that priors are subjective. Bayesian response: every frequentist analysis has implicit priors ($p$-value tests under point prior at $\theta = \theta_0$; AIC assumes uniform prior over models). The difference is that the Bayesian makes them explicit.

Family of objective priors: **Jeffreys** (invariant to reparameterization), **reference priors** (Bernardo), **maximum entropy** (Jaynes). For the Bernoulli model, the Jeffreys prior is $\text{Beta}(1/2, 1/2)$.

### Prosecutor's fallacy

Confusing $P(E \mid H_0)$ with $P(H_0 \mid E)$. Example: DNA evidence with 1/1,000,000 frequency in the population does not imply $P(\text{guilty} \mid \text{DNA match}) = 0.9999\cdots$. With a prior of guilt of 1/1,000,000 (anyone in the city could be the culprit), the posterior is only 50%. The Sally Clark case (1999) in the UK illustrates how the fallacy can convict the innocent.

### Bayesian Model Averaging (BMA)

Instead of selecting one model, weight by posterior probability:

$$P(y_{\text{new}} \mid D) = \sum_M P(y_{\text{new}} \mid M, D)\,P(M \mid D)$$

Reduces overfitting and captures structural uncertainty. High computational cost — in practice, Stacking (ensemble by regression) is preferred.

### Empirical Bayes (James-Stein)

Estimate the prior itself from the data. In simultaneous estimation of $k \geq 3$ normal means, the James-Stein estimator **dominates** the MLE in total MSE criterion — a surprising result that won the [2021 Nobel Prize in Economics](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/2021/summary/) indirectly via panel econometrics (shrinkage). Efron-Morris (1977) shows applications in sports and fMRI.

### Bayesian causal inference

Pearl + Bayes: posterior on treatment effect in observational data, given an explicit causal DAG. Allows separating association from causation when the causal structure is known.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practice — real scenarios">

### Scenario 1: COVID-19 — tracking in endemic phase

Prevalence 5%. Rapid test: sensitivity 80%, specificity 95%.

Using frequencies in 10,000 people:
- Sick: $10,000 \times 0.05 = 500$.
- TP: $500 \times 0.80 = 400$.
- Healthy: 9,500.
- FP: $9,500 \times 0.05 = 475$.

PPV = $400/(400 + 475) \approx 45.7\%$.

Conclusion: even with a reasonable test, almost half of the positives are false. Automatic isolation of all positives would have a high economic cost. Indication: confirm with PCR before isolating for more than 24 hours.

### Scenario 2: Spam filter

Prior: $P(\text{spam}) = 0.3$. Word "FREE" appears in 60% of spams and 5% of hams.

$$P(\text{spam} \mid \text{FREE}) = \frac{0.60 \times 0.30}{0.60 \times 0.30 + 0.05 \times 0.70} = \frac{0.18}{0.215} \approx 83.7\%$$

Naive Bayes concatenates a multiplication per word. An email with "FREE", "WON", and "PRIZE" quickly reaches over 99% posterior spam probability.

### Scenario 3: Industrial quality control

Production line has historical defect rate: $p_0 = 2\%$. Prior: $\theta \sim \text{Beta}(2, 98)$ (equivalent to "historical data of 100 parts"). In a batch of 50 parts, we find 4 defective. Posterior:

$$\theta \mid 4 \sim \text{Beta}(2 + 4, 98 + 46) = \text{Beta}(6, 144)$$

Posterior mean: $6/150 = 4\%$ — higher than historical, warning sign. 95% posterior CI: approximately $[1.5\%, 8.5\%]$. Decision: stop line and investigate if posterior mean exceeds 5% or upper CI exceeds 10%.

</Porta>

## Solved examples

<Exemplo titulo="Direct calculation with law of total probability (application)" numero="1">

**Problem:** A factory has three production lines: A (40% of production), B (35%), and C (25%). Defect rates are: A: 2%, B: 3%, C: 5%. A part is randomly selected and is defective. What is the probability it was produced by line B?

**Strategy:** Apply the law of total probability to calculate $P(\text{defect})$, then use Bayes' theorem to obtain $P(B \mid \text{defect})$.

**Resolution:**

1. Define events: $H_A$, $H_B$, $H_C$ = part comes from line A, B, C. $D$ = defective part.

2. Priors: $P(H_A) = 0.40$, $P(H_B) = 0.35$, $P(H_C) = 0.25$.

3. Likelihoods: $P(D \mid H_A) = 0.02$, $P(D \mid H_B) = 0.03$, $P(D \mid H_C) = 0.05$.

4. Law of total probability:
$$P(D) = 0.02 \times 0.40 + 0.03 \times 0.35 + 0.05 \times 0.25 = 0.008 + 0.0105 + 0.0125 = 0.031$$

5. Bayes for line B:
$$P(H_B \mid D) = \frac{0.03 \times 0.35}{0.031} = \frac{0.0105}{0.031} \approx 0.339$$

**Verification:** Let's also calculate for A and C:
$P(H_A \mid D) = 0.008/0.031 \approx 0.258$; $P(H_C \mid D) = 0.0125/0.031 \approx 0.403$. Sum: $0.258 + 0.339 + 0.403 = 1.000$. Correct.

**Source.** [Grinstead & Snell — Introduction to Probability §4.1](https://chance.dartmouth.edu/teaching_aids/books_articles/probability_book/book.html) — GNU FDL. (Problem adapted from the structure of Example 4.11, three causes with different likelihoods.)

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Base rate fallacy and PPV (application)" numero="2">

**Problem:** Disease X affects 0.5% of the population. Diagnostic test: 95% sensitivity, 90% specificity. A patient tests positive. What is the positive predictive value?

**Strategy:** Use absolute frequencies in a sample of 10,000 people — method recommended by OpenIntro Statistics to avoid intuition errors.

**Resolution:**

In 10,000 people:

1. Sick: $10,000 \times 0.005 = 50$.
2. True positives (TP): $50 \times 0.95 = 47.5 \approx 48$ (rounded).
3. Healthy: $10,000 - 50 = 9,950$.
4. False positives (FP): $9,950 \times (1 - 0.90) = 9,950 \times 0.10 = 995$.
5. Total positives: $48 + 995 = 1,043$.
6. $\text{PPV} = 48/1043 \approx 4.6\%$.

**Verification:** Via direct formula:
$$\text{PPV} = \frac{0.95 \times 0.005}{0.95 \times 0.005 + 0.10 \times 0.995} = \frac{0.00475}{0.00475 + 0.0995} = \frac{0.00475}{0.10425} \approx 4.6\%$$

Matches. The intuition of "95% chance of being sick" is wrong by a factor of 20 — classic illustration of the base rate fallacy.

**Source.** [OpenIntro Statistics 4e §3.2](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/) — CC-BY-SA. (Structure of Example 3.10, medical diagnosis with low prevalence.)

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Urn with two causes — classic form (application)" numero="3">

**Problem:** Urn A contains 3 red balls and 2 blue. Urn B contains 1 red ball and 4 blue. An urn is chosen at random (50%-50%) and a ball is drawn. The ball is red. What is the probability the chosen urn was A?

**Strategy:** Two hypotheses ($H_A$ and $H_B$), uniform prior, evidence = red ball. Apply Bayes directly.

**Resolution:**

1. Priors: $P(H_A) = P(H_B) = 0.5$.
2. Likelihoods: $P(R \mid H_A) = 3/5 = 0.60$; $P(R \mid H_B) = 1/5 = 0.20$.
3. Total probability of red ball:
$$P(R) = 0.60 \times 0.50 + 0.20 \times 0.50 = 0.30 + 0.10 = 0.40$$
4. Posterior:
$$P(H_A \mid R) = \frac{0.60 \times 0.50}{0.40} = \frac{0.30}{0.40} = 0.75$$

**Verification:** $P(H_B \mid R) = 0.10/0.40 = 0.25$. Sum $= 1$. Urn A has red balls in a proportion 3 times higher than Urn B, so it makes sense that the posterior of A is 3 times that of B ($0.75 = 3 \times 0.25$).

**Source.** [Grinstead & Snell — Introduction to Probability §4.1](https://chance.dartmouth.edu/teaching_aids/books_articles/probability_book/book.html) — GNU FDL. (Adapted from Exercise 4.1.1 on two urns.)

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Sequential updating with two tests (intermediate)" numero="4">

**Problem:** Disease prevalence: 2%. Two independent tests: Test 1 with 90% sensitivity and 95% specificity; Test 2 with 85% sensitivity and 92% specificity. Both are positive. What is the posterior after both positive results?

**Strategy:** Apply Bayes sequentially: the posterior of Test 1 becomes the prior for Test 2.

**Resolution:**

**Step 1 — after Test 1 positive:**
$$P(D \mid T_1^+) = \frac{0.90 \times 0.02}{0.90 \times 0.02 + 0.05 \times 0.98} = \frac{0.018}{0.018 + 0.049} = \frac{0.018}{0.067} \approx 0.269$$

**Step 2 — after Test 2 positive (prior = 0.269):**
$$P(D \mid T_1^+, T_2^+) = \frac{0.85 \times 0.269}{0.85 \times 0.269 + 0.08 \times 0.731} = \frac{0.229}{0.229 + 0.0585} = \frac{0.229}{0.287} \approx 0.798$$

**Verification by odds form:**
- Prior odds: $0.02/0.98 \approx 0.0204$.
- $\text{LR}_1^+ = 0.90/0.05 = 18$; $\text{LR}_2^+ = 0.85/0.08 = 10.625$.
- Posterior odds: $0.0204 \times 18 \times 10.625 = 3.898$.
- Posterior: $3.898/(1 + 3.898) \approx 79.6\%$. Confirms the calculation above.

**Source.** [OpenIntro Statistics 4e §3.3](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/) — CC-BY-SA. (Extension of Example 3.13 on sequential updating with two independent tests.)

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Beta-binomial conjugate prior (advanced)" numero="5">

**Problem:** A quality control uses prior Beta(2, 8) for the defect rate $\theta$ of a production line (equivalent to "we historically observed 2 defects in 10 inspections"). In a new batch, 20 parts are inspected and 4 are found defective. Determine: (a) the posterior, (b) the posterior mean, (c) an approximate 90% credible interval.

**Strategy:** Use the Beta-Binomial conjugation property. Posterior = Beta($\alpha + k$, $\beta + n - k$). For the interval, use the normal approximation of the Beta distribution for moderate parameters.

**Resolution:**

(a) **Posterior:** Prior Beta(2, 8), $n = 20$, $k = 4$.
$$\theta \mid 4 \sim \text{Beta}(2 + 4,\; 8 + 20 - 4) = \text{Beta}(6, 24)$$

(b) **Posterior mean:** $\mu = \alpha/(\alpha + \beta) = 6/(6 + 24) = 6/30 = 0.20$.

Compare with prior: $\mu_{\text{prior}} = 2/10 = 0.20$. The MLE would be $k/n = 4/20 = 0.20$. In this case they coincide because the prior was constructed with the same proportions.

(c) **90% credible interval:** The Beta(6, 24) distribution has standard deviation $\sigma = \sqrt{\alpha\beta/((\alpha+\beta)^2(\alpha+\beta+1))} = \sqrt{6 \times 24/(900 \times 31)} \approx \sqrt{144/27900} \approx 0.0718$.

Approximate interval $\mu \pm 1.645\sigma$: $[0.20 - 0.118, 0.20 + 0.118] = [0.082, 0.318]$. (The exact interval, by quantiles of Beta(6,24), is approximately $[0.090, 0.338]$.)

**Verification:** As $n \to \infty$, the influence of the prior decreases and the posterior concentrates on the MLE. With prior Beta(2,8) and 200 parts observed with 40 defects, the posterior would be Beta(42, 168) with mean $42/210 \approx 0.20$ and standard deviation $\approx 0.028$ — much more concentrated.
