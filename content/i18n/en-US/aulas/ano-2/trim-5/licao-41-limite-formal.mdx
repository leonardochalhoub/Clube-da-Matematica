### ε-δ Definition of Limit

<Definicao titulo="Limit of a function at a point (Cauchy 1821 / Weierstrass 1872)">

Let $f$ be defined on a punctured neighborhood of $a$ (not necessarily at $a$ itself). We say that

$$\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L$$

if and only if, for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists $\delta > 0$ such that

$$0 < |x - a| < \delta \;\Longrightarrow\; |f(x) - L| < \varepsilon.$$

The condition $0 < |x - a|$ means $x \neq a$ — the value $f(a)$ is irrelevant to the existence of the limit.

</Definicao>

> "We say that the limit of $f(x)$, as $x$ approaches $a$, equals $L$, …if we can make the values of $f(x)$ arbitrarily close to $L$ …by restricting $x$ to be sufficiently close to $a$ (on either side of $a$) but not equal to $a$." — [OpenStax Calculus Vol. 1 §2.2](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/2-2-the-limit-of-a-function)

### The ε-δ Method: How to Build the Proof

1. Write $|f(x) - L|$ and manipulate algebraically until a multiple of $|x - a|$ appears.
2. Restrict $|x - a| < 1$ (or another constant) to control additional factors.
3. Choose $\delta = \min\bigl(1,\; \varepsilon / C\bigr)$ where $C$ is the coefficient obtained.
4. Verify that the chain $0 < |x - a| < \delta \Rightarrow |f(x) - L| < \varepsilon$ closes.

### Model proof: $\lim_{x \to 2}(3x + 1) = 7$

**Scratch work:** $|3x + 1 - 7| = |3x - 6| = 3|x - 2|$. For $3|x-2| < \varepsilon$, it suffices that $|x-2| < \varepsilon/3$.

**Formal proof:** Given $\varepsilon > 0$, take $\delta = \varepsilon/3$. If $0 < |x - 2| < \delta$, then
$$|f(x) - 7| = 3|x - 2| < 3 \cdot \frac{\varepsilon}{3} = \varepsilon. \quad \square$$

### One-Sided Limits

<Definicao titulo="One-sided limits">

- **Right-hand limit:** $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to a^+} f(x) = L$ if $\forall\varepsilon>0,\,\exists\delta>0: 0 < x - a < \delta \Rightarrow |f(x)-L|<\varepsilon$.
- **Left-hand limit:** $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to a^-} f(x) = L$ if $\forall\varepsilon>0,\,\exists\delta>0: 0 < a - x < \delta \Rightarrow |f(x)-L|<\varepsilon$.

</Definicao>

<Teorema titulo="Condition for existence of the two-sided limit">
$$\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L \;\iff\; \lim_{x \to a^+} f(x) = L \;\text{ and }\; \lim_{x \to a^-} f(x) = L.$$
</Teorema>

### Limit at Infinity and Infinity as a Limit

<Definicao titulo="Four types of limit">

| Type | Definition |
|---|---|
| $\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L$ | $\forall\varepsilon>0,\,\exists\delta>0: 0<\|x-a\|<\delta \Rightarrow \|f(x)-L\|<\varepsilon$ |
| $\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = +\infty$ | $\forall M>0,\,\exists\delta>0: 0<\|x-a\|<\delta \Rightarrow f(x)>M$ |
| $\lim_{x \to +\infty} f(x) = L$ | $\forall\varepsilon>0,\,\exists N>0: x>N \Rightarrow \|f(x)-L\|<\varepsilon$ |
| $\lim_{x \to +\infty} f(x) = +\infty$ | $\forall M>0,\,\exists N>0: x>N \Rightarrow f(x)>M$ |

</Definicao>

### Algebraic Properties of Limits

Let $\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L$ and $\lim_{x \to a} g(x) = M$. Then:

<Equation explicacao="Addition and multiplication of limits. Provable directly via ε-δ using the triangle inequality." latex="\lim_{x\to a}[f(x)+g(x)]=L+M, \qquad \lim_{x\to a}[f(x)\cdot g(x)]=L\cdot M" />

<Equation explicacao="Quotient of limits: valid when the limit of the denominator is nonzero." latex="\lim_{x\to a}\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}=\frac{L}{M}, \quad M\neq 0" />

<Teorema titulo="Squeeze theorem (sandwich theorem)">
If $g(x) \leq f(x) \leq h(x)$ on a neighborhood of $a$ and $\lim_{x\to a} g(x) = \lim_{x\to a} h(x) = L$, then $\lim_{x\to a} f(x) = L$.

**Classic application:** $|x\sin(1/x)| \leq |x| \to 0$, therefore $\lim_{x\to 0} x\sin(1/x) = 0$.
</Teorema>

### Notable Limits

<Equation explicacao="The four fundamental limits of calculus, used throughout the simplification of indeterminate forms." latex="\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{\sin x}{x}=1,\quad \lim_{x\to 0}\frac{1-\cos x}{x^2}=\tfrac{1}{2},\quad \lim_{x\to 0}\frac{e^x-1}{x}=1,\quad \lim_{x\to\infty}\!\Bigl(1+\tfrac{1}{x}\Bigr)^{\!x}=e" />

<Insight>

The ε-δ definition reverses the order of the dialogue: **you** choose the tolerance $\varepsilon$ (how close you want $f(x)$ to be to $L$), and **the mathematician** responds with $\delta$ (how close $x$ must be to $a$). The limit exists when the mathematician can always respond — no matter how small an $\varepsilon$ you demand.

</Insight>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

Imagine you are playing a game of walking toward a wrapped present without opening it.

You want to get **really close** to the present. I say: "fine, come within one step." You say: "I want even closer!" I say: "OK, half a step."

Whatever size of closeness you can dream up, I know how to tell you exactly how many tiny steps to take. That is what the mathematician calls a **limit**: $f(x)$ gets close to $L$ when $x$ gets close to $a$.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

Think of a GPS. You are driving to a destination $L$ on the map. The car never stops exactly at the destination, but it keeps getting closer.

The mathematician asks: "for any bubble I draw around $L$ — no matter how small — does the car enter that bubble?"

If the answer is always **yes**, we say the **limit exists and equals $L$**.

The letter $\varepsilon$ is the radius of the bubble around $L$. The letter $\delta$ is how close $x$ needs to be to the arrival point $a$ to guarantee it.

**You choose $\varepsilon$** (it can be 1, or 0.001, or anything at all). **The mathematician replies with $\delta$** that makes it work. If the mathematician can always reply, the limit exists.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old teen">

In Lesson 19 you saw the limit informally. Now it becomes rigorous.

The central idea: $\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L$ means I can make $f(x)$ as close to $L$ **as I like**, as long as I bring $x$ close enough to $a$.

Formalizing with two Greek letters:
- **$\varepsilon$** (epsilon): tolerance for $f(x)$ — you choose it.
- **$\delta$** (delta): tolerance for $x$ — I guarantee it.

If for **every** $\varepsilon > 0$ (no matter how small) I can find $\delta > 0$ such that $0 < |x - a| < \delta$ guarantees $|f(x) - L| < \varepsilon$, then the limit exists and equals $L$.

**Numerical table for $\lim_{x \to 1} x^2 = 1$:**

| $x$ | $x^2$ | $\lvert x^2 - 1 \rvert$ |
|---|---|---|
| $0.9$ | $0.81$ | $0.19$ |
| $0.99$ | $0.9801$ | $0.0199$ |
| $0.999$ | $0.998001$ | $0.001999$ |
| $1.001$ | $1.002001$ | $0.002001$ |
| $1.01$ | $1.0201$ | $0.0201$ |

As $x$ approaches 1, $|x^2 - 1|$ shrinks. For $|x^2 - 1| < 0.01$, it suffices that $|x - 1| < 0.003$ (approximately). That is $\delta$ in action.

**When the limit does NOT exist:**
- $\lim_{x \to 0} 1/x$: from the right it goes to $+\infty$, from the left to $-\infty$ — one-sided limits differ.
- $\lim_{x \to 0} \sin(1/x)$: oscillates infinitely between $-1$ and $1$ — does not converge.

<Insight>

The limit **does not depend** on $f(a)$. The function may not even be defined at $a$ — only the behavior at nearby points matters. That is why $\lim_{x \to 2} (x^2 - 4)/(x-2) = 4$, even though the fraction is undefined at $x = 2$.

</Insight>

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

The $\varepsilon$-$\delta$ definition is the foundation of rigorous calculus. Before it, Newton and Leibniz used "infinitesimals" — quantities smaller than any positive number yet nonzero. It worked in practice but was logically vulnerable (Berkeley, 1734: "ghosts of departed quantities").

Cauchy formalized it in 1821 in the [*Cours d'analyse*](https://gallica.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/bpt6k90196z). Weierstrass refined it in 1872. All of modern analysis starts there.

### Model proof: $\lim_{x \to 3} x^2 = 9$

**Scratch work:** $|x^2 - 9| = |x - 3|\cdot|x + 3|$. Restricting $|x - 3| < 1$, we get $|x| < 4$, so $|x + 3| < 7$. Thus $|x^2 - 9| < 7|x - 3|$. For $7|x - 3| < \varepsilon$, we need $|x - 3| < \varepsilon/7$.

**Formal proof:** Given $\varepsilon > 0$, take $\delta = \min(1, \varepsilon/7)$. If $0 < |x - 3| < \delta$, then $|x + 3| \leq |x - 3| + 6 < 1 + 6 = 7$ and therefore

$$|x^2 - 9| = |x-3|\cdot|x+3| < \frac{\varepsilon}{7}\cdot 7 = \varepsilon. \quad\square$$

### Indeterminate Forms and Algebraic Techniques

| Form | Technique |
|---|---|
| $0/0$ — polynomial | Factor and cancel |
| $0/0$ — with radical | Multiply by the conjugate |
| $\infty/\infty$ | Divide by the highest degree |
| $0 \cdot \infty$ | Rewrite as $0/0$ or $\infty/\infty$ |
| Notable limits | $\sin x/x = 1$, $(e^x-1)/x = 1$ |

### Direct Engineering Applications

- **Instantaneous velocity:** $v(t) = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \bigl(s(t+\Delta t) - s(t)\bigr)/\Delta t$.
- **Current in a circuit:** $i(t) = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \Delta q/\Delta t$.
- **Discretization error:** validates that $\lim_{h \to 0} (f(x+h) - f(x))/h = f'(x)$ (basis of Euler's method).
- **Control:** a system is stable if $\lim_{t \to \infty} y(t) = 0$ (impulse response decays).

### Sequential Characterization (Heine)

$\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L$ $\iff$ for every sequence $(x_n)$ with $x_n \to a$ and $x_n \neq a$, we have $f(x_n) \to L$. Useful for **proving a limit does not exist**: just exhibit two sequences with different limits.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

The $\varepsilon$-$\delta$ definition is a special case of limits in a **metric space** $(X, d)$: $\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L$ means $\forall\varepsilon>0, \exists\delta>0: 0 < d_X(x,a) < \delta \Rightarrow d_Y(f(x),L) < \varepsilon$. The whole theory of calculus extends to Banach spaces, Fréchet spaces, and beyond.

### Topological Formulation (without a metric)

In a general topological space, $f(x) \to L$ as $x \to a$ if for every open neighborhood $V$ of $L$ there exists a neighborhood $U$ of $a$ such that $f(U \setminus \{a\}) \subseteq V$. This is equivalent to: $f$ is continuous at $a$ (except for the excluded point).

### Non-Standard Analysis (Robinson 1966)

The $\varepsilon$-$\delta$ definition has an equivalent formulation in the **hyperreals** $^*\mathbb{R}$: $\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L$ $\iff$ for every infinitesimal $\delta \neq 0$, $f(a+\delta) - L$ is infinitesimal. The notation approaches Leibniz's — but the logical power is the same as Weierstrass's.

### Uniform vs. Pointwise Convergence

Sequence of functions $f_n \to f$:
- **Pointwise:** $\forall x, \forall\varepsilon, \exists N(\varepsilon,x)$. $N$ depends on $x$.
- **Uniform:** $\forall\varepsilon, \exists N(\varepsilon), \forall x$. $N$ does not depend on $x$.

The order of quantifiers changes everything: uniform convergence preserves continuity and allows exchanging limits with integrals. Pointwise convergence does not. Classic counterexample: $f_n(x) = x^n$ on $[0,1]$ converges pointwise to the indicator function of $\{1\}$, but not uniformly.

### Numerical Tolerances as Finite ε-δ

In scientific computing (NumPy, MATLAB, SciPy), `atol` and `rtol` are discrete versions of $\varepsilon$. Convergence of iterations (Newton, conjugate gradient) is declared when the residual drops below `tol`. It is $\varepsilon$-$\delta$ with $\delta$ fixed by the user — the theory guarantees that, for arbitrary $\varepsilon$, the method always converges given sufficiently small $\delta$.

### Distribution Theory (Schwartz)

Distributions extend the notion of function: $\delta_0$ (Dirac delta) is not a function but a continuous linear functional on $C_c^\infty(\mathbb{R})$. Convergence $f_n \to T$ in the weak-* topology of $\mathcal{D}'$ replaces $\varepsilon$-$\delta$ with convergence of functionals. Central application: solutions of PDEs in mathematical physics.

</Porta>

### Recipe: How to Compute a Limit

**Step 1 — Direct substitution.** Try $f(a)$. If defined and $f$ is continuous, you are done.

**Step 2 — Algebraic manipulation** (when there is a $0/0$ indeterminate form):
- **Factor and cancel:** $(x^2 - 4)/(x - 2) = (x+2)(x-2)/(x-2) = x + 2$ for $x \neq 2$. So $\lim_{x\to 2} = 4$.
- **Conjugate:** $(\sqrt{x+1}-1)/x \cdot (\sqrt{x+1}+1)/(\sqrt{x+1}+1) = 1/(\sqrt{x+1}+1) \to 1/2$.
- **Notable limits:** $\lim_{x\to 0}\sin(5x)/(3x) = (5/3)\lim_{x\to 0}\sin(5x)/(5x) = 5/3$.

**Step 3 — Limits at infinity:** divide numerator and denominator by the highest power of $x$.

**Step 4 — SymPy to verify:**

```python
import sympy as sp
x = sp.Symbol('x')
sp.limit((x**2 - 4)/(x - 2), x, 2)           # 4
sp.limit(sp.sin(x)/x, x, 0)                  # 1
sp.limit((1 + 1/x)**x, x, sp.oo)             # E
sp.limit(1/x, x, 0, '+')                     # oo
sp.limit(1/x, x, 0, '-')                     # -oo
```

### When the Limit Does Not Exist

1. **One-sided limits differ:** $\lim_{x\to 0^+}1/x = +\infty \neq -\infty = \lim_{x\to 0^-}1/x$.
2. **Oscillation:** $\sin(1/x)$ oscillates infinitely near $0$ — does not converge to any value.
3. **Blow-up without a definite sign:** $1/x^2 \to +\infty$ (infinite limit, but well-behaved — both sides agree).

### Instantaneous Velocity: The Limit That Generates the Derivative

Position $s(t) = t^2$ (in meters). Average velocity between $t$ and $t + h$:

$$\frac{s(t+h) - s(t)}{h} = \frac{(t+h)^2 - t^2}{h} = \frac{2th + h^2}{h} = 2t + h.$$

Taking $h \to 0$: $v(t) = \lim_{h\to 0}(2t + h) = 2t$. This limit is the derivative — the central concept of Term 6.

## Worked Examples

<Exemplo titulo="Intuitive computation via numerical table (application)" numero="1">

**Problem.** Estimate $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 2} \frac{x^2 - 4}{x - 2}$ numerically and confirm algebraically.

**Strategy.** When direct substitution yields $0/0$, a numerical table suggests the value and factoring confirms it.

**Solution:**

Table of values near $x = 2$ (with $x \neq 2$):

| $x$ | $(x^2-4)/(x-2)$ |
|---|---|
| $1.9$ | $3.9$ |
| $1.99$ | $3.99$ |
| $1.999$ | $3.999$ |
| $2.001$ | $4.001$ |
| $2.01$ | $4.01$ |
| $2.1$ | $4.1$ |

The table suggests $\lim = 4$.

**Algebraic confirmation:** For $x \neq 2$,
$$\frac{x^2 - 4}{x - 2} = \frac{(x-2)(x+2)}{x-2} = x + 2.$$
Therefore $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 2}(x+2) = 4$.

**Verification:** the factoring cancels the $(x-2)$ factor that caused the indeterminate form. The value $f(2)$ is undefined, but the limit exists and equals 4.

**Source.** [Active Calculus §1.2, Preview Activity 1.2.1](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-1-2-lim.html) — license CC-BY-SA.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Formal ε-δ proof for a linear function (formal)" numero="2">

**Problem.** Prove rigorously via $\varepsilon$-$\delta$ that $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 3}(5x - 2) = 13$.

**Strategy.** Express $|f(x) - L|$ in terms of $|x - a|$, identify the linear relation, and choose $\delta = \varepsilon / C$.

**Solution:**

**Scratch work (to guide the choice of $\delta$):**
$$|f(x) - 13| = |5x - 2 - 13| = |5x - 15| = 5|x - 3|.$$
For $5|x-3| < \varepsilon$, we need $|x - 3| < \varepsilon/5$.

**Formal proof:** Let $\varepsilon > 0$ be given. Take $\delta = \varepsilon/5$. Suppose $0 < |x - 3| < \delta$. Then:
$$|(5x - 2) - 13| = 5|x - 3| < 5\cdot\frac{\varepsilon}{5} = \varepsilon.$$
Therefore, by definition, $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 3}(5x - 2) = 13$. $\square$

**Remark:** for linear functions $f(x) = mx + b$, the relation $|f(x) - (ma+b)| = |m|\cdot|x-a|$ is always direct. The choice $\delta = \varepsilon/|m|$ works without additional restrictions.

**Verification:** Taking $\varepsilon = 0.1$: $\delta = 0.02$. For $|x - 3| < 0.02$, we have $|5x - 15| = 5|x-3| < 5(0.02) = 0.1 = \varepsilon$. Checks out.

**Source.** [OpenStax Calculus Vol. 1 §2.5, Example 2.39](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/2-5-the-precise-definition-of-a-limit) — license CC-BY-NC-SA.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="One-sided limits and existence of the limit (comprehension)" numero="3">

**Problem.** Consider the function $f(x) = |x|/x$ for $x \neq 0$. Compute the one-sided limits at $x = 0$ and determine whether $\lim_{x \to 0} f(x)$ exists.

**Strategy.** Compute the right-hand and left-hand limits separately. The two-sided limit exists only if both coincide.

**Solution:**

Observe that:
$$f(x) = \frac{|x|}{x} = \begin{cases} 1, & x > 0, \\ -1, & x < 0. \end{cases}$$

**Right-hand limit** ($x \to 0^+$, i.e., $x > 0$): $f(x) = 1$ for all $x > 0$, so $\lim_{x \to 0^+} f(x) = 1$.

**Left-hand limit** ($x \to 0^-$, i.e., $x < 0$): $f(x) = -1$ for all $x < 0$, so $\lim_{x \to 0^-} f(x) = -1$.

Since $\lim_{x \to 0^+} f(x) = 1 \neq -1 = \lim_{x \to 0^-} f(x)$, the one-sided limits differ.

**Conclusion:** $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0} f(x)$ **does not exist**.

**Verification:** the graph of $f$ is a "step" with a jump at $x = 0$. It is the simplest possible jump discontinuity.

**Source.** [OpenStax Calculus Vol. 1 §2.2, Example 2.7](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/2-2-the-limit-of-a-function) — license CC-BY-NC-SA.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Limit at infinity of a rational function (application)" numero="4">

**Problem.** Compute $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to +\infty} \frac{3x^2 + 5x - 1}{2x^2 - 7}$.

**Strategy.** Divide numerator and denominator by the highest power of $x$ present ($x^2$) and use the fact that $k/x^n \to 0$ as $x \to \infty$ for any constant $k$ and $n > 0$.

**Solution:**

Divide everything by $x^2$:
$$\frac{3x^2 + 5x - 1}{2x^2 - 7} = \frac{3 + 5/x - 1/x^2}{2 - 7/x^2}.$$

As $x \to +\infty$: $5/x \to 0$, $1/x^2 \to 0$, $7/x^2 \to 0$. Therefore:
$$\lim_{x \to +\infty} \frac{3 + 5/x - 1/x^2}{2 - 7/x^2} = \frac{3 + 0 - 0}{2 - 0} = \frac{3}{2}.$$

**General rule:** for $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{a_n x^n + \cdots}{b_m x^m + \cdots}$, the result depends on $n$ vs. $m$:
- $n < m$: limit $= 0$.
- $n = m$: limit $= a_n/b_m$.
- $n > m$: limit $= \pm\infty$ (depending on the signs).

**Verification:** at $x = 1000$: $(3 \cdot 10^6 + 5000 - 1)/(2 \cdot 10^6 - 7) \approx 3005000/1999993 \approx 1.5025$ — converges to $3/2$. ✓

**Source.** [APEX Calculus §1.6, Example 1.6.1](https://www.apexcalculus.com/) — license CC-BY-NC.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Limit that does not exist — oscillation (challenge)" numero="5">

**Problem.** Show that $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0} \sin\!\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)$ does not exist.

**Strategy.** Use Heine's sequential characterization: if the limit existed, every sequence $x_n \to 0$ with $x_n \neq 0$ would satisfy $f(x_n) \to L$. We exhibit two sequences whose images converge to distinct values.

**Solution:**

Consider the two sequences:
$$x_n = \frac{1}{2n\pi} \quad \text{and} \quad y_n = \frac{2}{(4n+1)\pi}, \quad n \in \mathbb{N}.$$

Both satisfy $x_n \to 0$ and $y_n \to 0$ (denominators grow without bound).

**For $x_n$:**
$$\sin\!\left(\frac{1}{x_n}\right) = \sin(2n\pi) = 0 \quad \text{for all } n.$$
So $f(x_n) \to 0$.

**For $y_n$:**
$$\sin\!\left(\frac{1}{y_n}\right) = \sin\!\left(\frac{(4n+1)\pi}{2}\right) = \sin\!\left(2n\pi + \frac{\pi}{2}\right) = 1 \quad \text{for all } n.$$
So $f(y_n) \to 1$.

Since two sequences converging to $0$ produce different limits ($0$ and $1$), by Heine's characterization the limit $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 0}\sin(1/x)$ **does not exist**. $\square$

**Note:** the graph oscillates infinitely between $-1$ and $1$ as $x \to 0$ — no specific value is privileged.

**Source.** [Active Calculus §1.2, Activity 1.2.3](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-1-2-lim.html) — license CC-BY-SA.
</Exemplo>
