<Porta nivel="formal" titulo="Rigorous definitions">

### One-sided limits: epsilon-delta definitions

<Definicao titulo="Right-hand limit">

We say <Eq>{`\\lim_{x \\to a^+} f(x) = L`}</Eq> if, for every <Eq>{`\\varepsilon > 0`}</Eq>, there exists <Eq>{`\\delta > 0`}</Eq> such that

$$0 < x - a < \delta \implies \lvert f(x) - L \rvert < \varepsilon.$$

The condition <Eq>{`0 < x - a < \\delta`}</Eq> is equivalent to <Eq>{`a < x < a + \\delta`}</Eq>: we require <Eq>{`x`}</Eq> to be **to the right** of <Eq>{`a`}</Eq>, but never equal to <Eq>{`a`}</Eq>.

</Definicao>

<Definicao titulo="Left-hand limit">

We say <Eq>{`\\lim_{x \\to a^-} f(x) = M`}</Eq> if, for every <Eq>{`\\varepsilon > 0`}</Eq>, there exists <Eq>{`\\delta > 0`}</Eq> such that

$$0 < a - x < \delta \implies \lvert f(x) - M \rvert < \varepsilon.$$

The condition <Eq>{`0 < a - x < \\delta`}</Eq> is equivalent to <Eq>{`a - \\delta < x < a`}</Eq>: we require <Eq>{`x`}</Eq> to be **to the left** of <Eq>{`a`}</Eq>.

</Definicao>

> "We say the function has a right-hand limit equal to L at a if for every number ε > 0 there exists a corresponding number δ > 0 such that for all x with 0 < x − a < δ we have |f(x) − L| < ε." — [OpenStax Calculus Volume 1, §2.2](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/2-2-the-limit-of-a-function)

### Theorem on existence via one-sided limits

<Teorema titulo="Criterion for existence of the two-sided limit">

The limit <Eq>{`\\lim_{x \\to a} f(x)`}</Eq> exists and equals <Eq>{`L`}</Eq> if and only if

<Eq display>{`\\lim_{x \\to a^-} f(x) = L \\quad \\text{and} \\quad \\lim_{x \\to a^+} f(x) = L.`}</Eq>

If <Eq>{`\\lim_{x \\to a^-} f(x) \\neq \\lim_{x \\to a^+} f(x)`}</Eq>, the two-sided limit does not exist. This situation is called a **jump discontinuity**.

</Teorema>

> "A function has a limit at a point if and only if both the left and right limits exist at that point and are equal." — [APEX Calculus, §1.4](https://www.apexcalculus.com/)

### Table of quantified forms

| Type of limit | Condition on $x$ | Quantified form |
|---|---|---|
| $\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L$ | $0 < \lvert x - a \rvert < \delta$ | $\lvert f(x) - L \rvert < \varepsilon$ |
| $\lim_{x \to a^+} f(x) = L$ | $0 < x - a < \delta$ | $\lvert f(x) - L \rvert < \varepsilon$ |
| $\lim_{x \to a^-} f(x) = L$ | $0 < a - x < \delta$ | $\lvert f(x) - L \rvert < \varepsilon$ |
| $\lim_{x \to a^+} f(x) = +\infty$ | $0 < x - a < \delta$ | $f(x) > M$ |

### Domain and limits at boundary

If $a$ is the left endpoint of the domain of $f$ (for example, $f(x) = \sqrt{x}$ with domain $[0, +\infty)$), then only the right-hand limit is relevant:

$$\lim_{x \to 0^+} \sqrt{x} = 0.$$

The left-hand limit does not exist due to lack of domain. In these cases, the two-sided limit is identified with the one-sided limit that exists.

<Insight>

The theorem on existence via one-sided limits is the **universal diagnostic tool** for jump discontinuities: every jump discontinuity is detected by computing and comparing the two one-sided limits. If they coincide, the limit exists — but the function may still be discontinuous if $f(a)$ differs from this value (removable discontinuity, treated in Lesson 43).

</Insight>

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

Imagine you are walking down a hallway toward a door.

You can come from **the right side** of the hallway, or **the left side**.

- If you arrive from both sides and see **the same thing** behind the door — great! The limit exists.
- If from each side you see **different things** — one side has a toy, the other has a book — then you can't talk about "the thing" behind the door. Each side has its own answer.

Mathematicians call this a **one-sided limit**: what you find when arriving from just one side.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

Think about going up and down a stair step.

The function $f(x) = \lfloor x \rfloor$ (the greatest integer less than or equal to $x$) equals $1$ for $x \in [1, 2)$ and equals $2$ for $x \in [2, 3)$.

When $x$ approaches $2$:
- Coming **from the left** (with 1.9; 1.99; 1.999; ...): $f(x) = 1$ the whole time. Left limit $= 1$.
- Coming **from the right** (with 2.1; 2.01; 2.001; ...): $f(x) = 2$ the whole time. Right limit $= 2$.

The two sides give **different** answers (1 and 2). That is why the two-sided limit at $x = 2$ does not exist. It is a **jump**.

The idea is simple: whenever a function "jumps" from one value to another at a point, we need to compute the two sides separately.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old teen">

Every time a function changes "rule" at some point — like piecewise-defined functions — you need to ask: **do the two sides reach the same value?**

### The criterion in practice

Given <Eq>{`f(x) = \\begin{cases} x + 1 & x < 2 \\\\ 3 & x = 2 \\\\ x^2 - 1 & x > 2 \\end{cases}`}</Eq>

- From the left: $\lim_{x \to 2^-} (x + 1) = 3$.
- From the right: $\lim_{x \to 2^+} (x^2 - 1) = 4 - 1 = 3$.
- Both equal 3: two-sided limit $= 3$. (And since $f(2) = 3$ too, $f$ is continuous at 2.)

Now change the rule: <Eq>{`g(x) = \\begin{cases} x + 1 & x < 2 \\\\ x^2 & x \\geq 2 \\end{cases}`}</Eq>

- From the left: $\lim_{x \to 2^-} (x + 1) = 3$.
- From the right: $\lim_{x \to 2^+} x^2 = 4$.
- $3 \neq 4$: two-sided limit **does not exist**. Jump discontinuity of size $|4 - 3| = 1$.

### Infinite one-sided limits

$\lim_{x \to 0^+} \dfrac{1}{x} = +\infty$ (positive values grow without bound).

$\lim_{x \to 0^-} \dfrac{1}{x} = -\infty$ (negative values decrease without bound).

Since the sides differ (one goes to $+\infty$, the other to $-\infty$), the two-sided limit of $1/x$ at $x = 0$ does not exist — and $x = 0$ is a vertical asymptote.

### Limits at domain boundary

$\lim_{x \to 0^+} \sqrt{x} = 0$. The function $\sqrt{x}$ is not defined for $x < 0$, so only the right-hand limit exists. This limit is 0, and $f$ is continuous on the right at $x = 0$.

<Insight>

The theorem on existence via one-sided limits is the **universal diagnostic tool** for discontinuities: every jump discontinuity is detected by computing and comparing the two one-sided limits. If they coincide, the limit exists — but the function may still be discontinuous if $f(a)$ differs from this value (removable discontinuity, treated in Lesson 43).

</Insight>

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

One-sided limits are the right instrument for analyzing **piecewise-defined functions**, **integer-valued functions**, **switched signals**, and **systems with abrupt discontinuities**.

### Rigorous proof: $\lim_{x \to 0^+} \dfrac{1}{x} = +\infty$

Given $M > 0$ arbitrary. Choose $\delta = 1/M$. For $0 < x < \delta = 1/M$:

$$\frac{1}{x} > \frac{1}{\delta} = M.$$

Therefore, for any chosen $M > 0$, $f(x) = 1/x > M$ whenever $0 < x < 1/M$. The definition is satisfied. $\square$

### Proof: $\lim_{x \to 0^-} \dfrac{1}{x} = -\infty$

Given $M < 0$ arbitrary. Choose $\delta = 1/|M|$. For $-\delta < x < 0$:

$$x < 0 \implies \frac{1}{x} < 0 \implies \frac{1}{x} < -|M| = M.$$

Therefore $1/x < M$ whenever $-1/|M| < x < 0$. $\square$

### Proof: $\lim_{x \to 0^+} \sqrt{x} = 0$

Given $\varepsilon > 0$. Choose $\delta = \varepsilon^2$. For $0 < x < \delta$:

$$|\sqrt{x} - 0| = \sqrt{x} < \sqrt{\delta} = \sqrt{\varepsilon^2} = \varepsilon. \quad \square$$

### Analysis of switched functions (control systems)

The sign function <Eq>{`\\mathrm{sgn}(x) = \\begin{cases} +1 & x > 0 \\\\ 0 & x = 0 \\\\ -1 & x < 0 \\end{cases}`}</Eq> has:

$$\lim_{x \to 0^+} \mathrm{sgn}(x) = +1, \quad \lim_{x \to 0^-} \mathrm{sgn}(x) = -1.$$

The jump of amplitude $2$ at $x = 0$ generates **chattering** in sliding-mode controllers. The practical solution is to replace $\mathrm{sgn}(x)$ with $\tanh(kx)$ for large $k$.

### Cumulative distribution functions in probability

The cumulative distribution function $F_X(x) = P(X \leq x)$ is non-decreasing and right-continuous: $\lim_{x \to a^+} F_X(x) = F_X(a)$. The left-hand limit is $F_X(a^-) = P(X < a)$. The jump $F_X(a) - F_X(a^-) = P(X = a)$ — for continuous random variables, the jump is zero; for discrete variables, it is the point probability.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

### Topological interpretation

The right-hand limit at $a$ is the limit of $f$ with respect to the filter of right neighborhoods of $a$: $\mathcal{F}^+ = \{V \cap (a, +\infty) : V \in \mathcal{V}(a)\}$. The left-hand limit uses the filter $\mathcal{F}^-$. The two-sided limit uses the filter of punctured neighborhoods $\mathcal{V}^*(a)$.

The theorem on existence via one-sided limits states that $\mathcal{V}^*(a) = \mathcal{F}^+ \vee \mathcal{F}^-$ (supremum of the two filters in the reverse inclusion order), so a limit exists in $\mathcal{V}^*(a)$ if and only if it exists in both $\mathcal{F}^\pm$ and coincides.

### Limsup and liminf of one-sided limits

For functions not necessarily convergent, define:

$$\limsup_{x \to a^+} f(x) = \inf_{\delta > 0} \sup_{0 < x - a < \delta} f(x), \quad \liminf_{x \to a^+} f(x) = \sup_{\delta > 0} \inf_{0 < x - a < \delta} f(x).$$

The limit $\lim_{x \to a^+} f(x)$ exists in $\overline{\mathbb{R}}$ if and only if $\limsup = \liminf$, and then both coincide with the limit.

### Functions of bounded variation

A function $f : [a, b] \to \mathbb{R}$ of bounded variation possesses one-sided limits at **every point** in the interior. The set of discontinuities is at most countable. This guarantees that monotone functions have one-sided limits at every point — a classical result of Darboux-Jordan.

### PDE and transmission conditions

In interface problems (heat conduction with two materials, waves in stratified media), the solution $u$ satisfies transmission conditions that are essentially equalities between one-sided limits: $\lim_{x \to a^-} u(x) = \lim_{x \to a^+} u(x)$ (continuity of potential) and $\lim_{x \to a^-} \kappa_1 u'(x) = \lim_{x \to a^+} \kappa_2 u'(x)$ (continuity of flux), where $\kappa_i$ are the coefficients of the two media.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practical application — tiered pricing and tariff steps">

One-sided limits appear whenever a system changes regime abruptly.

### Recipe for computing one-sided limits

**Step 1.** Identify from which side $x$ approaches ($a^+$ or $a^-$).

**Step 2.** If $f$ is defined piecewise, select the "active formula" for that side.

**Step 3.** Substitute $x \to a$ into that formula (using limit algebra normally).

**Step 4.** Compare the two results. If they coincide, the two-sided limit exists and is that value.

### Example: tiered electricity rates (ANEEL 2025)

| Consumption range (kWh/month) | Rate ($/kWh) |
|---|---|
| $[0, 30)$ | \$0.42 |
| $[30, 100)$ | \$0.58 |
| $[100, 220)$ | \$0.71 |
| $[220, +\infty)$ | \$0.85 |

The rate $t(c)$ as a function of consumption $c$ has a jump at $c = 30$:

$$\lim_{c \to 30^-} t(c) = 0.42, \quad \lim_{c \to 30^+} t(c) = 0.58.$$

The one-sided limits exist but differ — jump discontinuity. The total cost (integral of the rate) is continuous; only the marginal rate jumps.

### Common errors

1. **Forgetting to check both sides.** For any point where the formula changes, always compute both $a^+$ and $a^-$.
2. **Confusing $f(a)$ with $\lim_{x \to a} f(x)$.** The value of the function at the point does not affect the limit.
3. **Saying "limit does not exist" when there is an infinite limit.** Infinite limit is a special case — we write $\lim = +\infty$, but formally it is not a real number.

</Porta>

## Worked examples

> Five examples with increasing difficulty — from reading graphs to determining constants for limit existence. Each example cites the source: the original problem always comes from an open book.

<Exemplo titulo="Reading one-sided limits from a graph" numero="1">

**Problem.** The graph of $f$ shows: the curve from the left of $x = 2$ approaches $y = 3$ (open dot); the curve from the right approaches $y = 5$ (open dot); there is a filled dot at $(2, 1)$. Determine $\lim_{x \to 2^-} f(x)$, $\lim_{x \to 2^+} f(x)$, $\lim_{x \to 2} f(x)$, and $f(2)$.

**Strategy.** Each piece of information from the graph plays a distinct role: the curve reveals the one-sided limits; the filled dot reveals the function value. The two-sided limit exists only if the one-sided limits coincide.

**Solution.**

1. **Left-hand limit.** The curve approaches $y = 3$ as $x \to 2^-$ (open dot confirms that 3 is not attained). Thus $\lim_{x \to 2^-} f(x) = 3$.

2. **Right-hand limit.** The curve approaches $y = 5$ as $x \to 2^+$. Thus $\lim_{x \to 2^+} f(x) = 5$.

3. **Two-sided limit.** Since $3 \neq 5$, by the existence criterion: $\lim_{x \to 2} f(x)$ **does not exist**. There is a jump discontinuity of size $|5 - 3| = 2$.

4. **Function value.** The filled dot at $(2, 1)$ indicates $f(2) = 1$. This value is independent of the one-sided limits.

**Verification.** $f(2) = 1$ differs from both one-sided limits (3 and 5), which is consistent: the point value does not alter the limits.

**Source.** [OpenStax Calculus Volume 1, §2.2](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/2-2-the-limit-of-a-function) — license CC-BY-NC-SA 4.0.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="One-sided limits of a piecewise-defined function" numero="2">

**Problem.** Let

<Eq>{`g(x) = \\begin{cases} x^2 - 1 & x < 3 \\\\ 2x - 3 & x \\geq 3 \\\\  \\end{cases}`}</Eq>

Compute $\lim_{x \to 3^-} g(x)$, $\lim_{x \to 3^+} g(x)$, and determine whether $\lim_{x \to 3} g(x)$ exists.

**Strategy.** For $x < 3$ we use the formula $x^2 - 1$; for $x > 3$ (in the right-hand limit, $x$ is never exactly 3) we use $2x - 3$. The sign $\geq$ in $g$ only affects the value $g(3)$, not the one-sided limit.

**Solution.**

1. **Left-hand limit** ($x \to 3^-$, with $x < 3$):

$$\lim_{x \to 3^-} g(x) = \lim_{x \to 3} (x^2 - 1) = 9 - 1 = 8.$$

2. **Right-hand limit** ($x \to 3^+$, with $x > 3$):

$$\lim_{x \to 3^+} g(x) = \lim_{x \to 3} (2x - 3) = 6 - 3 = 3.$$

3. **Comparison.** $8 \neq 3$, therefore $\lim_{x \to 3} g(x)$ **does not exist**. The jump has size $|8 - 3| = 5$.

4. **Function value.** $g(3) = 2(3) - 3 = 3$.

**Verification.** $\lim_{x \to 3^-} g(x) = 8 \neq g(3) = 3$: the function is also discontinuous on the left at $x = 3$.

**Source.** [OpenStax Calculus Volume 1, §2.2, example 2.13](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/2-2-the-limit-of-a-function) — license CC-BY-NC-SA 4.0.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Determining a constant for limit existence" numero="3">

**Problem.** The function

<Eq>{`h(x) = \\begin{cases} ax + 1 & x < 2 \\\\ x^2 - 1 & x \\geq 2 \\end{cases}`}</Eq>

should have $\lim_{x \to 2} h(x)$ existing. Find the value of $a$.

**Strategy.** For the two-sided limit to exist, the one-sided limits must be equal. Compute each one as a function of $a$ and set them equal.

**Solution.**

1. **Left-hand limit:**

$$\lim_{x \to 2^-} h(x) = \lim_{x \to 2} (ax + 1) = 2a + 1.$$

2. **Right-hand limit:**

$$\lim_{x \to 2^+} h(x) = \lim_{x \to 2} (x^2 - 1) = 4 - 1 = 3.$$

3. **Existence condition:** $2a + 1 = 3 \implies 2a = 2 \implies a = 1$.

4. **Verification.** With $a = 1$: left-hand limit $= 3$, right-hand limit $= 3$. The two-sided limit exists and equals $3$. Since $h(2) = 4 - 1 = 3$ as well, $h$ is continuous at $x = 2$ when $a = 1$.

**Source.** [APEX Calculus, §1.4, exercise 1.4.7](https://www.apexcalculus.com/) — license CC-BY-NC 4.0.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Infinite one-sided limit and vertical asymptote" numero="4">

**Problem.** Compute $\lim_{x \to 1^+} \dfrac{1}{x - 1}$ and $\lim_{x \to 1^-} \dfrac{1}{x - 1}$. Identify the vertical asymptote.

**Strategy.** Analyze the sign of $x - 1$ when $x \to 1^+$ (positive denominator) and $x \to 1^-$ (negative denominator).

**Solution.**

1. **From the right** ($x > 1$): $x - 1 > 0$, so $1/(x - 1) > 0$. As $x \to 1^+$, $x - 1 \to 0^+$, hence $1/(x-1) \to +\infty$.

$$\lim_{x \to 1^+} \frac{1}{x - 1} = +\infty.$$

2. **From the left** ($x < 1$): $x - 1 < 0$, so $1/(x - 1) < 0$. As $x \to 1^-$, $x - 1 \to 0^-$, hence $1/(x-1) \to -\infty$.

$$\lim_{x \to 1^-} \frac{1}{x - 1} = -\infty.$$

3. **Vertical asymptote.** Since at least one one-sided limit is infinite at $x = 1$, the line $x = 1$ is a vertical asymptote of the graph of $f(x) = 1/(x-1)$.

4. **Two-sided limit.** The two sides differ ($+\infty \neq -\infty$); the two-sided limit does not exist.

**Verification.** At $x = 1.01$: $1/(0.01) = 100 > 0$. At $x = 0.99$: $1/(-0.01) = -100 < 0$. Signs confirmed.

**Source.** [APEX Calculus, §1.4, example 1.4.2](https://www.apexcalculus.com/) — license CC-BY-NC 4.0.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Tiered rates: one-sided limits in real application" numero="5">

**Problem.** A streaming service charges a monthly fee $P(n)$ (in USD) depending on the number of simultaneous screens $n$:

<Eq>{`P(n) = \\begin{cases} 19.90 & 1 \\leq n \\leq 2 \\\\ 34.90 & 3 \\leq n \\leq 4 \\\\ 54.90 & n \\geq 5 \\\\  \\end{cases}`}</Eq>

Model $P$ as a function of $n \in \mathbb{R}^+$ by piecewise-constant interpolation. Compute the one-sided limits at $n = 2$ and at $n = 4$.

**Strategy.** Extend $P$ to real $n$ by step interpolation: the range $[1, 2]$ is \$19.90 up to $n = 2.999\ldots$; the next range starts at $n = 3$. Compute limits using the formula of each range.

**Solution.**

1. **At $n = 2$:**

$$\lim_{n \to 2^-} P(n) = 19.90 \quad \text{(range } [1, 2]\text{)}, \qquad \lim_{n \to 2^+} P(n) = 34.90 \quad \text{(next range)}.$$

Since \$19.90 $\neq$ \$34.90, the two-sided limit at $n = 2$ **does not exist**. The jump is \$15.00.

2. **At $n = 4$:**

$$\lim_{n \to 4^-} P(n) = 34.90, \qquad \lim_{n \to 4^+} P(n) = 54.90.$$

Since \$34.90 $\neq$ \$54.90, the two-sided limit at $n = 4$ **does not exist**. The jump is \$20.00.

3. **Interpretation.** Each tier boundary is a jump discontinuity. The customer pays the same price for any number of screens within the tier — a piecewise-constant function, with discontinuities at the tier transition points.

**Verification.** The value $P(2) = 19.90$ (defined by the contract) may differ from the right-hand limit — illustrating that $f(a)$ and $\lim_{x \to a^+} f(x)$ are distinct objects.

**Source.** [Active Calculus, §1.2, activity 1.2.2](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-1-2-lim.html) — license CC-BY-NC-SA 4.0.

</Exemplo>
