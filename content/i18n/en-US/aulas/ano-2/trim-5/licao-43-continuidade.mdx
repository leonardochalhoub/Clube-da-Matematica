### Continuity at a point

<Definicao titulo="Point continuity — three conditions">

Let $f$ be a real-valued function and $a$ be a point in the domain or an accumulation point. We say that $f$ is **continuous at $a$** if, and only if, the three conditions below hold simultaneously:

1. $f(a)$ is defined (the point belongs to the domain of $f$).
2. $\lim_{x \to a} f(x)$ exists (the one-sided limits coincide).
3. $\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = f(a)$ (the limit equals the function value).

If any of these conditions fails, $f$ is **discontinuous at $a$**.

</Definicao>

> "We say that a function $f$ is continuous at $a$ if $\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = f(a)$." — [OpenStax Calculus Volume 1, §2.4](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/2-4-continuity)

### Formulation via epsilon-delta

$f$ is continuous at $a$ if and only if

$$\forall\,\varepsilon > 0,\quad \exists\,\delta > 0 : |x - a| < \delta \Rightarrow |f(x) - f(a)| < \varepsilon.$$

Unlike the definition of limit, we now allow $x = a$: $|f(a) - f(a)| = 0 < \varepsilon$ is satisfied trivially.

### Types of discontinuity

<Definicao titulo="Classification of discontinuities">

| Type | Characterization | Canonical example |
|---|---|---|
| **Removable** | $\lim_{x \to a} f(x)$ exists but $\neq f(a)$ (or $f(a)$ is undefined) | $\tfrac{x^2-4}{x-2}$ at $x=2$ |
| **Jump** | One-sided limits exist but differ: $f(a^-) \neq f(a^+)$ | $\lfloor x \rfloor$ at integers |
| **Infinite** | At least one one-sided limit is $\pm\infty$ | $1/x$ at $x=0$ |
| **Oscillatory** | One-sided limits do not exist due to oscillation | $\sin(1/x)$ at $x=0$ |

A removable discontinuity can be eliminated by redefining $f(a)$; the others are structurally irreparable.

</Definicao>

### Continuity on intervals

<Definicao titulo="Continuity on an interval">

- $f$ is **continuous on $(a,b)$** if it is continuous at every point in $(a,b)$.
- $f$ is **continuous on $[a,b]$** if it is continuous on $(a,b)$ and, additionally:
  - $\lim_{x \to a^+} f(x) = f(a)$ (continuous from the right at $a$),
  - $\lim_{x \to b^-} f(x) = f(b)$ (continuous from the left at $b$).

</Definicao>

### Elementary continuous functions

The following types of functions are continuous on their entire natural domain:

- **Polynomials** $p(x) = a_n x^n + \cdots + a_0$: continuous on $\mathbb{R}$.
- **Rational functions** $p(x)/q(x)$: continuous where $q(x) \neq 0$.
- **Trigonometric** $\sin x$, $\cos x$: continuous on $\mathbb{R}$. $\tan x$ continuous on $\mathbb{R} \setminus \{(2k+1)\pi/2 : k \in \mathbb{Z}\}$.
- **Exponential** $e^x$, $a^x$ ($a > 0$): continuous on $\mathbb{R}$.
- **Logarithm** $\ln x$: continuous on $(0,+\infty)$.
- **Composition** of continuous functions is continuous (where defined).

### Algebra of continuous functions

<Teorema titulo="Operations that preserve continuity">

If $f$ and $g$ are continuous at $a$, then $f + g$, $f - g$, $f \cdot g$ are continuous at $a$. Also $f/g$ is continuous at $a$ provided $g(a) \neq 0$, and $f \circ g$ is continuous at $a$ when $g$ is continuous at $a$ and $f$ is continuous at $g(a)$.

</Teorema>

### Intermediate Value Theorem

<Teorema titulo="Intermediate Value Theorem (Bolzano-Cauchy)">

Let $f$ be continuous on the closed interval $[a,b]$ and let $k$ be any value strictly between $f(a)$ and $f(b)$. Then there exists at least one $c \in (a,b)$ such that $f(c) = k$.

$$f \in C([a,b]),\quad \min\{f(a),f(b)\} < k < \max\{f(a),f(b)\} \implies \exists\,c \in (a,b) : f(c) = k$$

**Corollary (existence of root):** If $f(a)$ and $f(b)$ have opposite signs, there exists $c \in (a,b)$ with $f(c) = 0$.

</Teorema>

> "If $f$ is continuous on a closed interval $[a, b]$, then for any number $M$ between $f(a)$ and $f(b)$, there is at least one point $c$ in $[a, b]$ such that $f(c) = M$." — [OpenStax Calculus Volume 1, §2.4](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/2-4-continuity)

### Weierstrass Theorem

<Teorema titulo="Extreme Value Theorem (Weierstrass)">

If $f$ is continuous on the closed and bounded interval $[a,b]$, then $f$ attains its maximum and minimum values on $[a,b]$: there exist $x_{\min}, x_{\max} \in [a,b]$ such that

$$f(x_{\min}) \leq f(x) \leq f(x_{\max}) \quad \forall\, x \in [a,b].$$

</Teorema>

<Insight>

The IVT **fails in $\mathbb{Q}$**: $f(x) = x^2 - 2$ is continuous in $\mathbb{Q}$, $f(0) = -2 < 0$, $f(2) = 2 > 0$, but $\sqrt{2} \notin \mathbb{Q}$, so there is no rational root in $(0,2)$. The completeness of $\mathbb{R}$ is indispensable.

</Insight>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

Imagine you are **drawing with a pencil on paper** and the pencil tip never leaves the paper.

If the function is continuous, you can make the drawing **without lifting the pencil**.

If you need to **lift the pencil** at some point — because there is a hole in the drawing, or the line suddenly jumps to another place — then the function is not continuous there.

That is all: continuous = a single unbroken line, with no interruption.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

A continuous function is one whose graph you can trace **without lifting your pencil from the paper**.

**Three situations where you would need to lift the pencil:**

1. **Hole:** the point exists in the middle of the graph but is missing there.
2. **Jump:** the graph jumps from one value to another without passing through the values in between.
3. **Vertical asymptote:** the graph goes up or down infinitely.

**Examples:**

- $f(x) = x^2$ — parabola with no holes. Continuous everywhere.
- $f(x) = 1/x$ — goes to infinity at $x = 0$. Discontinuous at $x = 0$.
- $f(x) = \lfloor x \rfloor$ (floor function) — at $x = 2$, the graph jumps from 1 to 2. Discontinuous at integers.

**Magic rule:** if the function is continuous, you can simply plug in $x = a$ directly to calculate the limit — $\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = f(a)$. No complications.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old teen">

Continuity is the **property that guarantees you can substitute directly** to compute limits. If $f$ is continuous at $a$, then $\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = f(a)$: plug-and-play.

### Checklist of the three conditions

| Condition | What fails when it does not hold |
|---|---|
| $f(a)$ is defined | Hole or asymptote — the function has no value at that point |
| $\lim_{x \to a} f(x)$ exists | Jump (one-sided limits differ) or infinite oscillation |
| $\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = f(a)$ | The function is defined, but the value was "changed" artificially |

### Four types of discontinuity

- **Removable:** the limit exists, but $f(a)$ is wrong or missing. Fixed by redefining $f(a)$.
  - Example: $f(x) = (x^2 - 4)/(x-2)$, discontinuous at $x = 2$ because $f(2)$ does not exist. But $\lim_{x \to 2} f(x) = 4$. Define $f(2) = 4$ and we are done.
- **Jump:** the one-sided limits exist but differ. Irreparable.
  - Example: $f(x) = \lfloor x \rfloor$ at $x = 1$. $\lim_{x \to 1^-} = 0$, $\lim_{x \to 1^+} = 1$.
- **Infinite:** at least one one-sided limit is $\pm\infty$.
  - Example: $f(x) = 1/x$ at $x = 0$.
- **Oscillatory:** the one-sided limits do not exist due to oscillation.
  - Example: $\sin(1/x)$ at $x = 0$ — oscillates between $-1$ and $1$ infinitely.

### Intermediate Value Theorem

If $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$ and $f(a) \cdot f(b) < 0$ (opposite signs), **there exists at least one zero of $f$ in $(a,b)$**.

This is the basis of the **bisection method**: divide the interval in half, check the sign, discard the half without the root, repeat. Each iteration cuts the interval in half.

**Quick example:** $f(x) = x^3 - x - 1$. $f(1) = -1 < 0$ and $f(2) = 5 > 0$. IVT guarantees a root in $(1, 2)$.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

Continuity is the **minimal regularity** that makes calculus operations well-defined: Riemann integration, IVT, Weierstrass, optimization.

### Proof: sum of continuous functions is continuous

Let $f, g$ be continuous at $a$. Given $\varepsilon > 0$:
- $\exists\,\delta_1 : |x - a| < \delta_1 \Rightarrow |f(x) - f(a)| < \varepsilon/2$
- $\exists\,\delta_2 : |x - a| < \delta_2 \Rightarrow |g(x) - g(a)| < \varepsilon/2$

Take $\delta = \min(\delta_1, \delta_2)$. By the triangle inequality:

$$|(f+g)(x) - (f+g)(a)| \leq |f(x) - f(a)| + |g(x) - g(a)| < \varepsilon. \quad \square$$

### Proof of IVT (sketch)

Define $S = \{x \in [a,b] : f(x) < k\}$. By hypothesis, $a \in S$ and $S$ is bounded above by $b$. Let $c = \sup S$. By continuity of $f$ and the supremum property, $f(c) = k$.

**Subtle point:** the argument uses **completeness of $\mathbb{R}$** — every non-empty bounded subset has a supremum. The IVT fails in $\mathbb{Q}$.

### Lipschitz continuity

$|f(x) - f(y)| \leq L|x - y|$ — a strong form of uniform continuity. Used in:
- **ODEs:** Picard-Lindelöf (existence and uniqueness of solutions).
- **Optimization:** gradient descent with learning rate $1/L$.
- **Neural networks:** controlling the Lipschitz constant regulates adversarial robustness.

### Dirichlet function

<Eq>{`D(x) = \\begin{cases} 1 & x \\in \\mathbb{Q} \\\\ 0 & x \\notin \\mathbb{Q} \\end{cases}`}</Eq>

At any point $a$, every neighborhood of $a$ contains both rationals and irrationals, so the limit does not exist. $D$ is discontinuous at **every** point in $\mathbb{R}$ — an extreme example to calibrate intuition.

### Engineering applications

- **Control:** a transfer function $H(s)$ continuous at real $j\omega$ ensures a Bode diagram without discontinuities.
- **Numerical integration:** uniform continuity on $[a,b]$ (Heine-Cantor) guarantees convergence of Riemann sums.
- **Optimization:** Weierstrass guarantees the existence of a global minimum on a compact set — without it, search algorithms have no guarantee.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

Continuity is the foundation of topology and functional analysis; the pointwise notion of calculus is merely a special case.

### Topological definition

$f : X \to Y$ is continuous if and only if the **preimage of every open set in $Y$ is open in $X$**:

$$\forall\, U \subseteq Y \text{ open},\quad f^{-1}(U) \subseteq X \text{ is open}.$$

In metric spaces, this is equivalent to the $\varepsilon$-$\delta$ definition.

### Sequential characterization (Heine)

$f$ is continuous at $a$ $\iff$ for every sequence $x_n \to a$, we have $f(x_n) \to f(a)$. In general topological spaces, one needs nets or filters.

### Compactness and connectedness preserved

- **Compactness:** $f$ continuous, $K$ compact $\Rightarrow$ $f(K)$ compact. Weierstrass is a corollary: $[a,b]$ compact $\Rightarrow$ $f([a,b])$ closed and bounded $\Rightarrow$ contains max and min.
- **Connectedness:** $f$ continuous, $C$ connected $\Rightarrow$ $f(C)$ connected. IVT is a corollary: $[a,b]$ connected $\Rightarrow$ $f([a,b])$ is an interval.

### Spaces of continuous functions

- $C(K)$ with norm $\|f\|_\infty = \sup_K |f|$ is a Banach space.
- **Stone-Weierstrass:** every $f \in C([a,b])$ can be uniformly approximated by polynomials.
- **Arzelà-Ascoli:** a subset of $C(K)$ is relatively compact iff it is uniformly bounded and equicontinuous.
- Hierarchy of regularity: $C^k$, $C^{\infty}$, $H^k$ (Sobolev) — each space is smaller and more regular than the previous one.

### Linear continuous operators

In Banach spaces, $T: X \to Y$ linear is continuous iff it is **bounded**: $\sup_{\|x\|=1} \|Tx\|_Y < \infty$.

### ML and robotics

- ReLU networks are continuous but not differentiable at zero; subdifferential preserves convex analysis.
- Lipschitz constant controls adversarial instability (WGAN uses gradient penalty).
- Continuous policy in reinforcement learning enables deterministic policy gradient (DDPG, SAC).

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practical application — verification and modeling">

### Continuity verification algorithm

Given a point $a$ and a function $f$, follow this order:

1. **Is $f(a)$ defined?** If not, check whether the limit exists. Limit exists: removable discontinuity. Limit does not exist: jump, infinite, or oscillatory.
2. **Does $\lim_{x \to a} f(x)$ exist?** Compute the one-sided limits. If they differ: jump. If one is $\pm\infty$: infinite. If it oscillates: oscillatory.
3. **Is $\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = f(a)$?** If not: removable (badly defined value).

### Common symptoms

| Expression | Suspected type |
|---|---|
| Fraction with denominator zero at $a$ | Removable (if factor out) or infinite |
| Piecewise function with change at $a$ | Check one-sided limits |
| $\sin(1/x)$, $\cos(1/x)$ at $x = 0$ | Oscillatory |
| $\lfloor x \rfloor$ at an integer | Jump |
| $e^{1/x}$ at $x = 0$ | Infinite ($\to 0$ from left, $\to +\infty$ from right) |

### IVT in practice: bisection to find a root

To show that $f(x) = 0$ has a solution in $(a,b)$:

1. Verify that $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$.
2. Compute $f(a)$ and $f(b)$.
3. If $f(a) \cdot f(b) < 0$, the IVT guarantees a root in $(a,b)$.

**Brazilian example:** electricity bill tariff. The utility projects monthly consumption $C(t)$ as a continuous function of time. If in January $C = 280$ kWh and in December $C = 410$ kWh, the IVT guarantees that some month had consumption of exactly 350 kWh — even without computing it explicitly. The IVT is a guarantee of existence, not uniqueness.

### When discontinuity does not matter

In Lebesgue integration, discontinuities on sets of measure zero do not affect the integral's value. A function with finitely many jump discontinuities on $[a,b]$ is Riemann integrable.

### When it matters greatly

- ODE solution: discontinuity in the forcing term may imply a non-classical solution (in the sense of distributions).
- Numerical algorithms: bisection requires strict continuity to apply the IVT.
- Control: discontinuity in the setpoint generates saturation and oscillation in the actuator.

</Porta>

## Worked examples

<Exemplo titulo="Verification of the three conditions — removable discontinuity" numero="1">

**Problem:** Determine whether $f(x) = \dfrac{x^2 - 9}{x - 3}$ is continuous at $x = 3$.

**Strategy:** Apply the three-condition checklist in order. If any fails, identify the type of discontinuity.

**Solution:**

1. **Is $f(3)$ defined?**
   $$f(3) = \frac{9 - 9}{3 - 3} = \frac{0}{0} \quad \text{(indeterminate)}$$
   $f(3)$ is not defined. Condition 1 fails.

2. **Does $\lim_{x \to 3} f(x)$ exist?**
   Factoring: $x^2 - 9 = (x-3)(x+3)$. For $x \neq 3$:
   $$f(x) = \frac{(x-3)(x+3)}{x-3} = x + 3$$
   $$\lim_{x \to 3} f(x) = \lim_{x \to 3} (x + 3) = 6$$
   The limit exists and equals $6$.

3. **Conclusion:** $f$ is **discontinuous** at $x = 3$, type **removable**. The limit exists ($= 6$), but $f(3)$ is not defined.

**Repair:** Define
<Eq>{`g(x) = \\begin{cases} f(x) & x \\neq 3 \\\\ 6 & x = 3 \\end{cases}`}</Eq>
Then $g$ is continuous at $x = 3$.

**Verification:** $f(2.9) = 5.9 \approx 6$ and $f(3.1) = 6.1 \approx 6$. The graph approaches $6$ from both sides — confirmed.

**Source.** [OpenStax Calculus Volume 1, §2.4](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/2-4-continuity) — license CC-BY-NC-SA 4.0.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Classification of the four discontinuity types" numero="2">

**Problem:** Classify the discontinuity of each function at the indicated point.

**(a)** <Eq>{`f(x) = \\begin{cases} x + 1 & x < 2 \\\\ x^2 & x \\geq 2 \\end{cases}`}</Eq>, at $x = 2$.

**(b)** $g(x) = \dfrac{1}{(x-1)^2}$, at $x = 1$.

**(c)** $h(x) = \sin\!\left(\dfrac{1}{x}\right)$, at $x = 0$.

**Strategy:** Compute one-sided limits in each case and compare with the function value.

**Solution:**

**(a) Jump.**

$\lim_{x \to 2^-} f(x) = 2 + 1 = 3$. $f(2) = 2^2 = 4$ and $\lim_{x \to 2^+} f(x) = 4$.

The one-sided limits differ ($3 \neq 4$). The two-sided limit does not exist. **Jump discontinuity** at $x = 2$, with magnitude $1$.

**(b) Infinite.**
$$\lim_{x \to 1} \frac{1}{(x-1)^2} = +\infty$$
$g(1)$ is not defined and the limit is $+\infty$. **Infinite discontinuity** at $x = 1$.

**(c) Oscillatory.**
When $x \to 0$, $1/x \to \pm\infty$ and $\sin(1/x)$ oscillates between $-1$ and $1$ without stabilizing. **Oscillatory discontinuity** at $x = 0$.

**Verification:** For (a), note that <Eq>{`f(x) = \\begin{cases} x + 1 & x < 2 \\\\ x^2 - 1 & x \\geq 2 \\end{cases}`}</Eq> would be continuous at $x = 2$ (both one-sided limits equal $3$). This illustrates that piecewise functions may or may not be continuous at change points — always verify.

**Source.** [APEX Calculus, §1.5](https://www.apexcalculus.com/) — license CC-BY-NC 4.0.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Determining a constant for continuity on R" numero="3">

**Problem:** Determine the value of $k$ for which

<Eq>{`f(x) = \\begin{cases} 2x^2 + k & x \\leq 1 \\\\ 5x - 1 & x > 1 \\end{cases}`}</Eq>

is continuous on $\mathbb{R}$.

**Strategy:** Polynomials are continuous on all of $\mathbb{R}$. The only possible discontinuity is at $x = 1$. For continuity at $x = 1$, the one-sided limits must match $f(1)$.

**Solution:**

1. **$f(1)$ from the left branch:** $f(1) = 2(1)^2 + k = 2 + k$.

2. **Limit from the right at $x = 1$:**
   $$\lim_{x \to 1^+} f(x) = 5(1) - 1 = 4$$

3. **Continuity condition:**
   $$f(1) = \lim_{x \to 1^+} f(x) \implies 2 + k = 4 \implies k = 2.$$

4. **Verification:** With $k = 2$: $f(1) = 4$, $\lim_{x \to 1^-} f(x) = 2(1) + 2 = 4$, $\lim_{x \to 1^+} f(x) = 4$. All agree. Since both branches are polynomials, $f$ is continuous on $\mathbb{R}$.

**Source.** [OpenStax Calculus Volume 1, §2.4](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/2-4-continuity) — license CC-BY-NC-SA 4.0.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Application of IVT — existence of a root" numero="4">

**Problem:** Prove that the equation $x^3 - x - 1 = 0$ has at least one real root in the interval $(1, 2)$.

**Strategy:** Apply the Intermediate Value Theorem. Define $f(x) = x^3 - x - 1$, verify continuity, evaluate at the endpoints, and conclude from sign change.

**Solution:**

1. **Continuity:** $f(x) = x^3 - x - 1$ is a polynomial, continuous on all of $\mathbb{R}$, in particular on $[1, 2]$.

2. **Evaluation at endpoints:**
   $$f(1) = 1 - 1 - 1 = -1 < 0$$
   $$f(2) = 8 - 2 - 1 = 5 > 0$$

3. **Application of IVT:** $f$ is continuous on $[1,2]$, $f(1) < 0 < f(2)$. The value $0$ lies between $f(1)$ and $f(2)$, so the IVT guarantees the existence of at least one $c \in (1,2)$ with $f(c) = 0$.

4. **Numerical refinement:** $f(1.3) = 2.197 - 1.3 - 1 = -0.103 < 0$ and $f(1.4) = 2.744 - 1.4 - 1 = 0.344 > 0$. The root is approximately $c \approx 1.3247$.

**Source.** [Active Calculus, §1.7](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-1-7-lim-cont-diff.html) — license CC-BY-SA 4.0.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Fixed point via IVT — proof" numero="5">

**Problem:** Let $f$ be continuous on $[0, 1]$ with $0 \leq f(x) \leq 1$ for all $x \in [0,1]$. Prove that $f$ has at least one **fixed point**: there exists $c \in [0,1]$ with $f(c) = c$.

**Strategy:** Define an auxiliary function $g(x) = f(x) - x$ and apply IVT to $g$.

**Solution:**

1. **Define $g(x) = f(x) - x$.**
   $g$ is continuous on $[0,1]$ (difference of continuous functions).

2. **Evaluate at endpoints:**
   - $g(0) = f(0) - 0 = f(0) \geq 0$ (since $f(0) \geq 0$).
   - $g(1) = f(1) - 1 \leq 0$ (since $f(1) \leq 1$).

3. **Apply IVT:**
   - **Case 1:** $g(0) = 0 \Rightarrow c = 0$ is a fixed point. ∎
   - **Case 2:** $g(1) = 0 \Rightarrow c = 1$ is a fixed point. ∎
   - **Case 3:** $g(0) > 0$ and $g(1) < 0$. The IVT guarantees $c \in (0,1)$ with $g(c) = 0$, i.e., $f(c) = c$. ∎

**Interpretation:** If an iterative process $x_{n+1} = f(x_n)$ operates in $[0,1]$ with $f$ continuous and mapping $[0,1]$ into itself, there exists at least one equilibrium state.

**Source.** [Active Calculus, §1.7, Activity 1.7.3](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-1-7-lim-cont-diff.html) — license CC-BY-SA 4.0.

</Exemplo>
