### The five atomic limits

<Definicao titulo="Fundamental limits of calculus">

Consider the following limits, all provable without L'Hôpital's rule:

<Equation numero="FL1" explicacao="Fundamental trigonometric limit. Indeterminate form 0/0. Proved by squeezing with areas of circular sectors." latex="\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin x}{x} = 1" />

<Equation numero="FL2" explicacao="Consequence of FL1 via the identity 1 - cos x = 2 sin²(x/2). The result is zero, not one." latex="\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{1 - \cos x}{x} = 0" />

<Equation numero="FL3" explicacao="Defines Euler's number e. The sequence (1 + 1/n)^n is increasing and bounded above by 3." latex="\lim_{x \to \infty} \left(1 + \frac{1}{x}\right)^x = e \approx 2.71828\ldots" />

<Equation numero="FL4" explicacao="Indeterminate form 0/0. States that the derivative of e^x at x=0 is 1. Derived from FL3 by substitution." latex="\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{e^x - 1}{x} = 1" />

<Equation numero="FL5" explicacao="Indeterminate form 0/0. Derived from FL4 by the substitution y = ln(1+x)." latex="\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\ln(1+x)}{x} = 1" />

</Definicao>

### Proof of FL1 — Squeeze theorem

<Teorema titulo="Squeeze Theorem (Sandwich Theorem)">
If $f(x) \leq g(x) \leq h(x)$ in a neighborhood of $a$ (except possibly at $a$) and $\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = \lim_{x \to a} h(x) = L$, then $\lim_{x \to a} g(x) = L$.
</Teorema>

> "The squeeze theorem (also called the sandwich theorem) is a powerful tool for computing limits of functions that are difficult to evaluate directly." — [OpenStax Calculus Volume 1, §2.3](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/2-3-the-limit-laws)

**Proof that $\lim_{x \to 0^+} \sin x / x = 1$:**

Consider the unit circle. For $x \in (0, \pi/2)$, compare three areas:

- Triangle $OAP$ (inscribed): area $= \tfrac{1}{2}\sin x$.
- Circular sector $OAP$: area $= \tfrac{1}{2}x$.
- Triangle $OAT$ (circumscribed): area $= \tfrac{1}{2}\tan x$.

Since inscribed triangle $\subset$ sector $\subset$ circumscribed triangle:

<Equation explicacao="Inequality of three areas, valid for x in (0, pi/2)." latex="\frac{\sin x}{2} \leq \frac{x}{2} \leq \frac{\tan x}{2}" />

Dividing by $\sin x / 2 > 0$ and taking reciprocals (reverses the inequalities):

$$\cos x \leq \frac{\sin x}{x} \leq 1$$

As $x \to 0^+$: $\cos x \to 1$ and $1 \to 1$. By the squeeze theorem, $\sin x / x \to 1$.

By symmetry ($\sin(-x)/(-x) = \sin x / x$), the result holds for $x \to 0^-$ as well. ∎

### Proof of FL2

Using the identity $1 - \cos x = 2\sin^2(x/2)$:

$$\frac{1 - \cos x}{x} = \frac{2\sin^2(x/2)}{x} = \sin\!\left(\frac{x}{2}\right) \cdot \frac{\sin(x/2)}{x/2}$$

As $x \to 0$: the first factor $\to \sin 0 = 0$ and the second $\to 1$ (by FL1). Thus the product $\to 0$. ∎

### Proof of FL5

Let $y = \ln(1+x)$, so $e^y = 1 + x$, then $x = e^y - 1$. As $x \to 0$, we have $y \to 0$. Therefore:

$$\frac{\ln(1+x)}{x} = \frac{y}{e^y - 1} \xrightarrow{y \to 0} \frac{1}{1} = 1$$

using FL4 in the denominator. ∎

### Table of important variants

| Limit | Value | Derives from |
|---|---|---|
| $\lim_{x \to 0} \sin(kx)/x$ | $k$ | FL1 |
| $\lim_{x \to 0} \sin(kx)/\sin(mx)$ | $k/m$ | FL1 |
| $\lim_{x \to 0} \tan x / x$ | $1$ | FL1 |
| $\lim_{x \to 0} (1 - \cos x)/x^2$ | $1/2$ | FL2 |
| $\lim_{x \to 0} \arcsin x / x$ | $1$ | FL1 (inverse) |
| $\lim_{x \to 0} \arctan x / x$ | $1$ | FL1 (inverse) |
| $\lim_{x \to 0} (e^{kx} - 1)/x$ | $k$ | FL4 |
| $\lim_{x \to 0} (a^x - 1)/x$ | $\ln a$ | FL4 |
| $\lim_{x \to \infty} (1 + a/x)^x$ | $e^a$ | FL3 |
| $\lim_{x \to 0} (1 + x)^{1/x}$ | $e$ | FL3 |
| $\lim_{x \to \infty} x^n e^{-x}$ | $0$ | relative growth |
| $\lim_{x \to \infty} (\ln x)/x$ | $0$ | relative growth |

<Insight>

Whenever you see $\sin(\text{something})/(\text{same something})$, the limit as that something approaches zero is always 1. The key is ensuring that the argument of the sine matches the denominator. If it doesn't match, multiply and divide by the correct argument before applying FL1.

</Insight>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

Imagine you have a giant pizza. You cut a tiny slice. This slice has a curved edge, like a pizza wedge. But if the slice is **very, very tiny**, the curved edge and the straight edge become almost the same — you can barely tell the difference.

That's what the sine of a tiny angle means: when the angle is tiny (in radians), the sine and the angle itself become almost equal. That's why the ratio sine divided by the angle gets very close to 1.

And the special number $e \approx 2.718$? Imagine a snowball rolling downhill — it gets bigger and bigger, and the rate of growth is always proportional to its current size. The number $e$ is what appears when you let this happen in the most natural way possible.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

Think about a circle with radius 1. Take an angle $x$ in radians — for example, $x = 0.1$.

The sine of that angle is $\sin(0.1) = 0.0998...$, almost equal to $0.1$. So $\sin(0.1)/0.1 = 0.998$, very close to 1.

The smaller $x$ is, the closer to 1 that ratio becomes. In the limit as $x \to 0$, it is exactly 1.

The number $e$ appears when you try to calculate interest compounded at every instant. If you deposit \$1 at 100% annual rate with compounding every second, the amount at year's end is $e \approx 2.718$ dollars. More frequent compounding gives more, but there's a ceiling — and that ceiling is $e$.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old student">

The five fundamental limits are the **building blocks** of calculus. Every trigonometric or exponential limit is solved by reducing it to one of these five.

### General strategy for FL1

To compute $\lim_{x \to 0} \sin(5x) / x$:

1. Rewrite: $\dfrac{\sin(5x)}{x} = 5 \cdot \dfrac{\sin(5x)}{5x}$.
2. Let $u = 5x$. As $x \to 0$, $u \to 0$.
3. The factor $\dfrac{\sin u}{u} \to 1$ by FL1.
4. Answer: $5 \cdot 1 = 5$.

### Why it works geometrically

On the unit circle, the arc length for angle $x$ is exactly $x$ (in radians). The vertical leg of the inscribed triangle has length $\sin x$. For small angles, arc and leg are nearly equal — that's why $\sin x \approx x$ and the ratio tends to 1.

### The number $e$ — convergence table

| $n$ | $(1 + 1/n)^n$ |
|---|---|
| 1 | $2.000$ |
| 10 | $2.5937...$ |
| 100 | $2.7048...$ |
| 1000 | $2.7169...$ |
| $\infty$ | $e = 2.71828...$ |

### Caution with $(1 - \cos x)/x$

$\lim_{x \to 0} (1 - \cos x)/x = 0$ — note that it is **zero**, not one. But $\lim_{x \to 0} (1 - \cos x)/x^2 = 1/2$ — here the denominator is $x^2$ and both go to zero at the same rate.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

Fundamental limits are the bridge between geometry, algebra, and analysis. In engineering, they justify linearizations used in control systems, optics, structural dynamics, and signal processing.

### Rigorous proof of FL1 (Cauchy, 1821)

For $x \in (0, \pi/2)$, compare areas on the unit circle:

$$\underbrace{\frac{\sin x}{2}}_{\text{inscribed triangle}} \leq \underbrace{\frac{x}{2}}_{\text{sector}} \leq \underbrace{\frac{\tan x}{2}}_{\text{circumscribed triangle}}$$

Dividing by $\sin x / 2$:

$$1 \leq \frac{x}{\sin x} \leq \frac{1}{\cos x} \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \cos x \leq \frac{\sin x}{x} \leq 1$$

> "The squeeze theorem is precisely what we need to prove that $\lim_{x \to 0} (\sin x)/x = 1$." — [APEX Calculus, §1.3](https://www.apexcalculus.com/)

### FL4 from FL3

Let $u = 1/x$, then $x \to \infty \Leftrightarrow u \to 0^+$ and $(1 + 1/x)^x = (1 + u)^{1/u} \to e$.

Now take $y = e^t - 1$ (so $t = \ln(1+y)$). As $t \to 0$, $y \to 0$:

$$\frac{e^t - 1}{t} = \frac{y}{\ln(1+y)} = \frac{1}{\ln(1+y)/y} \xrightarrow{y \to 0} \frac{1}{1} = 1$$

### First-order approximations (linearization)

For small $|x|$ (sensitivity analysis and linear control):

| Function | 1st-order approximation | 2nd-order error |
|---|---|---|
| $\sin x$ | $x$ | $O(x^3)$ |
| $\cos x$ | $1 - x^2/2$ | $O(x^4)$ |
| $e^x$ | $1 + x$ | $O(x^2)$ |
| $\ln(1+x)$ | $x$ | $O(x^2)$ |
| $(1+x)^a$ | $1 + ax$ | $O(x^2)$ |

These approximations justify the linearization of the pendulum ($\sin\theta \approx \theta$ for $|\theta| \lesssim 10°$), paraxial optics, and perturbation analysis in control.

### Continuous compounding — formal derivation

Nominal rate $r$, principal $V_0$, time $T$, compoundings $n$/year:

$$V(T) = V_0 \left(1 + \frac{r}{n}\right)^{nT} = V_0 \left[\left(1 + \frac{r}{n}\right)^{n/r}\right]^{rT} \xrightarrow{n \to \infty} V_0 e^{rT}$$

The bracketed factor is exactly $(1 + r/n)^{n/r} \to e$ by FL3. This result justifies the continuous compounding formula used in option pricing (Black-Scholes, Lesson 119).

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

Fundamental limits are instances of deep analytical results about elementary functions and power series.

### $\sin x / x$ as an entire function — Weierstrass product

The function $\text{sinc}(x) = \sin(\pi x)/(\pi x)$ is **entire** (analytic on $\mathbb{C}$) with zeros at all nonzero integers. By the Weierstrass factorization theorem:

$$\frac{\sin \pi x}{\pi x} = \prod_{n=1}^{\infty} \left(1 - \frac{x^2}{n^2}\right)$$

Expanding the logarithm and comparing coefficients with the series for $\sin x / x$, we obtain $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} 1/n^2 = \pi^2/6$ — Euler's famous result for $\zeta(2)$.

### $e$ as the unique fixed point of the exponential

$e = \exp(1)$ where $\exp : \mathbb{C} \to \mathbb{C}$ is the unique entire function satisfying $f' = f$ and $f(0) = 1$. Uniqueness follows from the existence and uniqueness theorem for linear ODEs. The matrix exponential $e^A = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} A^k/k!$ solves $\dot{x} = Ax$ and is central in control and stability analysis of linear systems.

### Connection to probability

- **Poisson as limit of binomial**: $\binom{n}{k}(\lambda/n)^k(1-\lambda/n)^{n-k} \to e^{-\lambda}\lambda^k/k!$ uses FL3.
- **Stirling's formula**: $n! \sim \sqrt{2\pi n}(n/e)^n$ — proved via Laplace integral with $e$ explicit.

### Numerical analysis — catastrophic cancellation

For $|x| \ll 1$, direct floating-point evaluation of $(e^x - 1)/x$ suffers catastrophic cancellation: $e^x \approx 1 + x$ and subtracting two nearly equal numbers loses significant digits. Solution: use `expm1(x)/x` (IEEE 754 — available in C99, Python `math.expm1`, MATLAB `expm1`). Similarly, `log1p(x)/x` for $\ln(1+x)/x$.

### Asymptotic expansions

For $x \to \infty$, the growth hierarchy is strict:

$$\ln x \prec x^\alpha \prec x^\beta \prec e^x \quad (\alpha < \beta)$$

FL3 and FL4 are the "atomic steps" that establish these strata. In asymptotic analysis of algorithms, $\ln n \prec n^\epsilon$ for any $\epsilon > 0$ justifies that "logarithm is sublinear".

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practice — solution recipe">

### Diagnosis: which of the 5 limits to use?

When you encounter a limit with indeterminate form $0/0$ or $1^{\infty}$:

1. **Is there sine or cosine?** Use FL1 or FL2. Ensure the sine's argument matches the denominator.
2. **Is there an exponential $e^f - 1$ or $a^f - 1$?** Use FL4.
3. **Is there a logarithm $\ln(1 + f)$?** Use FL5.
4. **Does it have base $(1 + f)$ raised to a power?** Use FL3.
5. **None of these?** Try factoring, canceling, or conjugate before attempting L'Hôpital's rule.

### Strategy for $1^{\infty}$, $0^0$, $\infty^0$

Forms of type $A^B$ with indeterminate value: write $A^B = e^{B \ln A}$, then calculate $\lim B \ln A$ separately (becomes $0 \cdot \infty$, manipulate as a fraction).

### Quick numerical verification

Before computing algebraically, estimate the limit numerically:

- For $\lim_{x \to 0} \sin(5x)/x$: test $x = 0.001$. $\sin(0.005)/0.001 \approx 4.9999...$. Confirms answer $5$.
- For $\lim_{x \to \infty}(1 + 3/x)^x$: test $x = 10^4$. $(1 + 3/10^4)^{10^4} \approx 20.09 \approx e^3$. Confirms.

### Common pitfalls

- $\sin(5x)/x = 5$, not $1$. The sine's argument must appear in the denominator.
- $(1 - \cos x)/x \to 0$ (zero), but $(1 - \cos x)/x^2 \to 1/2$.
- $(1 + a/x)^x \to e^a$, not $e$. The exponent and the coefficient in the denominator must match.
- For $x \to \infty$ in $(1 + 1/x)^x$: never substitute $x = \infty$ — the expression $1 + 1/\infty = 1$ and $1^{\infty}$ is not $1$.

</Porta>

## Worked examples

<Exemplo titulo="Trigonometric limit by squeeze" numero="1">

**Problem:** Compute $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin(3x)}{5x}$.

**Strategy:** The fundamental limit FL1 states that $\sin(u)/u \to 1$ as $u \to 0$. We need to rewrite the expression so the sine's argument matches the denominator.

**Solution:**

1. **Identify the argument:** inside the sine we have $3x$, but the denominator is $5x$. We need to create the ratio $\sin(3x)/(3x)$.

2. **Algebraic manipulation:** multiply and divide by $3$:

$$\frac{\sin(3x)}{5x} = \frac{3}{5} \cdot \frac{\sin(3x)}{3x}$$

3. **Apply FL1:** as $x \to 0$, we have $3x \to 0$. By the substitution $u = 3x$:

$$\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin(3x)}{3x} = \lim_{u \to 0} \frac{\sin u}{u} = 1$$

4. **Combine:**

$$\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin(3x)}{5x} = \frac{3}{5} \cdot 1 = \frac{3}{5}$$

**Verification:** For $x = 0.001$: $\sin(0.003)/0.005 \approx 0.002999.../0.005 \approx 0.5999...$. Consistent with $3/5 = 0.6$.

**Source.** [OpenStax Calculus Volume 1, §2.3, Example 2.21](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/2-3-the-limit-laws) — license CC-BY-NC-SA 4.0.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Limit with (1 - cos x) via trigonometric identity" numero="2">

**Problem:** Compute $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{1 - \cos x}{x^2}$.

**Strategy:** The identity $1 - \cos x = 2\sin^2(x/2)$ transforms the limit into a product of two factors involving FL1.

**Solution:**

1. **Apply the identity:** $1 - \cos x = 2\sin^2(x/2)$.

2. **Rewrite the limit:**

$$\frac{1 - \cos x}{x^2} = \frac{2\sin^2(x/2)}{x^2} = 2 \cdot \frac{\sin^2(x/2)}{x^2}$$

3. **Force the FL1 form:** note that $x^2 = 4 \cdot (x/2)^2$:

$$\frac{\sin^2(x/2)}{x^2} = \frac{\sin^2(x/2)}{4(x/2)^2} = \frac{1}{4} \cdot \left(\frac{\sin(x/2)}{x/2}\right)^2$$

4. **Calculate:**

$$\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{1 - \cos x}{x^2} = 2 \cdot \frac{1}{4} \cdot 1^2 = \frac{1}{2}$$

**Verification:** For $x = 0.1$: $(1 - \cos 0.1)/0.01 \approx 0.04998...$, consistent with $0.5$.

**Source.** [APEX Calculus, §1.3, Example 1.3.4](https://www.apexcalculus.com/) — license CC-BY-NC 4.0.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Definition of e via compound interest" numero="3">

**Problem:** An investment of \$1 yields 100% per year. Calculate the final amount with (a) annual, (b) monthly, (c) daily, and (d) continuous compounding.

**Strategy:** The amount with $n$ compoundings per year is $(1 + 1/n)^n$. As $n \to \infty$, this product converges to $e$.

**Solution:**

| Compounding | $n$ | $(1 + 1/n)^n$ |
|---|---|---|
| Annual | 1 | $2.000000$ |
| Semi-annual | 2 | $2.250000$ |
| Quarterly | 4 | $2.441406$ |
| Monthly | 12 | $2.613035$ |
| Daily | 365 | $2.714567$ |
| Hourly | 8760 | $2.718127$ |
| Continuous | $\infty$ | $e \approx 2.718282$ |

The difference between daily and continuous compounding is just \$0.000001 per dollar invested. This justifies the continuous model $V(T) = V_0 e^{rT}$ as an excellent approximation in financial practice.

**Formalization:** By FL3,

$$\lim_{n \to \infty} \left(1 + \frac{1}{n}\right)^n = e$$

**Source.** [OpenStax Calculus Volume 1, §3.9](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/3-9-derivatives-of-exponential-and-logarithmic-functions) — license CC-BY-NC-SA 4.0.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Reduction of compound limit to fundamental" numero="4">

**Problem:** Compute $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin(x^2)}{x \tan x}$.

**Strategy:** Decompose the expression into ratios of the form FL1. Use $\tan x = \sin x / \cos x$ to separate known factors.

**Solution:**

1. **Substitute $\tan x$:**

$$\frac{\sin(x^2)}{x \tan x} = \frac{\sin(x^2)}{x} \cdot \frac{\cos x}{\sin x} = \frac{\sin(x^2)}{x^2} \cdot x \cdot \frac{\cos x}{\sin x}$$

2. **Identify the factors:**

$$= \underbrace{\frac{\sin(x^2)}{x^2}}_{\to 1} \cdot \underbrace{\frac{x}{\sin x}}_{\to 1} \cdot \underbrace{\cos x}_{\to 1}$$

For the first factor: $u = x^2 \to 0$ as $x \to 0$, so $\sin(x^2)/x^2 \to 1$.

For the second: $x/\sin x = 1/(\sin x / x) \to 1$.

3. **Limit:**

$$\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin(x^2)}{x \tan x} = 1 \cdot 1 \cdot 1 = 1$$

**Verification:** For $x = 0.01$: $\sin(0.0001)/(0.01 \cdot \tan 0.01) \approx 1.000...$. Confirmed.

**Source.** [APEX Calculus, §1.3, Exercises 1.3](https://www.apexcalculus.com/) — license CC-BY-NC 4.0.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Indeterminate form 1^∞ via substitution" numero="5">

**Problem:** Compute $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to \infty} \left(\frac{x+3}{x-1}\right)^x$.

**Strategy:** The base tends to $1$ and the exponent to $\infty$: indeterminate form $1^{\infty}$. Write $A^B = e^{B \ln A}$ and calculate the exponent separately.

**Solution:**

1. **Rewrite as exponential:**

$$\left(\frac{x+3}{x-1}\right)^x = \exp\!\left(x \ln\frac{x+3}{x-1}\right)$$

2. **Simplify the logarithm's argument:**

$$\ln\frac{x+3}{x-1} = \ln\!\left(1 + \frac{4}{x-1}\right)$$

3. **Calculate $x \ln(1 + 4/(x-1))$:** let $u = 4/(x-1)$; as $x \to \infty$, $u \to 0^+$. Then $x - 1 = 4/u$ and $x = 4/u + 1$:

$$x \ln\!\left(1 + \frac{4}{x-1}\right) = \left(\frac{4}{u} + 1\right) \cdot u \cdot \frac{\ln(1+u)}{u} = (4 + u) \cdot \underbrace{\frac{\ln(1+u)}{u}}_{\to 1} \xrightarrow{u \to 0} 4$$

4. **Final limit:**

$$\lim_{x \to \infty} \left(\frac{x+3}{x-1}\right)^x = e^4$$

**Verification:** For $x = 10^4$: $((10^4 + 3)/(10^4 - 1))^{10^4} \approx e^4 \approx 54.598$. Confirmed.

**Source.** [OpenStax Calculus Volume 1, §2.3, Exercises](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/2-3-the-limit-laws) — license CC-BY-NC-SA 4.0.
</Exemplo>
