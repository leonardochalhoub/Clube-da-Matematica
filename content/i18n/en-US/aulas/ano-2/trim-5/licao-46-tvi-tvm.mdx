### Intermediate Value Theorem (IVT)

<Definicao titulo="Intermediate Value Theorem">

Let $f : [a, b] \to \mathbb{R}$ be continuous. If $k$ is any value between $f(a)$ and $f(b)$ — that is, $\min\{f(a), f(b)\} \leq k \leq \max\{f(a), f(b)\}$ — then there exists $c \in (a, b)$ such that $f(c) = k$.

</Definicao>

> "If $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$ and $k$ is any value between $f(a)$ and $f(b)$, then there exists at least one number $c$ in $(a, b)$ such that $f(c) = k$." — [OpenStax Calculus Volume 1, §2.4, Theorem 2.13](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/2-4-continuity)

**Corollary (existence of root).** If $f \in C([a, b])$ and $f(a) \cdot f(b) < 0$, then there exists $c \in (a, b)$ with $f(c) = 0$.

<Equation explicacao="Negative product means opposite signs: f(a) and f(b) are on opposite sides of zero, so f must cross zero at some interior point." latex="f(a) \cdot f(b) < 0 \implies \exists\, c \in (a,b) : f(c) = 0" />

**Proof sketch (via completeness).** Suppose $f(a) < 0 < f(b)$. Define $S = \{x \in [a, b] : f(x) < 0\}$. The set $S$ is nonempty ($a \in S$) and bounded above by $b$. By completeness of $\mathbb{R}$, there exists $c = \sup S \in [a, b]$. By continuity of $f$, if $f(c) \neq 0$ we obtain a contradiction. Hence $f(c) = 0$. $\square$

**Why continuity is indispensable.** The Heaviside function $H(x) = 0$ if $x < 0$ and $H(x) = 1$ if $x \geq 0$ satisfies $H(-1) = 0$ and $H(1) = 1$, but never attains $1/2$ — because it has a jump at $x = 0$ and is not continuous there.

<Insight>

The IVT is sufficient but not necessary for the existence of roots. If $f(a)$ and $f(b)$ have the same sign, the IVT does not apply — but there may still be roots in the interior. Example: $f(x) = (x-1)^2$ has $f(0) = f(2) = 1 > 0$, but $x = 1$ is a double root. The IVT guarantees, it does not exclude.

</Insight>

### Bisection Method

Given $f \in C([a, b])$ with $f(a)f(b) < 0$, bisection locates the root iteratively. At each step, we compute the midpoint and retain the half where $f$ changes sign:

<Equation explicacao="At each iteration, the midpoint m_n subdivides the current interval. The error is cut in half at each step — convergence is guaranteed and quantifiable." latex="m_n = \frac{a_n + b_n}{2}, \qquad |c - m_n| \leq \frac{b - a}{2^{n+1}}" />

For precision $\varepsilon$, we need $n \geq \lceil \log_2((b-a)/\varepsilon) \rceil - 1$ iterations.

### Average Rate of Change (ARC)

<Definicao titulo="Average Rate of Change">

For $f$ defined on $[a, b]$ with $a \neq b$, the **average rate of change** of $f$ over that interval is:

$$\text{ARC}_{[a,b]} = \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}$$

Geometrically, it is the **slope of the secant line** passing through the points $(a, f(a))$ and $(b, f(b))$ on the graph of $f$.

</Definicao>

> "The average rate of change of $f$ along the interval $[a, b]$ is $\text{AV}_{[a,b]} = \dfrac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}$. Geometrically, the average rate of change represents the slope of the line passing through the points $(a, f(a))$ and $(b, f(b))$." — [Active Calculus, §1.1, Definition 1.1.4](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-1-1-vel.html)

The notation using $h = b - a$ is equivalent:

<Equation explicacao="Substituting b = a + h, the ARC becomes expressed in terms of the increment h. When h → 0, this expression defines the derivative — the instantaneous rate of change." latex="\text{ARC} = \frac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h}, \quad h = b - a \neq 0" />

**Taking the limit.** If $f$ is differentiable at $a$:

$$f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h} = \lim_{b \to a} \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}$$

<Teorema titulo="Relationship between ARC and derivative">
If $f$ is differentiable at $a$, then:
$$\lim_{b \to a} \text{ARC}_{[a,b]} = f'(a)$$
The derivative is the limit of the average rate of change as the interval shrinks to a point. The ARC is the global version; the derivative, the local version.
</Teorema>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

Imagine you are climbing a staircase. At the beginning you are **down below** and at the end you are **up top**. To get there, you **must pass through all the steps in between** — there is no way to jump from the ground straight to the top.

The Intermediate Value Theorem says exactly this for continuous functions: if a function starts at a small value and ends at a large value, it **must pass through all the intermediate values**.

And the Average Rate of Change is like calculating the slope of a ramp. If you go up 4 meters in 2 steps, the average slope is 4 divided by 2: 2 meters per step.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

**Intermediate Value Theorem:** imagine a thermometer that reads $-3^\circ$ at midnight and $+12^\circ$ at noon. At some point it read exactly $0^\circ$. This is certain — temperature does not jump, it changes gradually.

Mathematically: if a continuous function starts negative and ends positive, **it crosses zero** at some point in between. This is the IVT.

Example: $f(x) = x^3 - x - 1$.
- $f(1) = 1 - 1 - 1 = -1$ (negative)
- $f(2) = 8 - 2 - 1 = 5$ (positive)

The IVT guarantees that there is a root between 1 and 2.

**Average Rate of Change:** if a car travels 120 km in 2 hours, the average velocity is:

$$\text{average velocity} = \frac{120}{2} = 60 \text{ km/h}$$

This is the ARC of position over time: how much the position changed, divided by the time. If position were a function $s(t)$, the ARC on the interval $[0, 2]$ would be $(s(2) - s(0))/(2 - 0)$.

</Porta>

### IVT — guarantee of the existence of roots

To show that the equation $g(x) = h(x)$ has a solution in $[a, b]$:
1. Define $f(x) = g(x) - h(x)$.
2. Verify that $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$ (polynomials, sine, cosine, exponential are always continuous).
3. Compute $f(a)$ and $f(b)$.
4. If they have opposite signs, the IVT guarantees that there exists $c \in (a, b)$ with $f(c) = 0$.

**Example: $\cos x = x$ on $[0, \pi/2]$.**

Define $f(x) = \cos x - x$.
- $f(0) = \cos 0 - 0 = 1 > 0$
- $f(\pi/2) = \cos(\pi/2) - \pi/2 = 0 - 1.57 \approx -1.57 < 0$

Sign change. Hence there exists $c \in (0, \pi/2)$ with $\cos c = c$ (numerically, $c \approx 0.739$).

### ARC — Average Rate of Change

$$\text{ARC} = \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}$$

**Geometry:** it is the slope of the line passing through the points $(a, f(a))$ and $(b, f(b))$ — the **secant line**.

**Physics:** if $s(t)$ is the position of an object, then the average velocity on the interval $[t_1, t_2]$ is exactly the ARC of $s$.

**Bridge to the derivative:** when the interval $[a, b]$ shrinks ($b \to a$), the secant approaches the **tangent line**. The slope of the tangent is the **derivative** — the instantaneous rate of change. Calculus begins exactly here.

<Insight>

The ARC is what you can compute without differential calculus. The derivative is the limit of the ARC as the interval goes to zero. The entire theory of the derivative is compressed in that one sentence.

</Insight>

### Bisection Method

If $f(a)$ and $f(b)$ have opposite signs, bisection locates the root:

| Iteration | $a$ | $b$ | $m = (a+b)/2$ | $f(m)$ | Next search |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 1 | 2 | $1.5$ | $+0.875$ | $[1;\; 1.5]$ |
| 1 | 1 | $1.5$ | $1.25$ | $-0.297$ | $[1.25;\; 1.5]$ |
| 2 | $1.25$ | $1.5$ | $1.375$ | $+0.225$ | $[1.25;\; 1.375]$ |
| 3 | $1.25$ | $1.375$ | $1.3125$ | $-0.051$ | $[1.3125;\; 1.375]$ |

Each iteration divides the error by 2. After 10 iterations, the error is less than $(b - a)/1024$.

</Porta>

### IVT — consequences and computational applications

**Why IVT fails in $\mathbb{Q}$.** The function $f(x) = x^2 - 2$ is continuous in $\mathbb{Q}$ (as a function restricted to the rationals), with $f(1) = -1 < 0$ and $f(2) = 2 > 0$. The classical IVT would require a root, which would be $\sqrt{2}$ — but $\sqrt{2} \notin \mathbb{Q}$. This demonstrates that the completeness of $\mathbb{R}$ is essential for the IVT.

**Bisection: error analysis.** After $n$ iterations: $|c - m_n| \leq (b - a)/2^{n+1}$. For precision $\varepsilon$: $n \geq \log_2\!\left(\frac{b-a}{\varepsilon}\right) - 1$. For $[1, 2]$ with $\varepsilon = 10^{-9}$: $n \geq \log_2(10^9) - 1 \approx 29$ iterations.

**Engineering applications:**
- Implied volatility in options pricing: $C(\sigma) = C_{\text{mkt}}$, solved by bisection when Newton oscillates.
- Internal Rate of Return (IRR): $\text{NPV}(r) = 0$, bisection over $r \in [0, 1]$.
- Roots of characteristic polynomials for stability analysis of dynamical systems.

**Fixed point via IVT.** If $g \in C([a, b])$ with $g([a, b]) \subseteq [a, b]$, define $f(x) = g(x) - x$. Then $f(a) = g(a) - a \geq 0$ and $f(b) = g(b) - b \leq 0$. By IVT, there exists $c \in [a, b]$ with $g(c) = c$. $\square$

### ARC — quantitative analysis

**Local linearization.** For $b = a + h$ with $h$ small:

$$f(b) \approx f(a) + f'(a) \cdot h$$

This is the first-order linearization (truncated Taylor). The error is $O(h^2)$.

**ARC in finance.** Let $P(t)$ be the price of an asset:

$$\text{absolute average return} = \frac{P(t_2) - P(t_1)}{t_2 - t_1}, \qquad \text{percentage return} = \frac{P(t_2) - P(t_1)}{P(t_1)}$$

The logarithmic derivative $(\ln P)'$ is the continuous instantaneous return — basis of the Black-Scholes model (Lesson 119).

**Velocity and acceleration.**

$$\text{average velocity} = \frac{s(b) - s(a)}{b - a} \xrightarrow{b \to a} s'(a) = \text{instantaneous velocity}$$

$$\text{average acceleration} = \frac{v(b) - v(a)}{b - a} \xrightarrow{b \to a} v'(a) = s''(a)$$

</Porta>

### IVT as a topological property

The IVT is an instance of a general fact: **the image of a connected set under a continuous function is connected**. In $\mathbb{R}$, the connected sets are exactly the intervals. Hence $f([a, b])$ is an interval containing $f(a)$ and $f(b)$, and therefore contains all values between them. The proof via $\sup$ is the analytical version of this topological fact.

**Generalization (Brouwer, 1912).** Every continuous function $g : B^n \to B^n$ (the closed unit ball in $\mathbb{R}^n$) has a fixed point. In dimension 1, this is the fixed-point corollary deduced from the IVT. In higher dimensions, the proof uses homology.

Nash equilibrium in finite games exists via Kakutani (the multivalued generalization of Brouwer). This line connects IVT → fixed point → Nash equilibrium — a result [honored with the Nobel Prize in Economics in 1994](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/1994/summary/).

### ARC — computational and financial perspective

**Finite differences.** In scientific computing, the derivative is always approximated by ARC:

$$f'(a) \approx \frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h} \quad \text{(forward difference, } O(h)\text{)}$$
$$f'(a) \approx \frac{f(a+h) - f(a-h)}{2h} \quad \text{(central difference, } O(h^2)\text{)}$$

The optimal $h$ balances truncation error ($O(h)$ or $O(h^2)$) with rounding error ($O(\varepsilon_{\text{mach}}/h)$): $h^* = O(\sqrt{\varepsilon_{\text{mach}}})$ for forward difference, $h^* = O(\varepsilon_{\text{mach}}^{1/3})$ for central.

**Time series.** In econometrics, $\Delta y_t = y_t - y_{t-1}$ is the difference operator (discrete ARC with $h = 1$). Series that become stationary after one difference are called I(1) — the basis of cointegration and ARIMA models.

**Methods beyond bisection:**
- Bisection: $O(\log 1/\varepsilon)$ — robust, no conditions beyond continuity.
- Newton-Raphson: quadratic convergence, requires $f'(c) \neq 0$ and good initial guess.
- Brent (1973): combines bisection + interpolation; the practical standard.
- ITP (Oliveira-Takahashi, 2020): guarantees $O(\log 1/\varepsilon)$ worst-case with empirical rate close to Newton.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practice — routines and examples with real data">

### Routine: IVT — prove existence of a root

1. **Identify $f$**: if the equation is $g(x) = h(x)$, define $f(x) = g(x) - h(x)$.
2. **Verify continuity**: is $f$ continuous on $[a, b]$? (Polynomials, sine, cosine, $e^x$, $\ln x$ with appropriate domain: yes.)
3. **Compute $f(a)$ and $f(b)$**: are the signs opposite?
4. **Conclude**: "By IVT, there exists $c \in (a, b)$ with $f(c) = 0$, hence $g(c) = h(c)$."

### Routine: ARC — compute the rate of change

1. **Identify $f$, $a$, $b$**: what is changing? Over which interval?
2. **Apply the formula**: $\text{ARC} = \dfrac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}$.
3. **Interpret units**: if $f$ is position (m) and the argument is time (s), ARC has unit m/s.
4. **Compare with derivative**: for small interval, ARC $\approx f'(a)$.

### Practical example: internet bill

Two residential internet plans in São Paulo (illustrative values, 2026):
- Plan A: R\$ 89.90/month (fiber 100 Mbps).
- Plan B: R\$ 129.90/month (fiber 500 Mbps).

Define $C(v) = $ monthly cost for velocity $v$ (in Mbps). The ARC between the two plans is:

$$\text{ARC} = \frac{129.90 - 89.90}{500 - 100} = \frac{40.00}{400} = 0.10 \text{ reais/Mbps}$$

Interpretation: each additional Mbps of velocity costs, on average, R\$ 0.10/month in this range.

### Common pitfalls

- **IVT guarantees existence, not uniqueness.** $f(x) = x^3 - x$ has three roots in $[-2, 2]$.
- **$f(a)f(b) > 0$ does not prove absence of roots.** $f(x) = (x-1)^2$ has a double root at 1, but $f(0) = f(2) = 1 > 0$.
- **ARC depends on the interval.** The same $f$ can have very different ARCs on $[0, 1]$ and on $[0, 10]$.

</Porta>

## Worked examples

<Exemplo titulo="IVT — existence of a polynomial root" numero="1">

**Problem.** Show that $f(x) = x^3 - x - 1$ has at least one root in the interval $(1, 2)$.

**Strategy.** Verify that $f$ is continuous (polynomial) and that $f(1)$ and $f(2)$ have opposite signs. If yes, the IVT guarantees existence.

**Solution.**

1. **Continuity:** $f(x) = x^3 - x - 1$ is a polynomial, hence continuous on $\mathbb{R}$, in particular on $[1, 2]$.

2. **Compute the endpoints:**

$$f(1) = 1^3 - 1 - 1 = -1 < 0$$
$$f(2) = 2^3 - 2 - 1 = 5 > 0$$

3. **Apply IVT:** since $f(1) < 0 < f(2)$ and $f$ is continuous on $[1, 2]$, by the IVT there exists $c \in (1, 2)$ such that $f(c) = 0$.

**Verification.** $f(1.3) = 2.197 - 1.3 - 1 = -0.103 < 0$ and $f(1.4) = 2.744 - 1.4 - 1 = 0.344 > 0$. Root confirmed in $(1.3;\; 1.4)$. Numerically, $c \approx 1.3247$.

**Source.** [Active Calculus §1.1, Example 1.1.2](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-1-1-vel.html) — Boelkins · CC-BY-NC-SA. Adapted.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Bisection — 4 iterations and error analysis" numero="2">

**Problem.** Use the bisection method to approximate the root of $f(x) = x^3 - x - 1$ in $(1, 2)$ with 4 iterations. What is the maximum error after these iterations?

**Strategy.** At each step compute the midpoint $m$, evaluate $f(m)$, and retain the half where the sign changes.

**Solution.**

Initial data: $a_0 = 1$, $b_0 = 2$, $f(1) = -1 < 0$, $f(2) = 5 > 0$.

**Iteration 1:** $m_1 = (1 + 2)/2 = 1.5$. $f(1.5) = 3.375 - 1.5 - 1 = 0.875 > 0$. New interval: $[1;\; 1.5]$.

**Iteration 2:** $m_2 = (1 + 1.5)/2 = 1.25$. $f(1.25) = 1.953 - 1.25 - 1 = -0.297 < 0$. New interval: $[1.25;\; 1.5]$.

**Iteration 3:** $m_3 = (1.25 + 1.5)/2 = 1.375$. $f(1.375) = 2.600 - 1.375 - 1 = 0.225 > 0$. New interval: $[1.25;\; 1.375]$.

**Iteration 4:** $m_4 = (1.25 + 1.375)/2 = 1.3125$. $f(1.3125) = 2.259 - 1.3125 - 1 = -0.053 < 0$. New interval: $[1.3125;\; 1.375]$.

**Maximum error after 4 iterations:**

$$|c - m_4| \leq \frac{b - a}{2^4} = \frac{1}{16} = 0.0625$$

**Verification.** The midpoint of $[1.3125;\; 1.375]$ is $1.34375 \approx 1.34$, with $f(1.34375) \approx -0.0059 < 0$. The true root $c \approx 1.3247$ lies in the interval — confirmed.

**Source.** [REAMAT — Numerical Calculus §3.1, Example 3.1](https://www.ufrgs.br/reamat/CalculoNumerico/livro-py/main.html) — UFRGS · CC-BY 4.0.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="ARC — kinematics and average velocity" numero="3">

**Problem.** The position (in meters) of a particle is $s(t) = t^2 + 3t$ for $t$ in seconds. Compute the ARC of $s$ on the interval $[1, 4]$ and interpret.

**Strategy.** Apply directly $\text{ARC} = (s(4) - s(1))/(4 - 1)$. Then compare with $s'(t) = 2t + 3$ to find the instant with instantaneous velocity equal to the ARC.

**Solution.**

1. **Compute $s(1)$ and $s(4)$:**

$$s(1) = 1 + 3 = 4 \text{ m}, \qquad s(4) = 16 + 12 = 28 \text{ m}$$

2. **Compute the ARC:**

$$\text{ARC} = \frac{28 - 4}{4 - 1} = \frac{24}{3} = 8 \text{ m/s}$$

3. **Interpretation:** the particle displaced 24 m in 3 s, with average velocity 8 m/s.

4. **Connection to derivative:** $s'(t) = 2t + 3$. We want $s'(c) = 8$: $2c + 3 = 8 \Rightarrow c = 2.5 \in (1, 4)$. The Mean Value Theorem of Calculus (Lesson 56) will formally guarantee that such a $c$ exists.

**Verification.** $s'(1) = 5$ m/s, $s'(4) = 11$ m/s. The ARC of 8 m/s is between the instantaneous velocities at the endpoints — correct.

**Source.** [Active Calculus §1.1, Preview Activity 1.1.1](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-1-1-vel.html) — Boelkins · CC-BY-NC-SA.

<Exemplo titulo="Algebraic ARC and taking the limit" numero="4">

**Problem.** For $f(x) = x^2$, compute the ARC on the interval $[2, 2+h]$ ($h \neq 0$) and show what happens when $h \to 0$.

**Strategy.** Compute the ARC as a function of $h$, simplify algebraically, and take the limit — this reveals $f'(2)$.

**Solution.**

1. **ARC on $[2, 2+h]$:**

$$\text{ARC} = \frac{f(2+h) - f(2)}{h} = \frac{(2+h)^2 - 4}{h}$$

2. **Expand $(2+h)^2 = 4 + 4h + h^2$:**

$$\text{ARC} = \frac{4 + 4h + h^2 - 4}{h} = \frac{4h + h^2}{h} = 4 + h \quad (h \neq 0)$$

3. **Take the limit:**

$$\lim_{h \to 0}(4 + h) = 4 = f'(2)$$

**Verification.** For $h = 1$: ARC $= 5$. For $h = 0.1$: ARC $= 4.1$. For $h = 0.01$: ARC $= 4.01$. The secant converges to the tangent at $(2, 4)$ with slope 4.

**Source.** [Active Calculus §1.3, Example 1.3.1](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-1-3-derivative-pt.html) — Boelkins · CC-BY-NC-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="ARC and IVT combined — tabular data" numero="5">

**Problem.** The position (in km) of a vehicle as a function of time (in hours) is:

| $t$ (h) | 0 | 0.5 | 1 | 1.5 | 2 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $s(t)$ (km) | 0 | 20 | 50 | 90 | 140 |

(a) Compute the ARC on each 0.5 h subinterval. (b) Compute the total ARC on $[0, 2]$. (c) Did the instantaneous velocity reach 80 km/h at some point in the journey?

**Strategy.** Apply the ARC formula on each interval; for (c), use the IVT applied to the velocity function.

**Solution.**

(a) **ARCs by subinterval:**

$$\text{ARC}_{[0;\, 0.5]} = \frac{20}{0.5} = 40 \text{ km/h}, \quad \text{ARC}_{[0.5;\, 1]} = \frac{30}{0.5} = 60 \text{ km/h}$$
$$\text{ARC}_{[1;\, 1.5]} = \frac{40}{0.5} = 80 \text{ km/h}, \quad \text{ARC}_{[1.5;\, 2]} = \frac{50}{0.5} = 100 \text{ km/h}$$

(b) **Total ARC:**

$$\text{ARC}_{[0;\, 2]} = \frac{140 - 0}{2} = 70 \text{ km/h}$$

(c) **Velocity of 80 km/h.** Assuming $s$ is differentiable, the velocity $v(t) = s'(t)$ is continuous. By the ARC: $v$ averages 60 km/h on $[0.5;\; 1]$ and 80 km/h on $[1;\; 1.5]$. By the IVT applied to $v$, there is at least one instant in $[0.5;\; 1.5]$ where $v(t) = 80$ km/h. Answer: **yes**.

**Verification.** The average velocity on $[0.5;\; 1.5]$ is $(90 - 20)/1 = 70$ km/h $< 80$ km/h, but the velocity increased from 60 to 100 km/h in the halves — it must have passed through 80 km/h in the interior.

**Source.** [Active Calculus §1.1, Example 1.1.3 and Exercises 1.1.3–1.1.5](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-1-1-vel.html) — Boelkins · CC-BY-NC-SA.

</Exemplo>
