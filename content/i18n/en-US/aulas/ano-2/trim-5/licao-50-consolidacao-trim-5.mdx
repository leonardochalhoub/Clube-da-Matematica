### Central definition

<Definicao titulo="Limit in the epsilon-delta sense (Weierstrass, 1872)">

$\displaystyle\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L$ means:

$$\forall\,\varepsilon > 0,\;\exists\,\delta > 0 : 0 < |x - a| < \delta \Rightarrow |f(x) - L| < \varepsilon.$$

Note: $x \neq a$ is required ($0 < |x - a|$), so the value of $f$ **at** $a$ does not affect the limit.

</Definicao>

> "We say that the limit of $f(x)$, as $x$ approaches $a$, is $L$, and we write $\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L$, if for every number $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists a number $\delta > 0$ such that if $0 < |x - a| < \delta$, then $|f(x) - L| < \varepsilon$." — [OpenStax Calculus Volume 1, §2.5](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/2-5-the-precise-definition-of-a-limit)

### Summary table of main theorems

| Theorem | Hypothesis | Conclusion |
|---|---|---|
| Squeeze (Confrontation) | $g(x) \leq f(x) \leq h(x)$ and $\lim g = \lim h = L$ | $\lim f = L$ |
| IVT | $f \in C([a,b])$, $k$ between $f(a)$ and $f(b)$ | $\exists\,c \in (a,b)$ with $f(c) = k$ |
| Weierstrass | $f \in C([a,b])$ | $f$ attains maximum and minimum |
| Bolzano-Weierstrass | $(a_n)$ bounded in $\mathbb{R}$ | Has convergent subsequence |
| Monotone bounded | $(a_n)$ increasing (decreasing) and bounded above (below) | Converges |
| Cauchy | $(a_n)$ is a Cauchy sequence in $\mathbb{R}$ | $(a_n)$ converges |

| Form | Standard technique |
|---|---|

| $0/0$ with roots | Multiply by the conjugate |

### Immediate engineering applications

- **System stability**: poles in left half-plane $\iff$ $\lim_{t \to \infty} h(t) = 0$.

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

Trimester 5 opens the doors to **real analysis**, **topology**, and **functional analysis**—foundations of ML, optimal control, quantitative finance, and theoretical physics.

### Advanced connections

**Real analysis (Rudin, Lebl)**: the Bolzano-Weierstrass and Heine-Cantor theorems are instances of sequential compactness in $\mathbb{R}$. In general metric spaces, compactness $\iff$ sequentially compact $\iff$ complete and totally bounded.

**General topology**: limits defined via neighborhoods without a metric. Filters and nets (Moore-Smith) generalize sequences for non-metrizable spaces.

**Measure theory and Lebesgue integration**: dominated convergence (DCT), monotone convergence (MCT), and Fatou's lemma are deep generalizations of elementary theorems about swapping limit and integral.

**Complex analysis**: limits of holomorphic functions have extraordinary properties—identity principle, maximum modulus, Cauchy integral representation.

### Emerging structures

- **Weak vs. strong convergence**: in Banach/Hilbert spaces, essential in PDEs and convex optimization.
- **Completion**: from $\mathbb{Q}$ to $\mathbb{R}$ by Cauchy construction; from norms to complete spaces (Sobolev, Hardy, Besov).
- **Compactification**: $\overline{\mathbb{R}}$, Riemann sphere, $\beta\mathbb{N}$—extensions to handle $\pm\infty$ formally.

### In ML and finance

- **SGD convergence**: analyzed via stochastic approximation (Robbins-Monro). Convergence conditions: $\sum \alpha_n = \infty$ and $\sum \alpha_n^2 < \infty$.
- **Black-Scholes**: parabolic PDE with boundary condition expressed as a limit. Convergence of finite-difference scheme via CFL condition.
- **Value at Risk / CVaR**: quantiles as limits of empirical distributions—Glivenko-Cantelli.

### Recommended reading to go further

- **Rudin, Principles of Mathematical Analysis** (baby Rudin): classical real analysis.
- **Royden, Real Analysis**: Lebesgue integration, $L^p$ spaces.
- **Folland, Real Analysis**: modern and concise treatment.

### Brazilian context: continuous compounding

In Brazilian finance, the Central Bank uses daily Selic rate. Continuous compounding is the limit as the period approaches zero:

$$\text{Amount} = P \cdot \lim_{n \to \infty} \left(1 + \frac{r}{n}\right)^{nt} = P \cdot e^{rt}.$$

With Selic at 10.75% p.a. and USD 4,700 (approx. R\$ 23,500) invested for 2 years: Amount $= 4700 \cdot e^{0.1075 \times 2} \approx$ USD 5,780 (approx. R\$ 28,900). The limit defining $e$ appears directly in fixed-income models.

### Types of discontinuity—pocket table

| Type | Condition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Removable | Limit exists, but $\neq f(a)$ or $f(a)$ undefined | $(x^2-1)/(x-1)$ at $x=1$ |
| Jump | One-sided limits exist but differ | $\text{sgn}(x)$ at $x=0$ |
| Infinite | One or both one-sided limits are $\pm\infty$ | $1/x$ at $x=0$ |
| Oscillating | No one-sided limit exists | $\sin(1/x)$ at $x=0$ |

### Asymptotes of rational functions $R(x) = P(x)/Q(x)$

- **Vertical**: where $Q(a) = 0$ and $P(a) \neq 0$.
- **Horizontal**: $\lim_{x \to \pm\infty} R(x)$—compares degrees of $P$ and $Q$.
- **Oblique**: when $\deg P = \deg Q + 1$—polynomial division.

## Worked examples

<Exemplo titulo="Limit 0/0 by factoring" numero="1">

**Problem:** Calculate $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 3} \frac{x^2 - 9}{x - 3}$.

**Strategy:** Direct substitution yields $0/0$. The numerator is a difference of squares and factors, revealing the factor $(x-3)$ that cancels with the denominator.

**Solution:**

1. **Direct substitution:** $\dfrac{9-9}{3-3} = \dfrac{0}{0}$. Indeterminate—we cannot conclude.
2. **Factor:** $x^2 - 9 = (x-3)(x+3)$.
3. **Cancel** (valid since $x \neq 3$ in the limit definition):
$$\lim_{x \to 3} \frac{(x-3)(x+3)}{x-3} = \lim_{x \to 3} (x+3) = 6.$$

**Verification:** For $x = 2.99$: $(2.99 + 3) = 5.99 \approx 6$. For $x = 3.01$: $(3.01 + 3) = 6.01 \approx 6$. Both converge to 6.

**Source.** [OpenStax Calculus Volume 1, §2.3, Example 2.17](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/2-3-the-limit-laws) — CC-BY-NC-SA 4.0 license.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="IVT—existence of a root" numero="2">

**Problem:** Show that $f(x) = x^3 - 2x - 1$ has at least one real root in the interval $(1, 2)$.

**Strategy:** Verify that $f$ is continuous on $[1,2]$ and that $f(1)$ and $f(2)$ have opposite signs. The IVT guarantees existence.

**Solution:**

1. **Continuity:** $f$ is a polynomial, hence continuous everywhere on $\mathbb{R}$, in particular on $[1, 2]$.
2. **Evaluate the endpoints:**
$$f(1) = 1 - 2 - 1 = -2 < 0, \qquad f(2) = 8 - 4 - 1 = 3 > 0.$$
3. **Apply IVT:** Since $f \in C([1,2])$ and $f(1) < 0 < f(2)$, there exists $c \in (1, 2)$ with $f(c) = 0$.

**Verification:** Bisection: $f(1.5) = 3.375 - 3 - 1 = -0.625 < 0$, so $c \in (1.5, 2)$. The real root is $c \approx 1.618$ (the golden ratio, since $\varphi^3 = 2\varphi + 1$).

**Source.** [OpenStax Calculus Volume 1, §2.4, Example 2.38](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/2-4-continuity) — CC-BY-NC-SA 4.0 license.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Vertical, horizontal, and oblique asymptotes" numero="3">

**Problem:** Determine all asymptotes of $f(x) = \dfrac{x^2 + 1}{x - 2}$.

**Strategy:** Check for vertical asymptote (denominator zero), horizontal (degrees), and oblique (division when $\deg P = \deg Q + 1$).

**Solution:**

**Vertical asymptote:** $x = 2$ zeros the denominator and $f(2)$ is undefined. Since $(x^2+1)|_{x=2} = 5 \neq 0$:
$$\lim_{x \to 2^-} f(x) = -\infty, \quad \lim_{x \to 2^+} f(x) = +\infty.$$
So $x = 2$ is a vertical asymptote.

**Horizontal asymptote:** $\deg(x^2+1) = 2 > 1 = \deg(x-2)$, so no horizontal asymptote exists.

**Oblique asymptote:** Euclidean division of $x^2 + 1$ by $x - 2$:
$$\frac{x^2 + 1}{x - 2} = x + 2 + \frac{5}{x - 2}.$$
Since $\lim_{x \to \pm\infty} \dfrac{5}{x-2} = 0$, the line $y = x + 2$ is an oblique asymptote in both directions.

**Verification:** For $x = 100$: $f(100) = 10001/98 \approx 102.05$ and $y(100) = 102$. Difference $\approx 0.05 \to 0$. Correct.

**Source.** [APEX Calculus, §1.6, Example 1.6.2](https://www.apexcalculus.com/) — CC-BY-NC 4.0 license.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Fundamental trigonometric limit with substitution" numero="4">

**Problem:** Calculate $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin(3x)}{5x}$.

**Strategy:** Rewrite to apply $\lim_{u \to 0} \sin u/u = 1$ with substitution $u = 3x$.

**Solution:**

1. **Rewrite:** $\dfrac{\sin(3x)}{5x} = \dfrac{3}{5} \cdot \dfrac{\sin(3x)}{3x}$.
2. **Apply the fundamental limit:** When $x \to 0$, also $3x \to 0$, so $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin(3x)}{3x} = 1$.
3. **Result:** $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin(3x)}{5x} = \frac{3}{5} \cdot 1 = \frac{3}{5}$.

**Verification:** For $x = 0.01$: $\sin(0.03)/0.05 \approx 0.02999/0.05 \approx 0.5998$. Converges to 0.6 = 3/5.

**Source.** [OpenStax Calculus Volume 1, §2.3, Checkpoint 2.20](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/2-3-the-limit-laws) — CC-BY-NC-SA 4.0 license.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Recursive sequence—monotone bounded" numero="5">

**Problem:** Let $a_1 = 1$ and $a_{n+1} = \sqrt{2 + a_n}$. Show that $(a_n)$ converges and find its limit.

**Strategy:** Prove by induction that $(a_n)$ is increasing and bounded above by 2. By the Monotone Bounded Sequence Theorem, $(a_n)$ converges. The limit satisfies a fixed-point equation.

**Solution:**

**Step 1 — Upper bound:** By induction, $a_n \leq 2$ for all $n$.
- Base: $a_1 = 1 \leq 2$.
- Step: if $a_n \leq 2$, then $a_{n+1} = \sqrt{2 + a_n} \leq \sqrt{4} = 2$.

**Step 2 — Increasing:** For $a_n \in [1, 2]$: $a_{n+1}^2 - a_n^2 = 2 + a_n - a_n^2 = -(a_n^2 - a_n - 2) = -(a_n - 2)(a_n + 1) \geq 0$. Since $a_n > 0$, we have $a_{n+1} > a_n$.

**Step 3 — Limit:** Let $L = \lim a_n$. Passing to the limit in $a_{n+1} = \sqrt{2 + a_n}$:
$$L = \sqrt{2 + L} \implies L^2 - L - 2 = 0 \implies (L-2)(L+1) = 0.$$
Since $a_n \geq 1$, we have $L \geq 1$, so $L = 2$.

**Verification:** $a_1 = 1,\ a_2 \approx 1.73,\ a_3 \approx 1.93,\ a_4 \approx 1.98$. The sequence converges rapidly to 2.

**Source.** [Active Calculus, §8.1, Activity 8.1.3](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-8-3-geometric.html) — CC-BY-SA 4.0 license.

</Exemplo>
