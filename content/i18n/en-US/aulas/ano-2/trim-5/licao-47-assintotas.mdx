<Porta nivel="formal" titulo="Definitions and theorems">

### Vertical asymptote

<Definicao titulo="Vertical asymptote (VA)">

The line $x = a$ is a **vertical asymptote** of $f$ if at least one of the one-sided limits is infinite:

$$\lim_{x \to a^+} f(x) = \pm\infty \quad \text{or} \quad \lim_{x \to a^-} f(x) = \pm\infty.$$

For a rational $f = P/Q$ with $P(a) \neq 0$ and $Q(a) = 0$, the line $x = a$ is a VA.

**Caution**: if $P(a) = 0$ and $Q(a) = 0$, the point may be **removable** — simplify first before concluding.

</Definicao>

> "We say the function has a vertical asymptote at $x = a$ if $\lim_{x \to a^-} f(x) = \pm\infty$ or $\lim_{x \to a^+} f(x) = \pm\infty$." — [OpenStax Calculus Volume 1, §2.2](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/2-2-the-limit-of-a-function)

### Horizontal asymptote

<Definicao titulo="Horizontal asymptote (HA)">

The line $y = L$ is a **horizontal asymptote** of $f$ if

$$\lim_{x \to +\infty} f(x) = L \quad \text{or} \quad \lim_{x \to -\infty} f(x) = L.$$

A function may have **two** distinct HAs — one at $+\infty$ and another at $-\infty$.

</Definicao>

> "A function $f$ has a horizontal asymptote of $y = L$ if $\lim_{x \to \infty} f(x) = L$ or $\lim_{x \to -\infty} f(x) = L$." — [OpenStax Calculus Volume 1, §4.6](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/4-6-limits-at-infinity-and-asymptotes)

**Rule for rational functions** $f = P/Q$ with $\deg P = m$, $\deg Q = n$:

| Case | HA | OA |
|---|---|---|
| $m < n$ | $y = 0$ | No |
| $m = n$ | $y = a_m/b_n$ (ratio of leading coefficients) | No |
| $m = n + 1$ | No | $y =$ quotient from long division |
| $m > n + 1$ | No | No (superlinear growth) |

### Oblique asymptote

<Definicao titulo="Oblique asymptote (OA)">

The line $y = mx + b$ with $m \neq 0$ is an **oblique asymptote** of $f$ if

$$\lim_{x \to \pm\infty} \bigl[f(x) - (mx + b)\bigr] = 0.$$

</Definicao>

<Equation
  explicacao="Compute m first; then b. If m = 0, the limit would be an HA, not an OA."
  latex="m = \lim_{x \to \pm\infty} \frac{f(x)}{x}, \qquad b = \lim_{x \to \pm\infty} \bigl(f(x) - mx\bigr)"
/>

> "If the degree of the numerator is one more than the degree of the denominator, the rational function has an oblique asymptote found by polynomial long division." — [APEX Calculus, §3.5](https://www.apexcalculus.com/)

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

Imagine a road that gets closer and closer to a **wall**, but never manages to touch it. The wall is the **asymptote** — the curve gets very close but does not touch.

There are three types of wall:

- **Vertical wall** (standing up): when you get near a certain point, the road goes up or down forever without stopping.
- **Horizontal wall** (lying flat): when you go very far, the road becomes almost parallel to the horizon — almost straight and flat.
- **Slanted wall**: when you go very far, the road becomes almost parallel to a tilted line — neither standing nor lying flat, but in between.

The secret: the road can get very, very close to the wall, but **never touches** it. In mathematics this is called a "limit" — approaching without reaching.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

Think about the graph of $f(x) = 1/x$: the closer $x$ gets to zero, the more the function explodes (goes to infinity). But it never touches $x = 0$. That line $x = 0$ is the **vertical asymptote**.

Now, if $x$ gets very large, $1/x$ gets smaller and smaller — almost zero, but never exactly zero. The line $y = 0$ is the **horizontal asymptote**.

Other examples:

- $f(x) = (x+1)/(x-1)$: VA at $x = 1$ (denominator is zero there), HA at $y = 1$ (for huge $x$, numerator and denominator become nearly equal, so the division is close to 1).
- $f(x) = (x^2 + 1)/x$: divide to get $x + 1/x$. The part $1/x$ goes to zero, so the curve approaches the line $y = x$ — an **oblique asymptote** (slanted).

Asymptotes help us sketch graphs confidently, without a calculator.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old teen">

Asymptotic analysis is the **standard tool** for sketching graphs of rational functions. Follow the roadmap:

### Analysis roadmap

1. **Domain**: where is $f$ defined?
2. **VAs**: zeros of the denominator where the numerator is nonzero. Compute $\lim_{x \to a^\pm} f$.
3. **HA**: compute $\lim_{x \to +\infty} f$ and $\lim_{x \to -\infty} f$. If finite, they are HAs.
4. **OA**: if there is no HA and $\deg(\text{num}) = \deg(\text{den}) + 1$, do polynomial long division.
5. **Zeros and sign**: complete the sketch with zeros of the function and sign in each interval.

### Complete example: $f(x) = (x^2 + 1)/(x - 2)$

**Domain**: $\mathbb{R} \setminus \{2\}$.

**VA at $x = 2$**: denominator zero, numerator $= 5 \neq 0$. Confirmed.

**HA?**: degree of numerator $= 2 >$ degree of denominator $= 1$. No HA.

**OA**: polynomial long division of $x^2 + 1$ by $x - 2$:

$$x^2 + 1 = (x-2)(x+2) + 5, \quad \text{so } f(x) = x + 2 + \frac{5}{x-2}.$$

When $x \to \pm\infty$, the remainder $5/(x-2) \to 0$. OA: $y = x + 2$.

### Caution: removable zero vs. pole

$g(x) = \dfrac{x^2 - 4}{x - 2} = \dfrac{(x+2)(x-2)}{x-2} = x + 2$ for $x \neq 2$. **No VA at $x = 2$** — the point is removable: the factor cancels. Graph of $g$: line $y = x+2$ with a **hole** at $(2, 4)$.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

### Formal definition via limit

**VA** at $x = a$: $\limsup_{x \to a} |f(x)| = +\infty$ (equivalent to the one-sided limit definitions when $f$ is rational).

**HA** $y = L$: $\forall \varepsilon > 0$, $\exists M > 0$ such that $|x| > M \Rightarrow |f(x) - L| < \varepsilon$.

**OA** $y = mx + b$: $f(x) - (mx + b) = o(1)$ as $x \to \pm\infty$ (Landau little-$o$ notation).

### Singularities: pole vs. removable zero

For rational $f = P/Q$, at $x = a$ with $Q(a) = 0$:

- **Simple pole** (VA): $(x-a)$ divides $Q$ but not $P$. Behavior $f(x) \sim C/(x-a)$ near $a$.
- **Pole of order $k$**: $(x-a)^k$ divides $Q$ but $(x-a)^{k-1}$ does not divide $P$. Behavior $\sim C/(x-a)^k$.
- **Removable zero**: $(x-a)^k$ divides $P$ with $k \geq$ multiplicity in $Q$. Finite limit — no VA.

### Applications in engineering

**Bode plots**: $H(j\omega) = K/(1 + j\omega/\omega_0)$. On a log-log scale:

- $\omega \ll \omega_0$: $|H| \approx K$ — low-frequency HA.
- $\omega \gg \omega_0$: $|H| \approx K\omega_0/\omega$ — roll-off of $-20$ dB/decade (OA on log scale).

**Transfer functions**: poles of $H(s)$ (zeros of the denominator) indicate natural frequencies. Poles in the right half-plane $\Rightarrow$ instability.

**Terminal velocity**: $v(t) = v_t(1 - e^{-t/\tau})$. $\lim_{t \to \infty} v(t) = v_t$ — physical HA.

**RC circuit**: $V_C(t) = V_\infty(1 - e^{-t/(RC)})$. HA $y = V_\infty$.

### Non-rational functions

- $\sqrt{x^2 + 1}$: divide by $|x|$ to get OA $y = x$ as $x \to +\infty$ and $y = -x$ as $x \to -\infty$.
- $f$ **can cross** an HA or OA (unlike a VA): e.g. $(\sin x)/x \to 0$ but oscillates infinitely often around $y = 0$.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

### Formal asymptotic analysis and Landau notation

**Hierarchy of scales** ($x \to \infty$): $1 \ll \ln x \ll x^\alpha \ll e^x \ll e^{e^x}$.

**Landau notation**:

- $f = O(g)$: $\limsup |f/g| < \infty$ — $f$ does not grow faster than $g$.
- $f = o(g)$: $\lim f/g = 0$ — $f$ grows strictly slower than $g$.
- $f \sim g$: $\lim f/g = 1$ (asymptotic equivalence).

The high-school asymptotes are the case $f(x) - (mx + b) = o(1)$.

### Asymptotic expansion (Laurent series in $1/x$)

Generalization: $f(x) \sim a_0 + a_1/x + a_2/x^2 + \cdots$ as $x \to \infty$. The series may be divergent but **asymptotic** — truncated at $N$ terms, the error is $O(x^{-(N+1)})$.

**Example**: $e^{-x}\int_x^{\infty} e^t/t\, dt \sim 1 - 1/x + 2!/x^2 - 3!/x^3 + \cdots$ — divergent series but asymptotic.

### Laplace's method (saddle-point integrals)

For integrals $\int e^{N f(t)} g(t)\,dt$ with $N \to \infty$:

$$\int e^{Nf(t)} g(t)\,dt \sim g(t_0)\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{N|f''(t_0)|}} e^{Nf(t_0)}$$

where $t_0$ is the maximum of $f$. Fundamental in statistical mechanics and asymptotic combinatorics.

### Padé approximants

Given that a Taylor series converges poorly (or diverges), approximate by $[m/n](x) = P_m(x)/Q_n(x)$ by choosing coefficients so that the Taylor series of the quotient matches $f$ up to order $m + n$. The poles of $[m/n]$ approximate singularities of $f$ — the rational version of oblique asymptote captures behavior at $\pm\infty$ much better than polynomial truncation.

### Scaling laws in language models (2022)

Chinchilla (Hoffmann 2022): for LLMs, $L(N, D) \sim a \cdot N^{-\alpha} + b \cdot D^{-\beta} + L_\infty$, where $L_\infty$ is the irreducible entropy of the data. The **horizontal asymptote** $L_\infty$ is the real quality bottleneck — not the model size $N$.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practical application — pharmacokinetics and economics">

### Drug concentration model (absorption)

In first-order absorption pharmacokinetics, the concentration of a drug in plasma is:

$$C(t) = \frac{A \cdot t}{t + k}, \quad t \geq 0, \quad A, k > 0.$$

- **HA**: $\lim_{t \to \infty} C(t) = A$ — concentration saturates at $A$ (therapeutic plateau).
- **VA**: the denominator $t + k \neq 0$ for $t \geq 0$, so no VA in the physical domain.
- **Clinical interpretation**: repeated doses seek to keep $C(t)$ near $A$; above is toxic, below is ineffective.

### Average cost of production

Total cost: $C(q) = F + cq$ (fixed $F$, variable per unit $c$). Average cost:

$$\bar{C}(q) = \frac{F}{q} + c.$$

- **VA at $q = 0$**: average cost explodes with zero volume.
- **HA as $q \to \infty$**: $\bar{C}(q) \to c$ — the fixed cost per unit goes to zero; only the variable cost $c$ remains.

**Managerial insight**: at industrial scale, average cost converges to marginal cost $c$. Growing volume dilutes the fixed cost — this is why companies pursue scale.

### Terminal velocity (falling with drag)

Object falling with air resistance proportional to velocity:

$$v(t) = v_t \!\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\right), \quad v_t = \frac{mg}{k}.$$

- $\lim_{t \to \infty} v(t) = v_t$: HA is the **terminal velocity**.
- $v_t$ increases with mass $m$ and decreases with drag coefficient $k$ — why dense, compact objects fall faster through air.

### Operational algorithm

```
1. Find zeros of the denominator → candidates for VA.
2. For each candidate a: compute lim x→a⁺ and lim x→a⁻.
   - Infinite: VA confirmed.
   - Finite: removable zero, no VA.
3. Compute lim x→+∞ f and lim x→-∞ f.
   - Finite: HA.
   - Infinite: try OA.
4. For OA: deg(num) = deg(den) + 1 → long division.
   Or: m = lim f(x)/x, b = lim (f(x) − mx).
```

</Porta>

## Worked examples

<Exemplo titulo="Vertical asymptotes of a rational function" numero="1">

**Problem.** Find all vertical asymptotes of $f(x) = \dfrac{2x}{x^2 - 9}$.

**Strategy.** Factor the denominator, check if the numerator vanishes at the same points, and compute one-sided limits.

**Solution.**

Step 1 — Zeros of the denominator: $x^2 - 9 = (x-3)(x+3) = 0 \Rightarrow x = 3$ or $x = -3$.

Step 2 — Numerator: $2(3) = 6 \neq 0$ and $2(-3) = -6 \neq 0$. No cancellation.

Step 3 — One-sided limits at $x = 3$: near $x = 3$, numerator $\approx 6$, denominator $\approx (x-3) \cdot 6$.

$$\lim_{x \to 3^+} f(x) = +\infty, \qquad \lim_{x \to 3^-} f(x) = -\infty.$$

Step 4 — One-sided limits at $x = -3$: near $x = -3$, numerator $\approx -6$, denominator $\approx (x+3) \cdot (-6)$.

$$\lim_{x \to -3^+} f(x) = +\infty, \qquad \lim_{x \to -3^-} f(x) = -\infty.$$

**Conclusion.** Two simple poles: VA at $x = 3$ and VA at $x = -3$.

**Verification.** Degree of numerator $= 1 <$ degree of denominator $= 2$: so $f \to 0$ at $\pm\infty$ (HA $y = 0$). Consistent with two simple poles and no OA.

**Source.** [OpenStax — Calculus Volume 1, §2.2, Example 2.12](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/2-2-the-limit-of-a-function) — CC-BY-NC-SA 4.0.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Horizontal asymptote — rule of degrees and cases" numero="2">

**Problem.** For each function, determine the horizontal asymptote (if it exists):

(a) $f(x) = \dfrac{3x^2 - 5}{2x^2 + 7x - 1}$, $\quad$ (b) $g(x) = \dfrac{4x - 1}{x^2 + 3}$, $\quad$ (c) $h(x) = \dfrac{x^3}{x^2 + 1}$.

**Strategy.** Divide numerator and denominator by the highest power of the denominator; identify the case by the pattern of degrees.

**Solution.**

**(a) Equal degrees** ($m = n = 2$): divide by $x^2$:

$$f(x) = \frac{3 - 5/x^2}{2 + 7/x - 1/x^2} \xrightarrow{x \to \pm\infty} \frac{3}{2}.$$

HA: $y = 3/2$.

**(b) Numerator degree smaller** ($m = 1 < n = 2$): divide by $x^2$:

$$g(x) = \frac{4/x - 1/x^2}{1 + 3/x^2} \xrightarrow{x \to \pm\infty} 0.$$

HA: $y = 0$.

**(c) Numerator degree larger** ($m = 3 > n = 2$): $h(x)/x = x^2/(x^2+1) \to 1$. There is an OA $y = x$, but no HA: $\lim_{x \to \pm\infty} h(x) = \pm\infty$.

**Verification.** In all three cases, the rule of degrees correctly predicted: $m < n \Rightarrow y=0$; $m = n \Rightarrow y = a_m/b_n$; $m > n \Rightarrow$ no HA.

**Source.** [OpenStax — Calculus Volume 1, §4.6, Examples 4.22–4.24](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/4-6-limits-at-infinity-and-asymptotes) — CC-BY-NC-SA 4.0.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Oblique asymptote via long division" numero="3">

**Problem.** Find the oblique asymptote of $f(x) = \dfrac{x^2 + 3x + 1}{x - 2}$.

**Strategy.** $\deg(\text{num}) = 2 = \deg(\text{den}) + 1$. Apply long division; the linear quotient is the OA.

**Solution.**

Polynomial long division of $x^2 + 3x + 1$ by $x - 2$:

- $x^2 \div x = x$. Subtract $x(x-2) = x^2 - 2x$. Remainder: $5x + 1$.
- $5x \div x = 5$. Subtract $5(x-2) = 5x - 10$. Remainder: $11$.

$$f(x) = x + 5 + \frac{11}{x - 2}.$$

As $x \to \pm\infty$: the remainder $11/(x-2) \to 0$. **OA: $y = x + 5$.**

**VA.** $x = 2$ (denominator zero; numerator $= 4 + 6 + 1 = 11 \neq 0$). Confirmed.

**Verification.** At $x = 0$: $f(0) = 1/(-2) = -0.5$. The OA at $x = 0$ gives $y = 5$. Difference: $f(0) - 5 = -5.5 = 11/(0-2)$. Correct.

**Source.** [OpenStax — Calculus Volume 1, §4.6, Example 4.28](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/4-6-limits-at-infinity-and-asymptotes) — CC-BY-NC-SA 4.0.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Function that crosses the horizontal asymptote" numero="4">

**Problem.** Let $f(x) = \dfrac{x \sin x}{x^2 + 1}$. Show that $y = 0$ is an HA but $f$ crosses this line infinitely many times.

**Strategy.** Compute the limit at infinity (squeeze); find the zeros of $f$.

**Solution.**

**Limit.** $|f(x)| \leq |x|/(x^2+1) \leq 1/(2\sqrt{x^2+1}) \to 0$. So $\lim_{x \to \pm\infty} f(x) = 0$. HA: $y = 0$.

**Crossings.** $f(x) = 0 \iff x \sin x = 0 \iff x = 0$ or $\sin x = 0$, i.e., $x = k\pi$ for $k \in \mathbb{Z}$.

There are infinitely many zeros at $x = k\pi$ — the curve crosses $y = 0$ infinitely many times, even while converging to it.

**Moral.** A function can cross its HA (and OA). Only the VA cannot be crossed — it lies outside the function's domain.

**Source.** [APEX Calculus, §1.4, Example 1.4.6](https://www.apexcalculus.com/) — CC-BY-NC 4.0.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Pharmacological concentration model — HA as equilibrium dose" numero="5">

**Problem.** The concentration (in mg/L) of a drug in plasma follows

$$C(t) = \frac{120t}{t + 4}, \quad t \geq 0 \text{ (hours)}.$$

(a) What is the maximum asymptotic concentration?
(b) At what time does the concentration reach 90% of the asymptotic value?
(c) Does the function have a VA for $t \geq 0$? Justify.

**Strategy.** Compute the HA; solve an equation for 90%; check the denominator on the physical domain.

**Solution.**

**(a) HA.** Divide by $t$: $C(t) = 120/(1 + 4/t) \to 120$ as $t \to \infty$. HA: $C = 120$ mg/L.

**(b) $90\% \times 120 = 108$ mg/L.**

$$\frac{120t}{t + 4} = 108 \iff 120t = 108(t + 4) = 108t + 432 \iff 12t = 432 \iff t = 36 \text{ h}.$$

**(c) VA.** The denominator $t + 4 = 0 \iff t = -4$. Since the physical domain is $t \geq 0$, there is no VA in the region of application.

**Interpretation.** The therapeutic plateau is $120$ mg/L. Reaching $90\%$ of it requires 36 hours — slow approach, typical of drugs with long half-lives.

**Source.** [OpenStax — Calculus Volume 1, §4.6, Example 4.31](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/4-6-limits-at-infinity-and-asymptotes) — CC-BY-NC-SA 4.0.

</Exemplo>
