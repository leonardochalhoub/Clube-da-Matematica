<Porta nivel="formal" titulo="Definitions and Axioms">

### Sample space and events

<Definicao titulo="Sample space and event">

A **random experiment** is any procedure whose outcome is not determined with certainty before it is performed.

- **Sample space** <Eq>{`\\Omega`}</Eq>: the set of all possible outcomes.
- **Event** <Eq>{`A`}</Eq>: any subset <Eq>{`A \\subseteq \\Omega`}</Eq>.
- **Elementary event**: a singleton subset <Eq>{`\\{\\omega\\}`}</Eq>, where <Eq>{`\\omega \\in \\Omega`}</Eq>.
- **Certain event**: <Eq>{`\\Omega`}</Eq> (always occurs). **Impossible event**: <Eq>{`\\emptyset`}</Eq>.

</Definicao>

### Kolmogorov's Axioms (1933)

<Definicao titulo="Kolmogorov's Axioms">

A **probability function** is a mapping <Eq>{`P: \\mathcal{P}(\\Omega) \\to \\mathbb{R}`}</Eq> satisfying:

1. **Non-negativity**: <Eq>{`P(A) \\geq 0`}</Eq> for every event <Eq>{`A`}</Eq>.
2. **Normalization**: <Eq>{`P(\\Omega) = 1`}</Eq>.
3. **Additivity** (for disjoint events <Eq>{`A \\cap B = \\emptyset`}</Eq>): <Eq>{`P(A \\cup B) = P(A) + P(B)`}</Eq>.

> "The probability of an event <Eq>{`A`}</Eq> is a number between 0 and 1 that indicates the proportion of times we expect <Eq>{`A`}</Eq> to occur when the experiment is repeated many times." — [OpenIntro Statistics, 4th ed., §3.1](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/)

</Definicao>

### Classical probability

<Definicao titulo="Classical probability (Laplace)">

When <Eq>{`\\Omega`}</Eq> is finite and all elementary outcomes are **equally likely**:

<Equation numero="P-classica" explicacao="Number of favorable outcomes for event A divided by the total number of possible outcomes." latex="P(A) = \frac{|A|}{|\Omega|}" />

</Definicao>

### Properties derived from the axioms

<Definicao titulo="Immediate consequences of the axioms">

- <Eq>{`P(\\emptyset) = 0`}</Eq>.
- <Eq>{`P(A^c) = 1 - P(A)`}</Eq> — **complement**.
- <Eq>{`P(A \\cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \\cap B)`}</Eq> — **inclusion–exclusion**.
- <Eq>{`A \\subseteq B \\Rightarrow P(A) \\leq P(B)`}</Eq> — **monotonicity**.
- <Eq>{`0 \\leq P(A) \\leq 1`}</Eq>.

</Definicao>

### Conditional probability

<Definicao titulo="Conditional probability">

Given that event <Eq>{`B`}</Eq> has occurred (<Eq>{`P(B) > 0`}</Eq>), the probability of <Eq>{`A`}</Eq> **given** <Eq>{`B`}</Eq> is:

<Equation numero="P-cond" explicacao="Restricts the sample space to event B. Only results compatible with B are considered." latex="P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}" />

</Definicao>

### Independence

<Definicao titulo="Independent events">

<Eq>{`A`}</Eq> and <Eq>{`B`}</Eq> are **independent** if and only if <Eq>{`P(A \\cap B) = P(A) \\cdot P(B)`}</Eq>.

Equivalently: <Eq>{`P(A \\mid B) = P(A)`}</Eq> — knowing that <Eq>{`B`}</Eq> occurred does not change the probability of <Eq>{`A`}</Eq>.

**Caution:** Independence <Eq>{`\\neq`}</Eq> mutually exclusive. If <Eq>{`A \\cap B = \\emptyset`}</Eq> and <Eq>{`P(A), P(B) > 0`}</Eq>, then <Eq>{`A`}</Eq> and <Eq>{`B`}</Eq> are mutually exclusive but **dependent** (knowing that <Eq>{`A`}</Eq> occurred guarantees that <Eq>{`B`}</Eq> did not).

</Definicao>

### Bayes' Theorem

<Teorema titulo="Bayes (simple form)">

For events <Eq>{`A`}</Eq> and <Eq>{`B`}</Eq> with <Eq>{`P(B) > 0`}</Eq>:

<Equation numero="Bayes" explicacao="Inverts the conditional: if we know P(B|A), we calculate P(A|B). Fundamental in diagnosis, ML, and inference." latex="P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(B \mid A) \cdot P(A)}{P(B)}" />

The denominator <Eq>{`P(B)`}</Eq> is calculated by the **law of total probability**:

<Eq>{`P(B) = P(B \\mid A) \\cdot P(A) + P(B \\mid A^c) \\cdot P(A^c)`}</Eq>

</Teorema>

> "Bayes' theorem is a tool for updating beliefs in light of new evidence. The prior <Eq>{`P(A)`}</Eq> is updated to the posterior <Eq>{`P(A \\mid B)`}</Eq> when we observe <Eq>{`B`}</Eq>." — [Grinstead-Snell, Introduction to Probability, Chapter 4](https://chance.dartmouth.edu/teaching_aids/books_articles/probability_book/book.html)

<Insight>

**Base rate fallacy:** Even a test with 90% sensitivity can have very low positive predictive value when disease prevalence is 1%. The denominator (total probability of a positive) is dominated by false positives in the healthy population. Bayes quantifies exactly this.

</Insight>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

Probability is the **chance** that something will happen.

Think of a die with 6 sides: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6. Each side has an equal chance. If I want to roll a 3, I have **1 chance in 6**.

The chance ranges from **0** (impossible, like rolling a 7 on a 6-sided die) to **1** (certain, like rolling a number between 1 and 6).

Flipping a coin: heads or tails. Each has a chance of **1 in 2**, or half.

The more possible outcomes, the lower the chance of getting a specific one.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

**Probability** measures the chance of an outcome occurring, and ranges from 0 to 1.

**Classical rule** (when all outcomes are equally likely):

<Eq>{`P(\\text{event}) = \\frac{\\text{favorable cases}}{\\text{total cases}}`}</Eq>

Direct examples:
- Die: <Eq>{`P(\\text{even}) = 3/6 = 1/2`}</Eq> (the evens are 2, 4, 6 — 3 outcomes out of 6).
- Two dice: <Eq>{`P(\\text{sum} = 7) = 6/36 = 1/6`}</Eq> (pairs summing to 7: (1,6), (2,5), (3,4), (4,3), (5,2), (6,1)).
- Standard deck: <Eq>{`P(\\text{ace}) = 4/52 = 1/13`}</Eq> (4 aces in 52 cards).

**Complement**: if the chance of rain is 0.3, the chance of no rain is 1 − 0.3 = 0.7.

**Conditional probability**: "given that I already know something, what is the new chance?" Example: given that the first die shows 4, what is the chance the sum is 7? Only the second die matters now — it needs to be 3. Chance: 1/6.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old teen">

Probability measures chance — a number between 0 and 1 — following three axioms (Kolmogorov, 1933).

**Sample space** <Eq>{`\\Omega`}</Eq>: all possible outcomes. Example: two dice, <Eq>{`|\\Omega| = 36`}</Eq>.

**Classical probability** (equiprobable space): <Eq>{`P(A) = |A|/|\\Omega|`}</Eq>.

**Rules derived from the axioms:**

- **Complement**: <Eq>{`P(A^c) = 1 - P(A)`}</Eq>. Useful for "at least one" — compute the complement (none) and subtract.
- **Inclusion–exclusion**: <Eq>{`P(A \\cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \\cap B)`}</Eq>. If the events overlap, the intersection was counted twice.

**Conditional probability**: <Eq>{`P(A \\mid B) = P(A \\cap B)/P(B)`}</Eq>. Restricts the space to what has already occurred.

**Independence**: <Eq>{`P(A \\cap B) = P(A) \\cdot P(B)`}</Eq>. Don't confuse with "mutually exclusive" — they are opposite concepts when probabilities are positive.

**Bayes**: given <Eq>{`P(B \\mid A)`}</Eq> and <Eq>{`P(A)`}</Eq>, calculates <Eq>{`P(A \\mid B)`}</Eq>. Classic application: medical diagnosis, spam filtering.

**Connection to combinatorics** (Lesson 38): <Eq>{`|A|`}</Eq> and <Eq>{`|\\Omega|`}</Eq> are counts — combinations and permutations serve exactly this purpose.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

Probability is the foundation of statistics, machine learning, stochastic control, quantitative finance, and information theory.

### Formal probability space

<Eq>{`(\\Omega, \\mathcal{F}, P)`}</Eq> where <Eq>{`\\mathcal{F}`}</Eq> is a <Eq>{`\\sigma`}</Eq>-algebra of subsets of <Eq>{`\\Omega`}</Eq> and <Eq>{`P: \\mathcal{F} \\to [0,1]`}</Eq> is <Eq>{`\\sigma`}</Eq>-additive. In discrete case, <Eq>{`\\mathcal{F} = 2^\\Omega`}</Eq>.

### Total probability and Bayes — medical diagnosis

Disease <Eq>{`D`}</Eq> with prevalence <Eq>{`P(D) = 0.01`}</Eq>. Test: sensitivity <Eq>{`P(+ \\mid D) = 0.95`}</Eq>, specificity <Eq>{`P(- \\mid D^c) = 0.90`}</Eq>.

<Eq>{`P(+) = P(+ \\mid D)P(D) + P(+ \\mid D^c)P(D^c) = 0.95 \\times 0.01 + 0.10 \\times 0.99 = 0.1085`}</Eq>

<Eq>{`P(D \\mid +) = \\frac{0.95 \\times 0.01}{0.1085} \\approx 0.0876`}</Eq>

Only **8.8%** — the base rate fallacy. High sensitivity does not guarantee high precision when prevalence is low.

### Applications in engineering

- **Series reliability**: <Eq>{`P(\\text{system works}) = \\prod P(\\text{component}_i \\text{ works})`}</Eq>.
- **Parallel reliability**: <Eq>{`P(\\text{system fails}) = \\prod P(\\text{component}_i \\text{ fails})`}</Eq>.
- **Quality control**: proportion of defects, binomial hypothesis test.
- **Communication networks**: collision probability, bit error rate.
- **ML**: Naive Bayes classifier, maximum likelihood estimation.

### Law of Large Numbers (preview)

<Eq>{`\\bar{X}_n \\xrightarrow{P} E[X]`}</Eq> as <Eq>{`n \\to \\infty`}</Eq>. Relative frequency converges to probability — frequentist justification.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

Kolmogorov's formalism (1933) axiomatizes probability as a measure on <Eq>{`\\sigma`}</Eq>-algebras. On discrete <Eq>{`\\Omega`}</Eq>, <Eq>{`\\mathcal{F} = 2^\\Omega`}</Eq> and everything is countable. On continuous <Eq>{`\\Omega`}</Eq> (e.g., <Eq>{`[0,1]`}</Eq>), non-measurable sets (Vitali) require restriction to Borel <Eq>{`\\sigma`}</Eq>-algebras.

### Bayesian inference in production

Posterior <Eq>{`\\propto`}</Eq> prior <Eq>{`\\times`}</Eq> likelihood. For conjugate likelihoods (Beta-Binomial, Normal-Normal, Dirichlet-Multinomial), the posterior has closed form. For generic models, MCMC (Metropolis-Hastings, HMC, NUTS) samples from the posterior.

### Change of measure — finance

Risk-neutral pricing: <Eq>{`V_0 = e^{-rT} E^Q[\\text{payoff}]`}</Eq>. The change <Eq>{`P \\to Q`}</Eq> via Radon-Nikodym derivative is the essence of mathematical finance; Girsanov's theorem transforms the drift of Brownian motion.

### Concentration inequalities

- **Markov**: <Eq>{`P(X \\geq a) \\leq E[X]/a`}</Eq> — requires only <Eq>{`E[X] < \\infty`}</Eq>.
- **Chebyshev**: <Eq>{`P(|X - \\mu| \\geq k\\sigma) \\leq 1/k^2`}</Eq> — requires finite variance.
- **Hoeffding**: <Eq>{`P(|\\bar{X}_n - \\mu| \\geq t) \\leq 2\\exp(-2nt^2/(b-a)^2)`}</Eq> — basis of PAC learning.

### Information theory

- Shannon entropy: <Eq>{`H(X) = -\\sum p_i \\log_2 p_i`}</Eq>.
- KL divergence: <Eq>{`D_{KL}(p \\| q) = \\sum p_i \\log(p_i/q_i)`}</Eq> — basis of variational methods and EM.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practical application">

**Solution flowchart for probability problems:**

1. **Define** <Eq>{`\\Omega`}</Eq> **explicitly.** Errors in definition cause 80% of probability mistakes.
2. **Are all outcomes equiprobable?** If yes, use <Eq>{`|A|/|\\Omega|`}</Eq> and combinatorics.
3. **Is the event a complement of something simple?** Compute the complement and subtract from 1.
4. **Is there overlap between events?** Use inclusion–exclusion.
5. **Is there conditioning or inversion of conditional?** Identify what is given and apply Bayes.
6. **Are events independent?** Multiply the probabilities. Don't assume independence — verify.

### Bayes table (diagnosis — template)

| | D (disease) | <Eq>{`D^c`}</Eq> | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| Test <Eq>{`+`}</Eq> | <Eq>{`P(+\\mid D) \\cdot P(D)`}</Eq> | <Eq>{`P(+\\mid D^c) \\cdot P(D^c)`}</Eq> | <Eq>{`P(+)`}</Eq> |
| Test <Eq>{`-`}</Eq> | <Eq>{`P(-\\mid D) \\cdot P(D)`}</Eq> | <Eq>{`P(-\\mid D^c) \\cdot P(D^c)`}</Eq> | <Eq>{`P(-)`}</Eq> |
| Total | <Eq>{`P(D)`}</Eq> | <Eq>{`P(D^c)`}</Eq> | 1 |

### Common pitfalls

- **Confusing** <Eq>{`P(A \\mid B)`}</Eq> **with** <Eq>{`P(B \\mid A)`}</Eq>: inversion is exactly what Bayes solves.
- **Independence** <Eq>{`\\neq`}</Eq> **mutually exclusive**.
- **Sums of dice are not equiprobable**: 36 pairs <Eq>{`(i,j)`}</Eq> are equiprobable, but the sums (2 to 12) are not.
- **Base rate fallacy**: ignoring the prior <Eq>{`P(A)`}</Eq> leads to serious errors in Bayes.
- **Sampling with vs. without replacement**: changes the sample space.

</Porta>

## Worked Examples

<Exemplo titulo="Classical probability with two dice">

**Problem.** Two fair six-sided dice are rolled simultaneously. Calculate the probability that their sum equals 8.

**Strategy.** Construct the sample space <Eq>{`\\Omega`}</Eq> of ordered pairs <Eq>{`(i, j)`}</Eq> with <Eq>{`i, j \\in \\{1,2,3,4,5,6\\}`}</Eq> and identify the pairs summing to 8.

**Solution.**

1. **Sample space**: <Eq>{`|\\Omega| = 36`}</Eq>.
2. **Event** <Eq>{`A`}</Eq> **= sum equals 8**: pairs <Eq>{`(2,6),(3,5),(4,4),(5,3),(6,2)`}</Eq>, so <Eq>{`|A| = 5`}</Eq>.
3. **Probability**:
<Eq>{`P(A) = \\frac{|A|}{|\\Omega|} = \\frac{5}{36}`}</Eq>

**Verification.** Sum 8 is less likely than sum 7 (6 pairs) and more likely than sum 9 (4 pairs) — consistent with the triangular distribution of sums.

**Source.** [OpenIntro Statistics, 4th ed., §3.1](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/) — Diez, Çetinkaya-Rundel, Barr · CC-BY-SA.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Complement and inclusion–exclusion in a deck">

**Problem.** A card is drawn at random from a standard 52-card deck. Calculate: (a) <Eq>{`P(\\text{king or hearts})`}</Eq>; (b) <Eq>{`P(\\text{not a king})`}</Eq>.

**Strategy.** For (a), apply inclusion–exclusion: <Eq>{`P(A \\cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \\cap B)`}</Eq>, since the king of hearts belongs to both events. For (b), use complement.

**Solution.**

1. <Eq>{`A = \\{\\text{king}\\}`}</Eq>, <Eq>{`B = \\{\\text{hearts}\\}`}</Eq>. <Eq>{`|A| = 4`}</Eq>, <Eq>{`|B| = 13`}</Eq>, <Eq>{`|A \\cap B| = 1`}</Eq>.

2. **(a) Inclusion–exclusion**:
<Eq>{`P(A \\cup B) = \\frac{4}{52} + \\frac{13}{52} - \\frac{1}{52} = \\frac{16}{52} = \\frac{4}{13} \\approx 0.308`}</Eq>

3. **(b) Complement**:
<Eq>{`P(\\text{not a king}) = 1 - \\frac{4}{52} = \\frac{48}{52} = \\frac{12}{13} \\approx 0.923`}</Eq>

**Verification.** In (a): <Eq>{`4 + 13 - 1 = 16`}</Eq> cards satisfy "king or hearts". Correct.

**Source.** [OpenStax Statistics, §3.3](https://openstax.org/books/statistics/pages/3-3-two-basic-rules-of-probability) — Illowsky, Dean · CC-BY.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Conditional probability in an urn without replacement">

**Problem.** An urn contains 5 red balls and 3 blue balls. Two balls are drawn **without replacement**. Calculate: (a) <Eq>{`P(\\text{second is blue} \\mid \\text{first was red})`}</Eq>; (b) <Eq>{`P(\\text{both blue})`}</Eq>.

**Strategy.** For (a), after drawing one red ball, the urn has 7 balls remaining, 3 blue. For (b), apply the multiplication rule.

**Solution.**

1. **(a)** After drawing 1 red, 7 balls remain: 4 red and 3 blue.
<Eq>{`P(\\text{2nd blue} \\mid \\text{1st red}) = \\frac{3}{7}`}</Eq>

2. **(b)** Multiplication rule:
<Eq>{`P(\\text{both blue}) = \\frac{3}{8} \\cdot \\frac{2}{7} = \\frac{6}{56} = \\frac{3}{28}`}</Eq>

**Verification via combinatorics**: <Eq>{`\\binom{3}{2}/\\binom{8}{2} = 3/28`}</Eq>. Matches.

**Source.** [OpenIntro Statistics, 4th ed., §3.2, example 3.28](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/) — CC-BY-SA.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Birthday paradox — complement">

**Problem.** In a room with 3 people, what is the probability that at least two share a birthday (365 equally likely days)?

**Strategy.** Calculate the complement: all 3 people have birthdays on different days.

**Solution.**

1. **Complement** (all days distinct):
<Eq>{`P(\\text{all distinct}) = \\frac{365}{365} \\cdot \\frac{364}{365} \\cdot \\frac{363}{365} = \\frac{365 \\cdot 364 \\cdot 363}{365^3}`}</Eq>

2. **Probability asked for**:
<Eq>{`P(\\text{at least 2 equal}) = 1 - \\frac{365 \\cdot 364 \\cdot 363}{365^3} \\approx 1 - 0.9918 = 0.0082`}</Eq>

Only 0.82% for 3 people. For 23 people, this value exceeds 50% — the famous birthday paradox.

**Source.** [OpenIntro Statistics, 4th ed., §3.1, exercise 3.5](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/) — CC-BY-SA.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Bayes' theorem — medical diagnosis">

**Problem.** A disease affects 1% of the population (<Eq>{`P(D) = 0.01`}</Eq>). A test has sensitivity <Eq>{`P(+ \\mid D) = 0.90`}</Eq> and specificity <Eq>{`P(- \\mid D^c) = 0.95`}</Eq>. A person tests positive. What is the probability they have the disease?

**Strategy.** Apply Bayes with the law of total probability in the denominator.

**Solution.**

1. **Total probability of positive test**:
<Eq>{`P(+) = 0.90 \\times 0.01 + 0.05 \\times 0.99 = 0.009 + 0.0495 = 0.0585`}</Eq>

2. **Bayes**:
<Eq>{`P(D \\mid +) = \\frac{0.90 \\times 0.01}{0.0585} = \\frac{0.009}{0.0585} \\approx 0.154`}</Eq>

Only **15.4%** probability of disease with positive test — the effect of low prevalence: most positives are false positives (<Eq>{`0.0495/0.0585 \\approx 84.6\\%`}</Eq>).

**Verification:** <Eq>{`P(D \\mid +) + P(D^c \\mid +) = 0.154 + 0.846 = 1`}</Eq>. Consistent.

**Source.** [OpenStax Statistics, §3.2, example 3.16](https://openstax.org/books/statistics/pages/3-2-independent-and-mutually-exclusive-events) — CC-BY. Adapted from [OpenIntro Statistics §3.2](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/).
</Exemplo>
