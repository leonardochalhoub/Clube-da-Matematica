<Porta nivel="formal" titulo="Rigorous definition">

### Matrices — basic types

A **matrix** $A$ of order $m \times n$ is a rectangular table of $mn$ real numbers with $m$ rows and $n$ columns. Its entry in position $(i, j)$ is $a_{ij}$.

<Definicao titulo="Special types of matrix">

| Type | Condition | Symbol |
|---|---|---|
| Row | $m = 1$ | $[a_{1j}]_{1 \times n}$ |
| Column | $n = 1$ | $[a_{i1}]_{m \times 1}$ |
| Square | $m = n$ | $[a_{ij}]_{n \times n}$ |
| Diagonal | square, $a_{ij}=0$ if $i \neq j$ | $\mathrm{diag}(d_1,\ldots,d_n)$ |
| Identity | diagonal with $d_i = 1$ | $I_n$ |
| Zero | all entries $= 0$ | $O$ |

</Definicao>

### Addition and scalar multiplication

<Definicao titulo="Matrix addition">

Defined only for matrices of **the same order** $m \times n$:

$$( A + B )_{ij} = a_{ij} + b_{ij}$$

</Definicao>

<Definicao titulo="Scalar multiplication">

For $c \in \mathbb{R}$ and $A \in M_{m \times n}(\mathbb{R})$:

$$(cA)_{ij} = c \cdot a_{ij}$$

</Definicao>

### Matrix product

<Definicao titulo="Product AB">

Defined **only when** the number of columns of $A$ equals the number of rows of $B$. If $A$ is $m \times n$ and $B$ is $n \times p$, then $C = AB$ is $m \times p$:

$$\boxed{(AB)_{ij} = \sum_{k=1}^{n} a_{ik}\, b_{kj}}, \quad i = 1, \ldots, m,\; j = 1, \ldots, p.$$

</Definicao>

> "The matrix product $AB$ is defined when the number of columns of $A$ equals the number of rows of $B$. If $A$ is $m \times n$ and $B$ is $n \times p$, then the product $AB$ is $m \times p$." — [OpenStax College Algebra 2e, §9.5](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/9-5-matrices-and-matrix-operations)

> "If $A$ is an $m \times n$ matrix and $B$ is an $n \times p$ matrix, then the product $AB$ is defined and is an $m \times p$ matrix. The $(i,j)$ element of $AB$ is the dot product of the $i$-th row of $A$ with the $j$-th column of $B$." — [Beezer, A First Course in Linear Algebra, §MO](http://linear.pugetsound.edu/html/section-MO.html)

### Properties

<Teorema titulo="Properties of matrix operations">

| Operation | Property | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Addition | commutative: $A+B = B+A$ | always |
| Addition | associative: $(A+B)+C = A+(B+C)$ | always |
| Addition | identity: $A + O = A$ | $O$ is the zero matrix |
| Scalar | $c(A+B) = cA + cB$ | distributive |
| Scalar | $(c+d)A = cA + dA$ | distributive |
| Product | associative: $(AB)C = A(BC)$ | always |
| Product | distributive: $A(B+C) = AB + AC$ | always |
| Product | **NOT commutative**: $AB \neq BA$ in general | critical rule |
| Product | identity: $AI_n = I_m A = A$ | for $A \in M_{m \times n}$ |
| Product | transpose: $(AB)^T = B^T A^T$ | order reverses |

</Teorema>

### Non-commutativity and composition

The product $AB$ corresponds to applying transformation $B$ first and then $A$. Since function composition depends on order, the product does not commute.

Canonical example: <Eq>{`A = \\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\\\ 0 & 0 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>, <Eq>{`B = \\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\\\ 1 & 0 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>. Then <Eq>{`AB = \\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\\\ 0 & 0 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq> and <Eq>{`BA = \\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\\\ 0 & 1 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>, which are different.

<Insight>

Non-commutativity is not a defect — it is the precise expression of the fact that **the order of transformations matters**. Rotating an object and then scaling it produces a different result than scaling it and then rotating. Matrix product captures this exactly.

</Insight>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

Imagine you have an **egg carton** with two levels and three columns. There are six spaces total.

To **add** two identical cartons, you pair egg with egg: the corner space with the corner space, the middle one with the middle one. Easy.

To **multiply everything by 3**, you replace each egg with three eggs. Each space becomes three times larger.

But there is a **special trick** called "matrix product" that is more complicated. It is as if you needed to pair the rows of one carton with the columns of another carton, multiply pair by pair, and add everything up. The computer does this trick millions of times per second to display images in video games.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

A **matrix** is a table of numbers organized in rows and columns. Like a spreadsheet.

**Addition is easy**: add number to number in the same position. The two matrices must be the same size.

**Multiplying by a number is also easy**: multiply each number in the table by the same value.

**Matrix product** is the difficult trick. Imagine:
- A table with **prices** of 3 products in US dollars (row: P1 = US$10, P2 = US$25, P3 = US$40).
- A table with **quantities sold** by store and by product.
- You want to know the **total revenue** by store: that is the matrix product.

To calculate each number in the result, you take an entire row of the first table and an entire column of the second, multiply the elements one by one, and add everything up.

**Dimension rule**: to multiply $A \times B$, the number of columns of $A$ must equal the number of rows of $B$.

**Important warning**: $A \times B$ is almost never equal to $B \times A$. Order matters a lot.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old teen">

Three distinct operations. The first two are straightforward; the third requires care.

**Addition** (same dimensions required):
$$\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} 5 & -1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 6 & 1 \\ 3 & 6 \end{pmatrix}$$

**Scalar** (multiplies each entry):
$$3 \cdot \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ 0 & 4 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 6 & -3 \\ 0 & 12 \end{pmatrix}$$

**Matrix product** — row times column, summed:

To compute <Eq>{`\\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\\\ 3 & 4 \\end{pmatrix} \\cdot \\begin{pmatrix} 5 & 6 \\\\ 7 & 8 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>:

- $(AB)_{11}$: row 1 of $A$ · column 1 of $B$: $1 \cdot 5 + 2 \cdot 7 = 19$
- $(AB)_{12}$: row 1 of $A$ · column 2 of $B$: $1 \cdot 6 + 2 \cdot 8 = 22$
- $(AB)_{21}$: row 2 of $A$ · column 1 of $B$: $3 \cdot 5 + 4 \cdot 7 = 43$
- $(AB)_{22}$: row 2 of $A$ · column 2 of $B$: $3 \cdot 6 + 4 \cdot 8 = 50$

Result: <Eq>{`\\begin{pmatrix} 19 & 22 \\\\ 43 & 50 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>.

**Dimension rule**: $A_{m \times n} \cdot B_{n \times p} = C_{m \times p}$. The inner dimensions ($n$) must match; the outer dimensions ($m$ and $p$) form the result's dimension.

**Non-commutativity**: swap $A$ and $B$ and compute $BA$. You get a different result. This is not a bug — it is a fundamental property.

**Identity**: $A \cdot I = I \cdot A = A$ for any square $A$. The identity $I_n$ has 1's on the diagonal and 0's elsewhere.

<Insight>
Why is the matrix product "row times column"? Because it represents the **composition** of linear transformations. If $B$ first transforms a vector and $A$ then transforms the result, then $AB$ is the unique transformation that does everything at once. Rotation followed by scaling is different from scaling followed by rotation — hence $AB \neq BA$.
</Insight>

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

Matrix operations are primitives of **computational linear algebra** (LAPACK/BLAS). The cost hierarchy:

- **Addition** $A + B$: $O(mn)$ flops — trivial.
- **Scalar** $cA$: $O(mn)$ flops — trivial.
- **Product** $A_{m \times n} B_{n \times p}$: $O(mnp)$ flops — dominates in deep networks.

### Product = composition of linear transformations

Let $T_B: \mathbb{R}^p \to \mathbb{R}^n$ and $T_A: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ be linear with matrices $B$ and $A$. Then $T_A \circ T_B: \mathbb{R}^p \to \mathbb{R}^m$ has matrix $AB$. Proof:

$$(T_A \circ T_B)(\mathbf{x}) = T_A(B\mathbf{x}) = A(B\mathbf{x}) = (AB)\mathbf{x}.$$

This explains non-commutativity (function composition does not commute in general) and associativity (composition is associative).

### Fundamental cross-domain identities

- **Control**: $\dot{\mathbf{x}} = A\mathbf{x} + B\mathbf{u}$ — state-space equation with sum and matrix product.
- **ML**: forward pass $\mathbf{z}^{(l)} = W^{(l)}\mathbf{a}^{(l-1)} + \mathbf{b}^{(l)}$ — GEMM + bias per layer.
- **Graphics**: homogeneous transformation $4 \times 4$ combines rotation, scale, translation.
- **Statistics**: covariance $\Sigma = E[(\mathbf{x}-\mu)(\mathbf{x}-\mu)^T]$ — outer product of vectors.

### Complexity and algorithms

| Algorithm | Cost | Practical use |
|---|---|---|
| Naïve (3 loops) | $O(n^3)$ | $n < 200$ |
| Strassen | $O(n^{2.807})$ | $n > 1\,000$ with overhead |
| BLAS Level 3 (DGEMM) | $O(n^3)$ with small constant | industry standard |
| Tensor Cores (NVIDIA) | mixed FP16/FP32 | generative AI |

### Special classes and powers

- **Idempotent** ($A^2 = A$): projection matrices.
- **Nilpotent** ($A^k = O$ for some $k$): Jordan blocks.
- **Involution** ($A^2 = I$): isometric reflections.

### Transpose rule

$(AB)^T = B^T A^T$ — order reverses. Essential in backpropagation (gradient of $W\mathbf{x}$ with respect to $W$ is $\mathbf{x}^T$).

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

The pair $(M_n(\mathbb{R}), +, \cdot)$ is a **unitary associative $\mathbb{R}$-algebra of dimension $n^2$**, non-commutative for $n \geq 2$. It is isomorphic to $\mathrm{End}(\mathbb{R}^n)$ — the ring of linear endomorphisms.

### Algebraic structure

- $(M_n, +)$ is a free abelian group over $\mathbb{R}$-module.
- $(M_n, \cdot)$ is a monoid with identity $I_n$, **non-commutative**.
- $M_n$ has **zero divisors**: <Eq>{`\\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\\\ 0 & 0 \\end{pmatrix}\\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\\\ 0 & 1 \\end{pmatrix} = O`}</Eq>. Thus it is not an integral domain.
- The group $GL_n(\mathbb{R}) = \{A \in M_n : \det A \neq 0\}$ is the group of invertible matrices.

### GEMM on modern hardware

- **Tensor Cores** (Volta+): GEMM in FP16 accumulated in FP32; $\sim 8\times$ speedup over FP64 naive.
- **TPU** (Google): systolic array $128 \times 128$ — each cycle advances one dot product.
- **Roofline model**: dense GEMM is compute-bound for large matrices; attention is memory-bound for short sequences.

### Finance and PDE

Crank-Nicolson method for Black-Scholes:

$$\mathbf{u}^{n+1} = (I - \tfrac{\tau}{2}A)^{-1}(I + \tfrac{\tau}{2}A)\mathbf{u}^n.$$

Each time step requires product $A\mathbf{u}^n$ (GEMV) and tridiagonal solve — operations in $M_n$.

### Current research

- Lower bound on complexity of matrix product: open whether $\omega = 2$ is achievable.
- **DeepMind AlphaTensor** (2022): discovered matrix product algorithms with fewer scalar multiplications than Strassen in certain fields.
- **GraphBLAS**: generalizes GEMM to semirings (e.g., $(\min, +)$ for shortest paths).

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practice — 3 scenarios with US data">

### Scenario 1: Production cost in a textile factory

A factory in North Carolina produces three shirt models (M1, M2, M3). The **unit costs** of raw material and labor are:

$$C = \begin{pmatrix} 12 & 8 \\ 18 & 11 \\ 25 & 15 \end{pmatrix} \text{ (rows = models, columns = [raw material, labor])}$$

In April, production at two facilities (CA and NC) was:

$$Q = \begin{pmatrix} 300 & 150 & 80 \\ 200 & 400 & 60 \end{pmatrix} \text{ (rows = facilities, columns = models)}$$

The **total cost per facility and per category** is $QC$ (dimension $2 \times 2$). Example: raw material cost for CA facility = $300(12) + 150(18) + 80(25) = 3{,}600 + 2{,}700 + 2{,}000 = 8{,}300$ dollars.

Matrix product $QC$ automatically gives the costs for all facilities in all categories — without repeating calculations row by row.

### Scenario 2: Markov chain for customer churn (digital bank)

A digital bank models client transitions between two states: **Active** (A) and **Inactive** (I). The monthly transition matrix is:

$$P = \begin{pmatrix} 0.92 & 0.08 \\ 0.15 & 0.85 \end{pmatrix}$$

(row 1 = starting from A: 92% stays active, 8% becomes inactive; row 2 = starting from I: 15% reactivates, 85% stays inactive.)

If today the distribution is $\pi_0 = (0.70,\; 0.30)^T$ (70% active), after two months:

$$\pi_2 = P^2 \pi_0$$

Compute $P^2$ and then $P^2 \pi_0$ — the result is the forecast distribution after two months. This matrix power structure appears in LTV (lifetime value) models for clients at US digital banks like Square, Stripe, and Affirm.

### Scenario 3: Geometric transformation in computer graphics

In game engines (Unity, Godot, Blender — all used by US studios), a rotation followed by scaling is represented by matrix composition:

$$M = S \cdot R(\theta) = \begin{pmatrix} s & 0 \\ 0 & s \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} s\cos\theta & -s\sin\theta \\ s\sin\theta & s\cos\theta \end{pmatrix}$$

For $\theta = 45°$ and $s = 2$: <Eq>{`M = \\begin{pmatrix} \\sqrt{2} & -\\sqrt{2} \\\\ \\sqrt{2} & \\sqrt{2} \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>.

Applying to vertex $(1, 0)^T$: result = $(\sqrt{2},\; \sqrt{2})^T$ — the vertex was rotated 45° and doubled in size, in **a single matrix product**, which is more efficient than applying transformations separately.

</Porta>

## Worked Examples

<Exemplo titulo="Matrix addition and scalar multiplication" numero="1">

**Problem.** Given <Eq>{`A = \\begin{pmatrix} 3 & -1 \\\\ 0 & 4 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq> and <Eq>{`B = \\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 5 \\\\ -3 & 1 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>, compute $A + B$ and $2A - B$.

**Strategy.** Addition and subtraction of matrices are entry-by-entry operations. Scalar multiplication multiplies each entry. We always verify that dimensions match — here both are $2 \times 2$, so the operations are defined.

**Solution.**

Step 1 — Addition:
$$(A + B)_{ij} = a_{ij} + b_{ij}$$
$$A + B = \begin{pmatrix} 3+2 & -1+5 \\ 0+(-3) & 4+1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 4 \\ -3 & 5 \end{pmatrix}$$

Step 2 — Scalar on $A$:
$$2A = \begin{pmatrix} 6 & -2 \\ 0 & 8 \end{pmatrix}$$

Step 3 — Difference $2A - B$:
$$2A - B = \begin{pmatrix} 6-2 & -2-5 \\ 0-(-3) & 8-1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & -7 \\ 3 & 7 \end{pmatrix}$$

**Verification.** $(2A - B)_{11} = 2 \cdot 3 - 2 = 4$. Correct.

**Source.** [OpenStax — College Algebra 2e §9.5](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/9-5-matrices-and-matrix-operations) — CC-BY 4.0 license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="2×2 matrix product and non-commutativity" numero="2">

**Problem.** Compute $AB$ and $BA$ for <Eq>{`A = \\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 3 \\\\ 2 & 0 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq> and <Eq>{`B = \\begin{pmatrix} -1 & 2 \\\\ 4 & 1 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>.

**Strategy.** Each entry $(AB)_{ij}$ is the dot product of row $i$ of $A$ with column $j$ of $B$. We will compute $AB$ and then $BA$ to explicitly demonstrate non-commutativity.

**Solution.**

Product $AB$:

$$(AB)_{11} = 1 \cdot (-1) + 3 \cdot 4 = -1 + 12 = 11$$
$$(AB)_{12} = 1 \cdot 2 + 3 \cdot 1 = 2 + 3 = 5$$
$$(AB)_{21} = 2 \cdot (-1) + 0 \cdot 4 = -2 + 0 = -2$$
$$(AB)_{22} = 2 \cdot 2 + 0 \cdot 1 = 4 + 0 = 4$$

$$AB = \begin{pmatrix} 11 & 5 \\ -2 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$$

Product $BA$:

$$(BA)_{11} = (-1) \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot 2 = -1 + 4 = 3$$
$$(BA)_{12} = (-1) \cdot 3 + 2 \cdot 0 = -3 + 0 = -3$$
$$(BA)_{21} = 4 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 2 = 4 + 2 = 6$$
$$(BA)_{22} = 4 \cdot 3 + 1 \cdot 0 = 12 + 0 = 12$$

$$BA = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & -3 \\ 6 & 12 \end{pmatrix}$$

**Conclusion.** <Eq>{`AB = \\begin{pmatrix} 11 & 5 \\\\ -2 & 4 \\end{pmatrix} \\neq \\begin{pmatrix} 3 & -3 \\\\ 6 & 12 \\end{pmatrix} = BA`}</Eq>. Matrix multiplication is not commutative.

**Source.** [OpenStax — College Algebra 2e §9.5](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/9-5-matrices-and-matrix-operations) — CC-BY 4.0 license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Matrix product with distinct dimensions — 2x3 by 3x2" numero="3">

**Problem.** Compute $AB$ for <Eq>{`A = \\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 2 \\\\ -1 & 3 & 1 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq> (order $2 \times 3$) and <Eq>{`B = \\begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \\\\ 2 & -1 \\\\ 0 & 4 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq> (order $3 \times 2$).

**Strategy.** We verify the dimension rule: columns of $A$ = 3 = rows of $B$. The result $AB$ will have dimension $2 \times 2$. We compute each of the four entries via dot product of row by column.

**Solution.**

Step 1 — Verification of dimensions: $A$ is $2 \times 3$ and $B$ is $3 \times 2$. The inner dimensions match ($n = 3$). The product $AB$ will be $2 \times 2$.

Step 2 — Computing entries:

$$(AB)_{11} = 1 \cdot 3 + 0 \cdot 2 + 2 \cdot 0 = 3 + 0 + 0 = 3$$
$$(AB)_{12} = 1 \cdot 1 + 0 \cdot (-1) + 2 \cdot 4 = 1 + 0 + 8 = 9$$
$$(AB)_{21} = (-1) \cdot 3 + 3 \cdot 2 + 1 \cdot 0 = -3 + 6 + 0 = 3$$
$$(AB)_{22} = (-1) \cdot 1 + 3 \cdot (-1) + 1 \cdot 4 = -1 - 3 + 4 = 0$$

$$AB = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 9 \\ 3 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$$

Step 3 — Note that $BA$ would be $3 \times 3$ (defined), but $AB$ and $BA$ have different dimensions — they are completely distinct.

**Verification.** $(AB)_{11} = $ row 1 of $A$ · column 1 of $B$ $= (1)(3) + (0)(2) + (2)(0) = 3$. Correct.

**Source.** [Beezer — A First Course in Linear Algebra, §MO](http://linear.pugetsound.edu/html/section-MO.html) — GNU FDL license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="3×3 matrix product" numero="4">

**Problem.** Compute $AB$ for <Eq>{`A = \\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 & 1 \\\\ 1 & 3 & -1 \\\\ 0 & 2 & 4 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq> and <Eq>{`B = \\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 0 \\\\ 0 & 1 & 3 \\\\ 2 & -1 & 1 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>.

**Strategy.** Both are $3 \times 3$, so $AB$ is also $3 \times 3$ — nine entries to compute. We proceed row by row of $A$, column by column of $B$.

**Solution.**

Row 1 of $A$ = $(2, 0, 1)$:

$$(AB)_{11} = 2(1) + 0(0) + 1(2) = 4$$
$$(AB)_{12} = 2(2) + 0(1) + 1(-1) = 3$$
$$(AB)_{13} = 2(0) + 0(3) + 1(1) = 1$$

Row 2 of $A$ = $(1, 3, -1)$:

$$(AB)_{21} = 1(1) + 3(0) + (-1)(2) = -1$$
$$(AB)_{22} = 1(2) + 3(1) + (-1)(-1) = 6$$
$$(AB)_{23} = 1(0) + 3(3) + (-1)(1) = 8$$

Row 3 of $A$ = $(0, 2, 4)$:

$$(AB)_{31} = 0(1) + 2(0) + 4(2) = 8$$
$$(AB)_{32} = 0(2) + 2(1) + 4(-1) = -2$$
$$(AB)_{33} = 0(0) + 2(3) + 4(1) = 10$$

$$AB = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 3 & 1 \\ -1 & 6 & 8 \\ 8 & -2 & 10 \end{pmatrix}$$

**Verification.** $(AB)_{22} = 1(2) + 3(1) + (-1)(-1) = 2 + 3 + 1 = 6$. Correct.

**Source.** [Beezer — A First Course in Linear Algebra, §MO](http://linear.pugetsound.edu/html/section-MO.html) — GNU FDL license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Total revenue by region via matrix product (modeling)" numero="5">

**Problem.** A company sells three products (P1, P2, P3) in two regions (South, North). The **unit price** matrix is <Eq>{`P = \\begin{pmatrix} 10 & 25 & 40 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq> (row vector, in US$). The **quantities sold** matrix by region is:

$$Q = \begin{pmatrix} 200 & 150 & 80 \\ 120 & 300 & 50 \end{pmatrix}$$

(rows = regions South/North, columns = products P1/P2/P3). Compute the **total revenue by region**.

**Strategy.** Revenue for a region is $\sum_j p_j \cdot q_{ij}$. This is exactly the matrix product $R = Q \cdot P^T$, where $P^T$ is the column vector of prices. The result $R$ will be a $2 \times 1$ vector — revenue per region.

**Solution.**

Step 1 — Organization: $Q$ is $2 \times 3$, $P^T$ is $3 \times 1$. Thus $R = Q P^T$ will be $2 \times 1$.

$$P^T = \begin{pmatrix} 10 \\ 25 \\ 40 \end{pmatrix}$$

Step 2 — Revenue for South region (row 1 of $Q$):

$$R_1 = 200 \cdot 10 + 150 \cdot 25 + 80 \cdot 40 = 2{,}000 + 3{,}750 + 3{,}200 = 8{,}950$$

Step 3 — Revenue for North region (row 2 of $Q$):

$$R_2 = 120 \cdot 10 + 300 \cdot 25 + 50 \cdot 40 = 1{,}200 + 7{,}500 + 2{,}000 = 10{,}700$$

$$R = QP^T = \begin{pmatrix} 8{,}950 \\ 10{,}700 \end{pmatrix} \text{ (in US dollars)}$$

**Interpretation.** North region generates US$10,700 and South region generates US$8,950. Matrix product automates the calculation for any number of regions or products.
