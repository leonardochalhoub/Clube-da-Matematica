### Factorial

<Definicao titulo="Factorial of n">

$n! = n \cdot (n-1) \cdot (n-2) \cdots 1$. Convention: $0! = 1$.

<Equation explicacao="Recursion: n! = n times (n-1) factorial. Base: 0! = 1 by convention (counts the number of ways to order zero objects)." latex="n! = n \cdot (n-1)!, \quad 0! = 1" />

</Definicao>

> "We define the factorial of $n$ as $n! = n(n-1)(n-2) \cdots 2 \cdot 1$ for $n \geq 1$, and $0! = 1$." — [OpenStax Algebra and Trigonometry 2e, §11.7](https://openstax.org/books/algebra-and-trigonometry-2e/pages/11-7-introduction-to-counting-and-probability)

### Simple permutation

<Definicao titulo="Permutation Pn">

$P_n = n!$ — ways to order $n$ distinct objects in a row.

**Proof via PFC:** the 1st position has $n$ options; the 2nd has $n-1$ (one object already placed); the $k$-th has $n-k+1$. Product: $n(n-1)\cdots 1 = n!$.

</Definicao>

### Permutation with repetition

For $n$ objects with $n_1$ of type 1, $n_2$ of type 2, ..., $n_k$ of type $k$ (with $n_1 + n_2 + \cdots + n_k = n$):

<Equation explicacao="We divide by n_i! because swapping equal elements of type i among themselves does not generate a new configuration." latex="P_n^{n_1, n_2, \ldots, n_k} = \frac{n!}{n_1! \cdot n_2! \cdots n_k!}" />

Anagrams of "ARARA" (3 A's, 2 R's): $5!/(3! \cdot 2!) = 10$.

> "The number of distinguishable permutations of $n$ objects where there are $n_1$ identical objects of type 1, $n_2$ of type 2, ..., and $n_r$ of type $r$, is $\frac{n!}{n_1! n_2! \cdots n_r!}$." — [OpenStax Algebra and Trigonometry 2e, §11.7](https://openstax.org/books/algebra-and-trigonometry-2e/pages/11-7-introduction-to-counting-and-probability)

### Simple arrangement

<Definicao titulo="Arrangement A(n,p)">

$$A_n^p = n(n-1)(n-2) \cdots (n-p+1) = \frac{n!}{(n-p)!}$$

Ways to order $p$ objects selected from $n$ available ($p \leq n$).

</Definicao>

### Circular permutation

$n$ objects in a circle: $(n-1)!$. Reason: the "first position" is arbitrary — rotating all together does not generate a new configuration. Formally: fix one object in a position; the other $n-1$ permute freely.

<Insight>

**Difference between permutation and arrangement:** permutation uses **all** $n$ objects; arrangement uses **$p \leq n$** of them. When $p = n$: $A_n^n = n!/(n-n)! = n!/0! = n! = P_n$ — total arrangement coincides with permutation.

</Insight>

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

Imagine you have **3 toys** in a row: doll, car, and ball. In how many ways can you arrange them?

You could put doll, car, ball. Or doll, ball, car. Or car, doll, ball... **6 ways total!**

That "6" is $3 \times 2 \times 1$ — factorial of 3, written $3!$. For each new toy you add, you multiply!

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

**Permutation** = changing the order of a whole group.

Example: you have 5 books and want to arrange them on a shelf. **How many ways?** $5! = 5 \times 4 \times 3 \times 2 \times 1 = 120$.

**Arrangement** is similar, but you use **only some** of the items, not all.

Example: you have 10 athletes and will award medals (1st, 2nd, 3rd). **$10 \times 9 \times 8 = 720$**.

**Pay attention**: in both, **order matters**. Swapping A with B on the podium is different. If order didn't matter (committee of 3 people), it would be combination — lesson 38.

**When letters repeat**: anagrams of PARA = $4!/2! = 12$ (because letter A repeats 2 times — divide).

**Round table** is special: 5 people around the table = $4! = 24$ (not $5!$). Why? Rotating everyone together doesn't count as a new arrangement.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old teen">

**Permutation** = order everything. How many ways to arrange 5 books on a shelf? $5! = 120$.

**Arrangement** = order a selection. How many ways to award 1st, 2nd, and 3rd place among 10 athletes? $A_{10}^3 = 10 \cdot 9 \cdot 8 = 720$.

**To use the formula directly**: in both ORDER MATTERS. Swapping A with B in first and second is different.

**Permutation with repetition** is special: anagrams of "LOVE" are $4! = 24$, but anagrams of "LOOP" are $4!/2! = 12$ (not $24$, because OO equals OO).

**Classic cases**:
- "MISSISSIPPI" (11 letters: 1M, 4I, 4S, 2P): $11!/(1! \cdot 4! \cdot 4! \cdot 2!) = 34,650$.
- Round table with 8 people: $7! = 5,040$.

**Factorial grows fast**: $5! = 120$, $10! \approx 3.6$ million, $20!$ is already bigger than $10^{18}$. That's why permutation problems are "explosive".

**Arrangement vs PFC**: $A_n^p = n(n-1)(n-2)\cdots(n-p+1)$ is just PFC with the restriction "no repeats".

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

Permutations and arrangements are combinatorial primitives for enumeration and probabilistic estimation.

### Proof: $P_n = n!$

PFC: 1st position has $n$ options, 2nd position has $n-1$, ..., last has 1. Product: $n!$.

### Proof: arrangement via PFC

$A_n^p = n(n-1)(n-2)\cdots(n-p+1) = n!/(n-p)!$.

### Factorial growth

Stirling: $\ln(n!) = n\ln n - n + O(\ln n)$. Thus $n! \sim \sqrt{2\pi n}(n/e)^n$. In ML, important for Gaussian log-likelihood, entropy of categorical models.

### Cross-domain

- **TSP (traveling salesman)**: $n!$ routes → NP-hard. Heuristics (2-opt, Lin-Kernighan) attack practical instances.
- **Shuffling a deck**: $52! \approx 8 \times 10^{67}$ — Bayer-Diaconis (1992) proved that ~7 riffle shuffles suffice for nearly uniform distribution.
- **Sorting**: worst-case comparisons for sort = $\Omega(n \log n)$ because decision tree has $\geq n!$ leaves.

### Permutation as element of $S_n$

$S_n$ is the symmetric group of order $n!$. Group structure — composition, cycles, sign — is central in abstract algebra and Galois theory.

- **Sign**: $\text{sgn}(\sigma) \in \{+1, -1\}$ via parity of the number of transpositions.
- **Appears in $\det$**: $\det A = \sum_\sigma \text{sgn}(\sigma) \prod a_{i,\sigma(i)}$.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

Permutations are instances of the **symmetric group** $S_n$ — a central object in algebra, geometry, and physics.

### Structure of $S_n$

- $|S_n| = n!$.
- **Generators**: adjacent transpositions $(i, i+1)$.
- $S_n$ non-abelian for $n \geq 3$.
- **Alternating subgroup** $A_n$: permutations with sign +1, $|A_n| = n!/2$.
- $S_n$ non-solvable for $n \geq 5$ — hence impossibility of a general formula for roots of degree-5 polynomials (Galois).

### Quant — pricing

- **Hidden Markov models**: hidden states form an alphabet; permutation of labels is model symmetry.
- **Stochastic discount factor**: enumeration of historical scenarios by permutation for bootstrap.

### Permutations in modern ML

- **Transformer attention** is equivariant by token permutation — hence position encoding.
- **Permutation-invariant networks**: DeepSets, PointNet — aggregate features via sum/max.
- **Sinkhorn-Knopp**: learns soft permutation via doubly stochastic matrices.

### Combinatorial generation

- **Lehmer code**: bijection $S_n \leftrightarrow \prod \{0, \ldots, n-1\}$. Permits random indexing without list generation.
- **Heap's algorithm**: generates all $n!$ permutations in $O(n!)$ total time.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practical application">

**Workflow for permutation/arrangement.**

1. **Does order matter?** If yes, this is the place. If not, it's combination (Lesson 38).
2. **Use all elements?** Permutation. **Subset?** Arrangement.
3. **Repeat elements?** Divide by factorials of multiplicities.
4. **Circular setup?** $(n-1)!$.

### Python recipe

```python
import math
import itertools

# Factorial
math.factorial(10)        # 3628800

# Arrangement A(n, p)
math.perm(10, 3)          # 720

# Permutations generated
list(itertools.permutations([1, 2, 3]))   # 6 tuples

# Permutations of size k
list(itertools.permutations([1, 2, 3, 4], 2))   # A(4,2) = 12

# Permutation with repetition: generate and remove duplicates
def perm_com_rep(letras):
    return set(itertools.permutations(letras))

print(len(perm_com_rep("ARARA")))    # 10
```

### Pitfalls

- **Anagrams with equal letters** need division by factorial of each multiplicity.
- **Overflow**: $n! > 10^{300}$ for $n > 170$. Use `math.factorial` (Python arbitrary-precision int) or logarithms.
- **"At least one together"** is usually easier by complement.

</Porta>

## Worked examples

<Exemplo titulo="Direct arrangement calculation" numero="1">

**Problem:** Calculate $A_8^3$ — how many ways to choose and order 3 objects from 8 available.

**Strategy:** Apply the formula $A_n^p = n!/(n-p)!$, or equivalently $A_n^p = n(n-1)\cdots(n-p+1)$ ($p$ descending factors starting from $n$).

**Solution:**

1. Factorial form: $A_8^3 = \dfrac{8!}{(8-3)!} = \dfrac{8!}{5!} = \dfrac{8 \cdot 7 \cdot 6 \cdot 5!}{5!} = 8 \cdot 7 \cdot 6$.
2. Multiplication: $8 \cdot 7 = 56$ and $56 \cdot 6 = 336$.
3. Result: $A_8^3 = 336$.

**Verification:** By direct counting via PFC (1st position: 8 options; 2nd: 7; 3rd: 6) we get $8 \cdot 7 \cdot 6 = 336$. Consistent.

**Source.** [OpenStax Algebra and Trigonometry 2e §11.7](https://openstax.org/books/algebra-and-trigonometry-2e/pages/11-7-introduction-to-counting-and-probability) — CC-BY 4.0 license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Anagrams with repeated letters" numero="2">

**Problem:** How many distinct anagrams can be formed from the word "BANANA"?

**Strategy:** The word has 6 letters with repetitions: 3 letter A, 2 letters N, and 1 letter B. Apply the formula for permutation with repetition.

**Solution:**

1. Identify multiplicities: $n = 6$, $n_A = 3$, $n_N = 2$, $n_B = 1$.
2. Apply the formula: $P_6^{3,2,1} = \dfrac{6!}{3! \cdot 2! \cdot 1!}$.
3. Calculate: $6! = 720$, $3! = 6$, $2! = 2$, $1! = 1$. Thus $720/(6 \cdot 2 \cdot 1) = 720/12 = 60$.

**Verification:** Without dividing by repetitions, we would have $6! = 720$ permutations, but swaps among the 3 equal A's don't generate a new anagram ($3! = 6$ identical permutations), and swaps among the 2 N's give $2! = 2$ identical permutations. $720/(6 \cdot 2) = 60$.

**Source.** [Wikilivros — Matemática elementar / Combinatória](https://pt.wikibooks.org/wiki/Matem%C3%A1tica_elementar/Combinat%C3%B3ria) — CC-BY-SA license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Circular permutation with restriction" numero="3">

**Problem:** 8 people will sit around a round table. Two of them, Ana and Bruno, want to sit together. How many distinct configurations?

**Strategy:** Treat Ana and Bruno as a single block. Then we have 7 "objects" to distribute around the round table: $(7-1)! = 6!$. Within the block, Ana and Bruno can swap places: $2!$ ways.

**Solution:**

1. Block "AB" + 6 other people = 7 objects around round table: $(7-1)! = 6! = 720$.
2. Internal permutation of block: $2! = 2$ (AB or BA).
3. Total: $720 \cdot 2 = 1,440$.

**Verification:** Without the restriction, 8 people around a round table give $7! = 5,040$ configurations. Given Ana's place, Bruno has 2 possible neighbors among 7 remaining spots — fraction $2/7$. $5,040 \cdot 2/7 = 1,440$. Consistent.

**Source.** [Wikilivros — Matemática elementar / Combinatória](https://pt.wikibooks.org/wiki/Matem%C3%A1tica_elementar/Combinat%C3%B3ria) — CC-BY-SA license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Solving a factorial equation" numero="4">

**Problem:** Find $n \in \mathbb{N}$ such that $\dfrac{(n+2)!}{n!} = 56$.

**Strategy:** Simplify the factorial quotient using $(n+2)! = (n+2)(n+1) \cdot n!$, reducing to a polynomial equation in $n$.

**Solution:**

1. Simplify: $\dfrac{(n+2)!}{n!} = \dfrac{(n+2)(n+1) \cdot n!}{n!} = (n+2)(n+1)$.
2. Equation: $(n+2)(n+1) = 56$, or $n^2 + 3n + 2 = 56 \Rightarrow n^2 + 3n - 54 = 0$.
3. Quadratic formula: $n = \dfrac{-3 \pm \sqrt{9 + 216}}{2} = \dfrac{-3 \pm 15}{2}$. Solutions: $n = 6$ or $n = -9$.
4. Since $n \in \mathbb{N}$, we discard $n = -9$. Answer: $n = 6$.

**Verification:** $(6+2)!/6! = 8!/6! = 8 \cdot 7 = 56$. Checks out.

**Source.** [Stitz–Zeager Precalculus §9.5](https://www.stitz-zeager.com/szprecalculus07042013.pdf) — CC-BY-NC-SA license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Passwords with positional restriction" numero="5">

**Problem:** A password has 5 characters, all different, chosen from the alphabet $\{A, B, C, \ldots, Z\}$ (26 letters). By requirement, the **first** character must be a vowel ($A, E, I, O, U$). How many distinct passwords exist?

**Strategy:** By PFC: count the first position (vowel) and then the arrangement of the 4 remaining positions from the 25 letters left over.

**Solution:**

1. First character: 5 options (vowels).
2. Next 4 positions: arrangement $A_{25}^4 = 25 \cdot 24 \cdot 23 \cdot 22$, since no repetition.
3. Calculation: $25 \cdot 24 = 600$, $600 \cdot 23 = 13,800$, $13,800 \cdot 22 = 303,600$.
4. Total: $5 \cdot 303,600 = 1,518,000$.

**Verification:** Without restriction, $A_{26}^5 = 26 \cdot 25 \cdot 24 \cdot 23 \cdot 22 = 7,893,600$. The fraction of passwords starting with a vowel is $5/26$. $7,893,600 \cdot 5/26 = 1,518,000$. Consistent.

**Source.** [OpenStax Algebra and Trigonometry 2e §11.7](https://openstax.org/books/algebra-and-trigonometry-2e/pages/11-7-introduction-to-counting-and-probability) — CC-BY 4.0 license.
</Exemplo>
