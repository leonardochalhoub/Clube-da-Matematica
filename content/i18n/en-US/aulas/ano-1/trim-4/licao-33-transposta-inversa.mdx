### Transpose

<Definicao titulo="Transposed matrix">

Given $A \in M_{m \times n}(\mathbb{R})$, the **transpose** $A^T \in M_{n \times m}(\mathbb{R})$ is defined by

<Equation explicacao="The element in row i, column j of A transpose is the element in row j, column i of A. Rows become columns and columns become rows." latex="(A^T)_{ij} = a_{ji}, \quad 1 \leq i \leq n,\ 1 \leq j \leq m." />

</Definicao>

**Properties of the transpose** — for compatible $A, B$ and $\alpha \in \mathbb{R}$:

| Property | Formula |
|---|---|
| Involution | $(A^T)^T = A$ |
| Linearity (sum) | $(A + B)^T = A^T + B^T$ |
| Linearity (scalar) | $(\alpha A)^T = \alpha A^T$ |
| Product (order reverses) | $(AB)^T = B^T A^T$ |
| Inverse-transpose | $(A^{-1})^T = (A^T)^{-1}$ |

**Symmetric matrix**: $A = A^T$ (implies $A$ is square). **Skew-symmetric matrix**: $A^T = -A$ (implies main diagonal is zero).

> "For matrices $A$ and $B$, the transpose of the product satisfies $(AB)^T = B^T A^T$. The order is reversed, as also happens with the inverse." — [Beezer, A First Course in Linear Algebra, §MO](http://linear.pugetsound.edu/html/section-MO.html)

### Identity matrix

<Definicao titulo="Identity matrix">

$I_n$ is the $n \times n$ matrix with Kronecker delta as entries:

<Equation explicacao="The Kronecker delta equals 1 if i = j (main diagonal) and 0 if i ≠ j (off-diagonal). It is the multiplicative identity element for matrix multiplication." latex="(I_n)_{ij} = \delta_{ij} = \begin{cases} 1 & i = j \\ 0 & i \neq j \end{cases}" />

Central property: $A I_n = I_m A = A$ for $A \in M_{m \times n}$.

</Definicao>

### Inverse matrix

<Definicao titulo="Invertibility">

A square matrix $A \in M_{n \times n}(\mathbb{R})$ is **invertible** (or nonsingular) if there exists $A^{-1} \in M_{n \times n}(\mathbb{R})$ such that

<Equation explicacao="The inverse satisfies both equalities — left and right multiplication yields the identity. If such a matrix exists, it is unique." latex="A A^{-1} = A^{-1} A = I_n." />

</Definicao>

> "A square matrix $A$ has an inverse if and only if its columns are linearly independent — equivalently, if and only if $\det A \neq 0$." — [Beezer, FCLA, §MI](http://linear.pugetsound.edu/html/section-MI.html)

<Teorema titulo="Equivalence Theorem (square matrices)">

For $A \in M_{n \times n}(\mathbb{R})$, the following statements are equivalent:

1. $A$ is invertible.
2. $\det A \neq 0$.
3. $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$ has only the solution $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$.
4. The columns of $A$ are linearly independent.
5. $\text{rank}(A) = n$.
6. $A$ is a product of elementary matrices.

</Teorema>

### Explicit formula for $2 \times 2$

<Equation explicacao="For A = [[a,b],[c,d]]: swap the main diagonal elements, negate the off-diagonal elements, divide by the determinant. Valid only if ad - bc ≠ 0." latex="A = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix} \implies A^{-1} = \frac{1}{ad - bc}\begin{pmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{pmatrix}" />

### Gauss-Jordan method for $n \times n$

Form the augmented matrix $[A \mid I_n]$ and apply elementary row operations until you obtain $[I_n \mid A^{-1}]$. If $A$ is singular, the left side will not reach $I_n$ — the inverse does not exist.

### Properties of the inverse

<Equation explicacao="Five fundamental identities involving the inverse. The reversal of order in the product is analogous to the reversal in the transpose." latex="(A^{-1})^{-1} = A, \quad (AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1}, \quad (A^T)^{-1} = (A^{-1})^T, \quad (\alpha A)^{-1} = \tfrac{1}{\alpha}A^{-1}, \quad \det(A^{-1}) = \tfrac{1}{\det A}" />

> "If $A$ and $B$ are invertible matrices of the same order, then $AB$ is invertible and $(AB)^{-1} = B^{-1} A^{-1}$." — [Hefferon, Linear Algebra, cap. 3 §1](https://hefferon.net/linearalgebra/)

<Insight>

**Transpose and inverse commute.** The identity $(A^{-1})^T = (A^T)^{-1}$ says it does not matter: you can transpose and then invert, or invert and then transpose — you end up at the same place. This makes the notation $A^{-T}$ well-defined and widely used in statistics (least squares) and computer graphics.

</Insight>

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

**Transpose** is flipping the matrix on its side. What was in a horizontal row becomes a vertical column. It is like tilting a book sideways: the rows become columns.

**Inverse** is like **undoing** something. If you put on a jacket, the inverse is taking off the jacket. Put on and take off: back to the beginning. For matrices, multiplying $A$ by $A^{-1}$ gives the identity — which is like the "nothing" of matrix multiplication.

But be careful: not every matrix has an inverse. Some matrices "squash" information in a way that cannot be unsquashed — like crushing a sheet of paper: you can fold it again, but the creases stay.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

**Transpose**: swap rows for columns. A matrix with 2 rows and 3 columns becomes one with 3 rows and 2 columns:

$$\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \end{pmatrix}^T = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 4 \\ 2 & 5 \\ 3 & 6 \end{pmatrix}$$

**Inverse**: it is the matrix that "undoes" another. If $A$ scrambles a set of information, $A^{-1}$ unscrambles it. $A \cdot A^{-1} = I$ (the identity — the matrix that changes nothing, like multiplying by 1).

For a $2 \times 2$ matrix: swap the diagonal elements ($a$ and $d$ trade places), put negative signs on the others ($b$ and $c$ become $-b$ and $-c$), and divide everything by $ad - bc$.

If $ad - bc = 0$, there is no inverse. That matrix lost information and cannot be recovered.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old teen">

**Transpose** is simple: $(A^T)_{ij} = a_{ji}$. Row $i$, column $j$ of $A^T$ picks the element row $j$, column $i$ of $A$.

The most important — and counterintuitive — property is $(AB)^T = B^T A^T$. The order reverses. The same with inverse: $(AB)^{-1} = B^{-1} A^{-1}$.

**Symmetric matrix**: $A = A^T$. The diagonal is the axis of symmetry. Example: <Eq>{`\\begin{pmatrix} 3 & 7 \\\\ 7 & 5 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>.

**2×2 inverse**: given <Eq>{`A = \\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\\\ c & d \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq> with $\det A = ad - bc \neq 0$:

$$A^{-1} = \frac{1}{ad - bc}\begin{pmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{pmatrix}$$

**Example**: <Eq>{`A = \\begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \\\\ 5 & 2 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>. $\det A = 6 - 5 = 1$. <Eq>{`A^{-1} = \\begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\\\ -5 & 3 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>.

Verification: <Eq>{`\\begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \\\\ 5 & 2 \\end{pmatrix}\\begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\\\ -5 & 3 \\end{pmatrix} = \\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\\\ 0 & 1 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>. Correct.

**Immediate application**: system $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ has solution $\mathbf{x} = A^{-1}\mathbf{b}$ — as long as $A$ is invertible.

<Insight>

**Why does order reverse in $(AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1}$?** Think of getting dressed: $A$ = socks, $B$ = shoes. To undo, you first remove the shoes ($B^{-1}$) and then the socks ($A^{-1}$). Reversing the order of composed actions always requires reversing the order to undo them.

</Insight>

</Porta>

### Numerical computation of the inverse — methods

**1. Closed form via adjugate**: $A^{-1} = \frac{1}{\det A} \text{adj}(A)$. Cost $O(n!)$ — pedagogical only for $n \leq 3$.

**2. Gauss-Jordan on $[A \mid I]$** — cost $O(n^3)$, standard for hand and pedagogical work.

**3. LU factorization**: $A = LU$ — solve $L\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{e}_j$ (forward substitution) and then $U\mathbf{x}_j = \mathbf{y}$ (back substitution) for each column $j$ of $I$. Total cost $O(n^3)$, but reuses the factorization for multiple systems.

**Computational golden rule**: `np.linalg.solve(A, b)` is preferable to `np.linalg.inv(A) @ b` — numerically more stable and faster.

### Conditioning

The **condition number** $\kappa(A) = \|A\| \cdot \|A^{-1}\|$ measures solution sensitivity to perturbations in $\mathbf{b}$:

$$\frac{\|\delta \mathbf{x}\|}{\|\mathbf{x}\|} \leq \kappa(A) \cdot \frac{\|\delta \mathbf{b}\|}{\|\mathbf{b}\|}$$

If $\kappa(A) = 10^k$, approximately $k$ significant digits are lost. Poorly conditioned matrix: $\kappa \gg 1$.

### Cross-domain applications

- **Cryptography (Hill cipher)**: encrypt $\mathbf{c} = A\mathbf{m} \pmod{26}$; decrypt $\mathbf{m} = A^{-1}\mathbf{c} \pmod{26}$.
- **Computer graphics**: View-Projection transformation; moving the camera is equivalent to applying the inverse to the objects.
- **Economics (Leontief)**: total production $\mathbf{x} = (I - L)^{-1}\mathbf{d}$ for final demand $\mathbf{d}$.
- **Statistics**: normal equation for least squares $\hat{\beta} = (X^T X)^{-1} X^T \mathbf{y}$.

### Proof: $(AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1}$

$(AB)(B^{-1}A^{-1}) = A(BB^{-1})A^{-1} = AIA^{-1} = AA^{-1} = I$. By uniqueness of the inverse, we are done. $\square$

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

The class of invertible matrices $GL_n(\mathbb{R}) = \{A \in M_n(\mathbb{R}) : \det A \neq 0\}$ forms the **general linear group** — a central object in Lie theory. Every continuous group of matrices is a closed subgroup of $GL_n(\mathbb{R})$.

### Decompositions and inverse

- **LU**: $A = LU \Rightarrow A^{-1} = U^{-1}L^{-1}$. Triangular matrices inverted by back-substitution.
- **QR**: $A = QR \Rightarrow A^{-1} = R^{-1}Q^T$ (stable in least squares).
- **SVD**: $A = U\Sigma V^T \Rightarrow A^{-1} = V\Sigma^{-1}U^T$ ($A$ square nonsingular).
- **Cholesky** ($A$ SPD): $A = LL^T \Rightarrow A^{-1} = L^{-T}L^{-1}$, twice as fast as LU.

### Sherman-Morrison-Woodbury

Rank-1 inverse update (Sherman-Morrison):

$$(A + \mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^T)^{-1} = A^{-1} - \frac{A^{-1}\mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^T A^{-1}}{1 + \mathbf{v}^T A^{-1}\mathbf{u}}$$

Critical in RLS filters, online learning, sequential optimization.

### Moore-Penrose pseudo-inverse

For any $A \in M_{m \times n}$ (rectangular or singular): $A = U\Sigma V^T \Rightarrow A^+ = V\Sigma^+ U^T$ where $\Sigma^+$ inverts nonzero singular values. Minimum-norm solution: $\mathbf{x}^* = A^+\mathbf{b}$.

### Markowitz — quantitative finance

Optimal weight of minimum-variance portfolio:

$$\mathbf{w}^* = \frac{\Sigma^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\mu} - r_f \mathbf{1})}{\mathbf{1}^T \Sigma^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\mu} - r_f \mathbf{1})}$$

Estimation of $\Sigma^{-1}$ in high dimension is poorly conditioned — robust estimators (graphical lasso, Ledoit-Wolf shrinkage) are necessary in production.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practical application">

### Golden rule

**Never invert a matrix if you can avoid it.** `np.linalg.solve(A, b)` is faster and more stable than `np.linalg.inv(A) @ b`. Computing $A^{-1}$ explicitly is worthwhile only when you will reuse it for many vectors $\mathbf{b}$.

### NumPy

```python
import numpy as np

A = np.array([[3., 1.], [5., 2.]])
b = np.array([7., 11.])

# Transpose (no copy — just a view of the array)
print(A.T)

# Solve Ax = b (preferred over inverse)
x = np.linalg.solve(A, b)

# Explicit inverse (use only when needed)
A_inv = np.linalg.inv(A)

# Conditioning — if > 1e10, beware numerical error
print(np.linalg.cond(A))

# Pseudo-inverse for singular or rectangular matrices
A_pinv = np.linalg.pinv(A)
```

### SymPy (exact form)

```python
import sympy as sp
A = sp.Matrix([[3, 1], [5, 2]])
print(A.T)
print(A.inv())   # exact form — fractions, not floats
print(A.det())
```

### When you really need $A^{-1}$

1. Apply the same inverse transformation to many vectors — compute once, reuse.
2. Symbolic derivation of closed-form formulas (least squares, Kalman filter).
3. Numerical verification of algebraic identities.

In all other cases: use `solve`, LU, or Cholesky.

### Brazilian context: SISU and seat allocation

SISU uses a linear system $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ to calculate cutoffs: $A$ encodes the relationship between candidate scores, seats per course, and bonuses (quotas, income). The "inverse" of that transformation recovers the optimal distribution of seats — although in production the MEC uses iteration, not direct inversion.

## Worked examples

<Exemplo titulo="Computing the transpose of a 2×3 matrix" numero="1">

**Problem:** Given <Eq>{`A = \\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -2 & 0 \\\\ 3 & 4 & -1 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>, compute $A^T$ and verify that $(A^T)^T = A$.

**Strategy:** Apply $(A^T)_{ij} = a_{ji}$: each row of $A$ becomes a column of $A^T$.

**Solution:**

$A$ has format $2 \times 3$, so $A^T$ will have format $3 \times 2$.

1. Row 1 of $A$ = $(1, -2, 0)$ becomes column 1 of $A^T$.
2. Row 2 of $A$ = $(3, 4, -1)$ becomes column 2 of $A^T$.

$$A^T = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ -2 & 4 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$$

**Verification of $(A^T)^T = A$:** Transposing $A^T$, the rows $(1,3)$, $(-2,4)$, $(0,-1)$ become columns, recovering exactly $A$. The involution is confirmed.

**Source.** [Beezer, A First Course in Linear Algebra, §MO](http://linear.pugetsound.edu/html/section-MO.html) — GNU FDL license.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Computing the 2×2 inverse by direct formula" numero="2">

**Problem:** Compute $A^{-1}$ for <Eq>{`A = \\begin{pmatrix} 4 & 7 \\\\ 2 & 6 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq> and verify $AA^{-1} = I$.

**Strategy:** Apply the formula <Eq>{`A^{-1} = \\frac{1}{\\det A}\\begin{pmatrix} d & -b \\\\ -c & a \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>.

**Solution:**

**Step 1 — Compute the determinant:**
$$\det A = 4 \cdot 6 - 7 \cdot 2 = 24 - 14 = 10$$

Since $\det A = 10 \neq 0$, the inverse exists.

**Step 2 — Apply the formula:**
$$A^{-1} = \frac{1}{10}\begin{pmatrix} 6 & -7 \\ -2 & 4 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.6 & -0.7 \\ -0.2 & 0.4 \end{pmatrix}$$

**Step 3 — Verify $AA^{-1} = I$:**
$$\begin{pmatrix} 4 & 7 \\ 2 & 6 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 0.6 & -0.7 \\ -0.2 & 0.4 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2.4 - 1.4 & -2.8 + 2.8 \\ 1.2 - 1.2 & -1.4 + 2.4 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$$

Verified.

**Source.** [OpenStax College Algebra 2e, §9.7](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/9-7-solving-systems-with-inverses) — CC-BY 4.0 license.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="3×3 inverse by Gauss-Jordan method" numero="3">

**Problem:** Compute $A^{-1}$ for <Eq>{`A = \\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \\\\ 0 & 1 & 3 \\\\ 2 & 3 & 0 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq> using Gauss-Jordan.

**Strategy:** Form $[A \mid I_3]$ and apply elementary row operations until you obtain $[I_3 \mid A^{-1}]$.

**Solution:**

Augmented matrix initially:
<Eq>{`\\left[\\begin{array}{ccc|ccc} 1 & 2 & 1 & 1 & 0 & 0 \\\\ 0 & 1 & 3 & 0 & 1 & 0 \\\\ 2 & 3 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 \\end{array}\\right]`}</Eq>

**R3 ← R3 − 2·R1:**
<Eq>{`\\left[\\begin{array}{ccc|ccc} 1 & 2 & 1 & 1 & 0 & 0 \\\\ 0 & 1 & 3 & 0 & 1 & 0 \\\\ 0 & -1 & -2 & -2 & 0 & 1 \\end{array}\\right]`}</Eq>

**R3 ← R3 + R2:**
<Eq>{`\\left[\\begin{array}{ccc|ccc} 1 & 2 & 1 & 1 & 0 & 0 \\\\ 0 & 1 & 3 & 0 & 1 & 0 \\\\ 0 & 0 & 1 & -2 & 1 & 1 \\end{array}\\right]`}</Eq>

**R2 ← R2 − 3·R3; R1 ← R1 − R3:**
<Eq>{`\\left[\\begin{array}{ccc|ccc} 1 & 2 & 0 & 3 & -1 & -1 \\\\ 0 & 1 & 0 & 6 & -2 & -3 \\\\ 0 & 0 & 1 & -2 & 1 & 1 \\end{array}\\right]`}</Eq>

**R1 ← R1 − 2·R2:**
<Eq>{`\\left[\\begin{array}{ccc|ccc} 1 & 0 & 0 & -9 & 3 & 5 \\\\ 0 & 1 & 0 & 6 & -2 & -3 \\\\ 0 & 0 & 1 & -2 & 1 & 1 \\end{array}\\right]`}</Eq>

Therefore:
$$A^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} -9 & 3 & 5 \\ 6 & -2 & -3 \\ -2 & 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$$

**Verification (first column of $AA^{-1}$):**
$$\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 3 \\ 2 & 3 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} -9 \\ 6 \\ -2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -9+12-2 \\ 0+6-6 \\ -18+18+0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}\ \checkmark$$

**Source.** [Beezer, A First Course in Linear Algebra, §MISLE](http://linear.pugetsound.edu/html/section-MISLE.html) — GNU FDL license.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="System solution via inverse (modeling)" numero="4">

**Problem:** A security company uses a 2×2 Hill cipher with key <Eq>{`K = \\begin{pmatrix} 3 & 2 \\\\ 5 & 4 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>. The received ciphertext vector is <Eq>{`\\mathbf{c} = \\begin{pmatrix} 17 \\\\ 31 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>. Recover the original message $\mathbf{m}$ by solving $K\mathbf{m} = \mathbf{c}$.

**Strategy:** Compute $K^{-1}$ and multiply $\mathbf{m} = K^{-1}\mathbf{c}$.

**Solution:**

**Step 1 — Determinant:**
$$\det K = 3 \cdot 4 - 2 \cdot 5 = 12 - 10 = 2$$

**Step 2 — Inverse:**
$$K^{-1} = \frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix} 4 & -2 \\ -5 & 3 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -5/2 & 3/2 \end{pmatrix}$$

**Step 3 — Recover $\mathbf{m}$:**
$$\mathbf{m} = K^{-1}\mathbf{c} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -5/2 & 3/2 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 17 \\ 31 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 34 - 31 \\ -85/2 + 93/2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix}$$

The original message is $\mathbf{m} = (3, 4)^T$.

**Verification:** <Eq>{`K\\mathbf{m} = \\begin{pmatrix} 3 & 2 \\\\ 5 & 4 \\end{pmatrix}\\begin{pmatrix} 3 \\\\ 4 \\end{pmatrix} = \\begin{pmatrix} 9+8 \\\\ 15+16 \\end{pmatrix} = \\begin{pmatrix} 17 \\\\ 31 \\end{pmatrix} = \\mathbf{c}`}</Eq>. Correct.

**Source.** [OpenStax College Algebra 2e, §9.7](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/9-7-solving-systems-with-inverses) — CC-BY 4.0 license.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Proof: $(A^{-1})^T = (A^T)^{-1}$" numero="5">

**Problem:** Prove that for every invertible matrix $A$, we have $(A^{-1})^T = (A^T)^{-1}$.

**Strategy:** Start from the definition $AA^{-1} = I$, transpose both sides, and use $(AB)^T = B^T A^T$.

**Solution:**

**Step 1.** From $AA^{-1} = I$, take the transpose of both sides:
$$(AA^{-1})^T = I^T$$

**Step 2.** Apply $(AB)^T = B^T A^T$ and use $I^T = I$:
$$(A^{-1})^T A^T = I$$

**Step 3.** The equation $(A^{-1})^T \cdot A^T = I$ says that $(A^{-1})^T$ is the left inverse of $A^T$. By uniqueness of the inverse:
$$(A^{-1})^T = (A^T)^{-1}\ \square$$

**Verification with example:** Take <Eq>{`A = \\begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \\\\ 5 & 2 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>, <Eq>{`A^{-1} = \\begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\\\ -5 & 3 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq> (det = 1). Then <Eq>{`(A^{-1})^T = \\begin{pmatrix} 2 & -5 \\\\ -1 & 3 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>. And <Eq>{`A^T = \\begin{pmatrix} 3 & 5 \\\\ 1 & 2 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>, <Eq>{`(A^T)^{-1} = \\frac{1}{1}\\begin{pmatrix} 2 & -5 \\\\ -1 & 3 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>. Equal.

**Source.** [Beezer, A First Course in Linear Algebra, §MI](http://linear.pugetsound.edu/html/section-MI.html) — GNU FDL license.

</Exemplo>
