### The four pillars

Year 1 built four pillars that form the substrate of calculus, linear algebra, and probability:

<Definicao titulo="Pillar 1 — Functions and analysis (Trim 1)">

- Domain, range, composition, and inverse: $f^{-1} \circ f = \mathrm{id}_D$.
- Linear, quadratic, exponential $a^x$, and logarithmic $\log_a x$ functions.
- **Average rate of change:**

<Equation numero="TVM" explicacao="Ratio of change of f between two points. For linear functions it is constant; for nonlinear functions it varies with the interval — and in the limit as b → a it becomes the derivative." latex="\text{TVM}_{[a,b]} = \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}" />

- Intuitive limit of a sequence: $\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty} a_n = L$.

> "The average rate of change of a function $f$ over an interval $[a, b]$ is $\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}$. We can think of this as the slope of the line connecting the two points $(a, f(a))$ and $(b, f(b))$." — [Active Calculus §1.3](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-1-3-derivative-pt.html)

</Definicao>

<Definicao titulo="Pillar 2 — Trigonometry and sequences (Trim 2)">

- Trigonometric circle, fundamental identity: $\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1$.
- Law of sines: $\dfrac{a}{\sin A} = \dfrac{b}{\sin B} = \dfrac{c}{\sin C}$.
- Law of cosines: $c^2 = a^2 + b^2 - 2ab\cos C$.
- Arithmetic progression (AP) and geometric progression (GP):

<Equation numero="PA" explicacao="Sum of the first n terms of an AP with first term a₁ and common ratio r." latex="S_n = \frac{n(a_1 + a_n)}{2} = \frac{n(2a_1 + (n-1)r)}{2}" />

<Equation numero="PG" explicacao="Sum of the first n terms of a GP with ratio q ≠ 1. When |q| < 1 and n → ∞, the sum converges to a₁/(1-q)." latex="S_n = a_1\,\frac{q^n - 1}{q - 1}" />

</Definicao>

<Definicao titulo="Pillar 3 — Analytic geometry and vectors (Trim 3)">

- Distance between points: $d = \sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2 + (y_2-y_1)^2}$.
- Equation of a line: $y = mx + b$ or general form $ax + by + c = 0$.
- Conics: circle, ellipse $\frac{x^2}{a^2}+\frac{y^2}{b^2}=1$, hyperbola, parabola.
- Dot product and angle between vectors:

<Equation numero="PE" explicacao="The dot product is zero if and only if the vectors are perpendicular. The angle θ is obtained by dividing the dot product by the product of the magnitudes." latex="\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = u_x v_x + u_y v_y = |\mathbf{u}||\mathbf{v}|\cos\theta" />

> "The dot product is distributive over addition and satisfies $\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} = |\mathbf{u}||\mathbf{v}|\cos\theta$ where $\theta$ is the angle between $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$." — [OpenStax Algebra and Trigonometry 2e §10.4](https://openstax.org/books/algebra-and-trigonometry-2e/pages/10-4-vectors)

</Definicao>

<Definicao titulo="Pillar 4 — Linear algebra, combinatorics, and probability (Trim 4)">

- Matrices, determinant (rule of Sarrus or Laplace expansion), inverse $A \cdot A^{-1} = I$.
- Systems $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$: Gaussian elimination, Cramer's rule.
- Structured counting:

<Equation numero="Comb" explicacao="Number of subsets of size p from a set of n elements. Order does not matter. Permutation A_n^p = n!/(n-p)! counts when order matters." latex="\binom{n}{p} = \frac{n!}{p!\,(n-p)!}" />

- Conditional probability and Bayes's theorem:

<Equation numero="Bayes" explicacao="Bayes inverts the conditional: given that B occurred, what is the probability of A? The denominator is the total probability of B, calculated by the law of total probability." latex="P(A|B) = \frac{P(B|A)\,P(A)}{P(B|A)\,P(A) + P(B|\bar{A})\,P(\bar{A})}" />

> "Bayes' theorem gives a mathematical rule for revising an estimate or forecast in light of experience and new information." — [OpenIntro Statistics §3.2](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/)

</Definicao>

### The unifying thread

<Insight>

The average rate of change $\text{TVM} = \Delta f / \Delta x$ runs through all of Year 1 in different guises: slope of a line (Trim 3), angular velocity $\omega = \Delta\theta/\Delta t$ (Trim 2), logarithmic growth in compound interest (Trim 1). In Year 2, the question changes: what happens when $\Delta x \to 0$? The answer is the derivative — the central object of differential calculus.

</Insight>

**Structured recap of Year 1.**

**Trim 1 — Functions and change**

Functions $f: D \to \mathbb{R}$, composition $(f \circ g)(x) = f(g(x))$, inverse $f^{-1}$. Exponential $f(x) = a^x$ and logarithm $\log_a x$. Average rate of change $\text{TVM} = \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}$: the "average slope" of any function.

**Trim 2 — Trigonometry and sequences**

Identities: $\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1$; laws of sines and cosines for any triangle. Arithmetic progressions (AP) and geometric progressions (GP). Sum of infinite GP: $S = \frac{a_1}{1-q}$ if $|q| < 1$.

**Trim 3 — Analytic geometry and vectors**

Distance, line, relative position, conics. Vectors: $\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = u_x v_x + u_y v_y$; angle via $\cos\theta = \frac{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}}{|\mathbf{u}||\mathbf{v}|}$. Systems $2 \times 2$ and $3 \times 3$.

**Trim 4 — Linear algebra, combinatorics, probability**

Matrices, determinant, inverse. Gauss and Cramer. Counting principle, $n!$, $A_n^p$, $\binom{n}{p}$. Conditional probability $P(A|B)$ and Bayes's theorem.

**Average time per exercise in this workshop:** 10 to 15 minutes. Total estimate: 6 to 10 hours.

In Year 2, the question becomes: what happens when $b \to a$? That is the derivative.

### Where Year 1 reappears in advanced work

**Matrices and linear algebra:** Foundation for spectral analysis ($A = Q\Lambda Q^T$), PCA, SVD decomposition. In quantitative finance, the covariance matrix $\Sigma$ is the heart of Markowitz. In ML, backpropagation is the chain rule on matrices.

**Combinatorics and counting:** Algorithmic complexity ($O(n!)$, $O(\binom{n}{k})$). Combinatorial structures in cryptography (RSA implicitly uses factorials). Expansion of $(a+b)^n$ is the foundation of the binomial model in Black-Scholes (Lesson 119).

**Rate of change:** The $\text{TVM}$ appearing in this lesson is the foundation of the derivative, which is the foundation of the differential equation, which is the language of physics and engineering.

### Common errors in this workshop

- Confusing $A_n^p$ (permutation) with $\binom{n}{p}$ (combination): permutation cares about order, combination does not.
- Forgetting that $\det A$ changes sign after swapping rows.
- Using $(AB)^T = A^T B^T$ incorrectly: the right formula is $(AB)^T = B^T A^T$.
- Swapping $P(A|B)$ with $P(B|A)$ — the Bayes problem hinges on exactly this asymmetry.
- Domain of $\log_a f(x)$: requires $f(x) > 0$, not $f(x) \geq 0$.
- Sign error in quadratic factorization: always check roots by substitution.

## Worked examples

<Exemplo titulo="Composite function + rate of change (Trim 1)" numero="1">

**Problem:** Let $f(x) = x^2 - 1$ and $g(x) = 2x + 3$. (a) Calculate $(f \circ g)(x)$. (b) Calculate $\text{TVM}_{[0,2]}$ of $h = f \circ g$.

**Strategy:** Composition first; then apply the definition of TVM. Two concepts from Trim 1 chained together.

**Solution:**

1. **Composition:** $(f \circ g)(x) = f(g(x)) = f(2x+3) = (2x+3)^2 - 1 = 4x^2 + 12x + 8$.

2. **TVM on interval $[0, 2]$:**

$$\text{TVM}_{[0,2]} = \frac{h(2) - h(0)}{2 - 0} = \frac{(4 \cdot 4 + 24 + 8) - (0 + 0 + 8)}{2} = \frac{48 - 8}{2} = 20.$$

**Verification:** $h(0) = 4(0) + 12(0) + 8 = 8$. $h(2) = 4(4) + 12(2) + 8 = 16 + 24 + 8 = 48$. $\frac{48-8}{2} = 20$. Correct.

**Source.** [Stitz–Zeager Precalculus §4.1](https://www.stitz-zeager.com/szprecalc07042013.pdf) — CC-BY-NC-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Law of cosines + trigonometric identity (Trim 2)" numero="2">

**Problem:** In a triangle with $a = 7$, $b = 5$, and $C = 120°$, determine side $c$ and $\cos A$.

**Strategy:** Law of cosines for $c$; then second application for $\cos A$. Chains two uses of the same law.

**Solution:**

1. **Calculate $c$:**

$$c^2 = a^2 + b^2 - 2ab\cos C = 49 + 25 - 2(7)(5)\cos(120°).$$

$$\cos(120°) = -\frac{1}{2}, \text{ so } c^2 = 74 - 70 \cdot \left(-\frac{1}{2}\right) = 74 + 35 = 109.$$

$$c = \sqrt{109} \approx 10.44.$$

2. **Calculate $\cos A$** (law of cosines: $a^2 = b^2 + c^2 - 2bc\cos A$):

$$\cos A = \frac{b^2 + c^2 - a^2}{2bc} = \frac{25 + 109 - 49}{2 \cdot 5 \cdot \sqrt{109}} = \frac{85}{10\sqrt{109}}.$$

**Verification:** $85 / (10\sqrt{109}) \approx 85/104.4 \approx 0.814$, so $A \approx 35.4°$. Angle smaller than $C = 120°$ — consistent with opposite side $a = 7 < c = \sqrt{109}$.

**Source.** [OpenStax Algebra and Trigonometry 2e §10.2](https://openstax.org/books/algebra-and-trigonometry-2e/pages/10-2-non-right-triangles-law-of-cosines) — CC-BY 4.0.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Conic + linear system (Trim 3 crossed with Trim 4)" numero="3">

**Problem:** Find the intersection points of the ellipse $\dfrac{x^2}{25} + \dfrac{y^2}{9} = 1$ with the line $y = x - 1$.

**Strategy:** Substitute the line into the conic equation to get a quadratic equation. Conic + line system — classical analytic geometry problem.

**Solution:**

1. **Substitute $y = x - 1$:**

$$\frac{x^2}{25} + \frac{(x-1)^2}{9} = 1.$$

2. **LCM = 225**, multiply everything by 225:

$$9x^2 + 25(x-1)^2 = 225.$$

$$9x^2 + 25x^2 - 50x + 25 = 225 \implies 34x^2 - 50x - 200 = 0 \implies 17x^2 - 25x - 100 = 0.$$

3. **Solve via quadratic formula:**

$$\Delta = 625 + 4 \cdot 17 \cdot 100 = 625 + 6800 = 7425.$$

$$x = \frac{25 \pm \sqrt{7425}}{34} = \frac{25 \pm 15\sqrt{33}}{34}.$$

4. **Coordinates $y$:** $y = x - 1$ in each case.

**Verification:** the two points satisfy both equations (verify numerically by substituting $x \approx 3.28$ and $x \approx -1.81$).

**Source.** [OpenStax Algebra and Trigonometry 2e §12.3](https://openstax.org/books/algebra-and-trigonometry-2e/pages/12-3-the-ellipse) — CC-BY 4.0.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Determinant + conditional probability (Trim 4)" numero="4">

**Problem:** The matrix <Eq>{`A = \\begin{pmatrix} k & 2 \\\\ 3 & k-1 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq> has determinant zero when $k = ?$. Next: in an urn with 4 red balls and 6 blue balls, one ball is drawn without replacement. What is the probability the second is blue, given the first was red?

**Strategy:** Two Trim 4 problems in sequence — determinant and conditional probability.

**Solution:**

**Part 1 — Determinant:**

$$\det A = k(k-1) - 6 = k^2 - k - 6 = (k-3)(k+2) = 0.$$

So $k = 3$ or $k = -2$.

**Part 2 — Conditional probability:**

Event $B_2$: second ball is blue. Event $V_1$: first ball is red.

$$P(B_2 | V_1) = \frac{\text{blue remaining}}{\text{total remaining}} = \frac{6}{9} = \frac{2}{3}.$$

**Verification:** After drawing one red ball, $10 - 1 = 9$ balls remain, of which 6 are blue. Correct.

**Source.** [OpenStax College Algebra 2e §11.4](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/11-4-probability) and [§11.5](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/11-5-counting-principles) — CC-BY 4.0.

</Ejemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Integrative problem (Trim 1 + 2 + 3 + 4)" numero="5">

**Problem:** A company models its monthly profit (in thousands of US dollars) by $L(t) = 10 + 3t - t^2/10$, where $t$ is the month ($t = 1, 2, \ldots, 12$). (a) Calculate TVM of $L$ between $t = 2$ and $t = 8$. (b) The vector $\mathbf{v} = (L(3),\, L(6))$ points to which quadrant? (c) How many marketing strategies are possible if the company chooses 2 from 5 available channels (order irrelevant)?

**Strategy:** Part (a) uses Trim 1 (TVM). Part (b) uses Trim 3 (vectors in plane). Part (c) uses Trim 4 (combinations). Explicitly integrative problem.

**Solution:**

**Part (a) — TVM:**

$L(2) = 10 + 6 - 0.4 = 15.6$. $L(8) = 10 + 24 - 6.4 = 27.6$.

$$\text{TVM}_{[2,8]} = \frac{27.6 - 15.6}{8 - 2} = \frac{12}{6} = 2.$$

Profit grows 2 thousand US dollars per month on average during that period.

**Part (b) — Vector:**

$L(3) = 10 + 9 - 0.9 = 18.1$ and $L(6) = 10 + 18 - 3.6 = 24.4$.

$\mathbf{v} = (18.1;\, 24.4)$. Both coordinates positive: **first quadrant**.

**Part (c) — Combinations:**

$$\binom{5}{2} = \frac{5!}{2!\,3!} = 10 \text{ possible strategies}.$$

**Source.** [OpenStax College Algebra 2e §11.3](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/11-3-systems-of-nonlinear-equations-and-inequalities-two-variables) and [§11.5](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/11-5-counting-principles) — CC-BY 4.0.

</Exemplo>
