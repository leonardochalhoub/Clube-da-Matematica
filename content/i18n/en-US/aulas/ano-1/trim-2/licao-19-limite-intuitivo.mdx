<Porta nivel="formal" titulo="Concept and properties">

### The central question

> "Given a sequence $(a_n)$, to what value (if any) do the terms approach as $n \to \infty$?"

When this value exists, we say the sequence **converges** and write $\lim_{n \to \infty} a_n = L$.

<Definicao titulo="Limit of sequence (intuitive version)">

We say $\lim_{n \to \infty} a_n = L$ if the terms $a_n$ become **arbitrarily close to $L$** when $n$ is sufficiently large.

The rigorous formalization (Lesson 41):

$$\forall \varepsilon > 0,\ \exists N \in \mathbb{N} :\ n \geq N \Rightarrow |a_n - L| < \varepsilon$$

For now: trust in "approaches."

</Definicao>

> "A sequence $\{a_n\}$ converges to a real number $L$ if, and only if, for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists an integer $N$ such that if $n > N$, then $|a_n - L| < \varepsilon$." — [OpenStax Calculus Volume 2, §5.1](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-2/pages/5-1-sequences)

### Notable limits

| Sequence | Limit | Intuitive justification |
|---|---|---|
| $1/n$ | $0$ | terms decrease indefinitely |
| $1/n^k$ with $k > 0$ | $0$ | same, faster |
| $q^n$ with $\|q\| < 1$ | $0$ | decreasing GP (Lesson 18) |
| $q^n$ with $\|q\| > 1$ | does not exist | GP explodes |
| $(1 + 1/n)^n$ | $e \approx 2.71828$ | Euler's number |
| $\sqrt[n]{n}$ | $1$ | via logarithm |
| $n^k / a^n$ with $a > 1$ | $0$ | exponential beats polynomial |
| $n! / n^n$ | $0$ | $n^n$ explodes faster than factorial |

### Operations with limits

If $\lim a_n = A$ and $\lim b_n = B$ (both finite):

<Equation numero="1"
  explicacao="The limit of a sum is the sum of the limits — holds for all cases where both limits exist and are finite."
  latex="\lim(a_n \pm b_n) = A \pm B,\quad \lim(a_n \cdot b_n) = A \cdot B,\quad \lim\frac{a_n}{b_n} = \frac{A}{B}\ (B \neq 0)" />

### Sequences that do NOT converge

- **Diverge to $\pm\infty$**: $a_n = n$, $a_n = 2^n$.
- **Oscillate without limit**: $a_n = (-1)^n$ — alternates between $1$ and $-1$.
- **Bounded without limit**: $a_n = \sin n$ — bounded but does not converge.

### Squeeze theorem

<Teorema titulo="Squeeze theorem">

If there exist sequences $(a_n), (b_n), (c_n)$ such that

$$a_n \leq b_n \leq c_n \quad \text{for all } n \geq N_0,$$

and $\lim_{n \to \infty} a_n = \lim_{n \to \infty} c_n = L$, then $\lim_{n \to \infty} b_n = L$.

</Teorema>

> "Squeeze Theorem: if $a_n \leq b_n \leq c_n$ for all $n \geq N$ and $\lim_{n\to\infty} a_n = \lim_{n\to\infty} c_n = L$, then $\lim_{n\to\infty} b_n = L$." — [Lebl, Basic Analysis I, §2.1](https://www.jirka.org/ra/)

### Monotone bounded criterion

<Teorema titulo="Monotone bounded converges (Bolzano-Weierstrass)">

Every monotone and bounded sequence in $\mathbb{R}$ converges.

**Practical corollary**: to show that $(1 + 1/n)^n$ has a limit, it suffices to show that it is increasing and bounded above by $3$ (via binomial). The existence of the limit is guaranteed without calculating the exact value.

</Teorema>

<Insight>

**Why are sequence limits and function limits the same concept?** Because a sequence is a function $f: \mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{R}$. When we study $\lim_{x \to \infty} f(x)$ in Lesson 41, it will be the natural extension to domain $\mathbb{R}$. The $\varepsilon$-$N$ of sequences becomes $\varepsilon$-$\delta$ for functions. Every derivative $f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0}(f(a+h)-f(a))/h$ is a limit of a sequence of ratios.

</Insight>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

Imagine a very long hallway. There is a door way at the end. You take a big step, then a smaller step, then an even smaller step, then almost like tiptoeing...

Each step smaller and smaller. But you are **getting** to the door. Really close.

The "door" is the **limit**. The little steps are the terms of the sequence. You approach the limit, without necessarily touching it.

Example: $1, 1/2, 1/3, 1/4, 1/5, \ldots$

With each step you get closer to **zero**. The door is zero.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

The sequence $1/n$ has terms $1, 1/2, 1/3, 1/4, 1/5, \ldots, 1/100, \ldots, 1/1\,000\,000, \ldots$

**Where is it going?** To **zero**. It never hits exactly zero, but gets as close as you want.

That "where is it going" is the **limit**. We write it like this:

$$\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{1}{n} = 0$$

Read: "limit, as $n$ tends to infinity, of $1/n$, equals zero."

**Other examples:**
- $\lim (1/2)^n = 0$ — GP halving at each step.
- $\lim n = \infty$ — only grows, no fixed destination.
- $\lim (-1)^n$ does not exist — jumps between $1$ and $-1$ forever, goes nowhere.
- $\lim (1 + 1/n)^n = e \approx 2.71828$ — the famous Euler number.

**Why does this matter?** Because limit is the foundation of **all calculus** you will study in the coming years. Every derivative and every integral is a hidden limit.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old teen">

**What is a sequence limit?** The idea is simple: a sequence $(a_n)$ has limit $L$ if the terms become as close to $L$ as you want, for $n$ large enough.

$$\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{1}{n} = 0, \quad \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{3n+5}{n+2} = 3, \quad \lim_{n \to \infty} \left(1 + \frac{1}{n}\right)^n = e$$

**Quick tricks for $\lim P(n)/Q(n)$ with $P, Q$ polynomials:**

| If | Then |
|---|---|
| $\deg P = \deg Q$ | ratio of leading coefficients |
| $\deg P > \deg Q$ | $\pm \infty$ (diverges) |
| $\deg P < \deg Q$ | $0$ |

**Why does it matter?** This lesson is **deliberately** intuitive. The rigorous formalization ($\varepsilon$-$N$) comes in Trim 5, Lesson 41. For now: trust in "approaches" and practice with the exercises.

But note now: **every derivative is a limit**. When you compute $f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0}(f(x+h)-f(x))/h$ in Trim 5, it will be a sequence limit — exactly the concept of this lesson.

**Practical criterion for GP:** $\lim q^n = 0$ if $|q| < 1$; $\lim q^n = +\infty$ if $q > 1$; does not exist if $q \leq -1$.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

Limit is the **cornerstone concept** of analysis. Sequences converge or not; when they converge, the limit is unique (uniqueness — Exercise 19.33).

### Fundamental theorems

- **Squeeze**: if $a_n \leq b_n \leq c_n$ and $\lim a_n = \lim c_n = L$, then $\lim b_n = L$.
- **Monotone bounded converges**: every monotone and bounded sequence in $\mathbb{R}$ has a limit (completeness of $\mathbb{R}$).
- **Cauchy $\Rightarrow$ converges**: Cauchy sequence in $\mathbb{R}$ always converges (equivalent completeness).

### Asymptotic notation (computer science)

- $a_n = O(b_n)$: $|a_n/b_n|$ bounded — same order of growth.
- $a_n = o(b_n)$: $a_n/b_n \to 0$ — $a_n$ is "much smaller" than $b_n$.
- $a_n \sim b_n$: $a_n/b_n \to 1$ — asymptotically equivalent.

Examples: $n \ln n = o(n^2)$; $\ln n = o(n^{0.01})$; $\binom{2n}{n} \sim 4^n / \sqrt{\pi n}$ (Stirling).

### The number $e$

$$e = \lim_{n \to \infty} \left(1 + \frac{1}{n}\right)^n = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{1}{k!} \approx 2.71828\ldots$$

Generalization: $\lim_{n \to \infty} (1 + r/n)^n = e^r$ — foundation of continuous interest, exponential decay, the ODE $\dot y = ky$.

### Engineering applications

- **Stability of LTI systems**: pole $p$ with $|p| < 1 \Leftrightarrow$ impulse response $p^n \to 0 \Leftrightarrow$ system stable.
- **Newton-Raphson convergence**: iteration $x_{n+1} = x_n - f(x_n)/f'(x_n)$ converges **quadratically** near simple root.
- **MCMC** (Metropolis-Hastings): ergodic chain converges to stationary distribution.
- **SGD** (stochastic gradient): with $\eta_n \to 0$ appropriately, parameters converge to minimum (under convexity).

### Caution: indeterminate form $1^{\infty}$

$\lim (1 + 1/n)^n = e \neq 1^{\infty}$ — the form $1^{\infty}$ is **indeterminate**. Do not naively compute "base goes to 1 so limit is $1^{\infty} = 1$." Use $\lim (1 + r/n)^{nt} = e^{rt}$.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

Limit is the concept that **defines** $\mathbb{R}$. Classical constructions:

- **Cauchy**: $\mathbb{R}$ = equivalence classes of Cauchy sequences of rationals.
- **Dedekind**: $\mathbb{R}$ = cuts of $\mathbb{Q}$.

Both equivalent. The choice defines the **metric completion** of $\mathbb{Q}$ in the usual norm. Completeness is what guarantees every Cauchy sequence converges — property $\mathbb{Q}$ lacks (e.g., sequence of rationals approaching $\sqrt{2}$ is Cauchy but does not converge in $\mathbb{Q}$).

### Underlying topology

Convergence is **topological** phenomenon: $a_n \to L$ if every open neighborhood of $L$ contains all $a_n$ from some $N$ onward. In metric spaces, neighborhood = ball $B(L, \varepsilon)$.

### Generalizations

- **$\mathbb{R}^n$**: convergence by components (Euclidean norm).
- **Function spaces**: pointwise, uniform, $L^p$ convergence — distinct in general. $f_n(x) = x^n$ on $[0,1]$: converges pointwise to discontinuous function, not uniformly.
- **Net limits**: generalizes sequence to partially ordered sets (general topology).
- **Categorical limit** (cones): in category theory, universal structure unifying sequence limits, products, pullbacks, etc.

### In ML/DL and statistics

- **Law of large numbers**: $\bar X_n \to \mu$ — foundation of Monte Carlo, batch SGD.
- **CLT**: $\sqrt n (\bar X_n - \mu)/\sigma \to N(0,1)$ — justification for confidence intervals.
- **SGD convergence**: gradient decays as $O(1/\sqrt T)$ under convexity and Lipschitz. Proof uses Lyapunov sequence.
- **Catastrophic forgetting in deep networks**: gradients decay geometrically across layers — variance tends to zero (vanishing gradient problem).

```python
import sympy as sp
n = sp.symbols('n', integer=True, positive=True)
print(sp.limit((1 + 1/n)**n, n, sp.oo))    # E
print(sp.limit((3*n + 5)/(n + 2), n, sp.oo))  # 3
print(sp.limit(n**100 / 2**n, n, sp.oo))   # 0  (exp dominates)
print(sp.limit(sp.sin(n)/n, n, sp.oo))     # 0
```

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practical application — Brazil and engineering">

### Scenario 1: Continuous compounding (SELIC)

If SELIC is $r = 10.75\%$ annually and you compound $n$ times per year, the annual factor is $(1 + r/n)^n$. When $n \to \infty$ (continuous compounding):

$$\lim_{n \to \infty} \left(1 + \frac{0.1075}{n}\right)^n = e^{0.1075} \approx 1.1134$$

For US\$ 10,000 applied, at end of 1 year: US\$ 11,134. Compare with simple annual compounding: US\$ 11,075. The difference comes from Euler's limit.

### Scenario 2: Capacitor discharge (electronics)

A capacitor discharging through a resistor follows $V_n = V_0 \cdot (0.9)^n$ per period. Since $|0.9| < 1$:

$$\lim_{n \to \infty} V_n = V_0 \cdot 0 = 0 \text{ V}$$

The capacitor discharges completely — zero limit. If the ratio were $q = 1.1 > 1$, voltage would explode (circuit instability).

### Scenario 3: Convergence of iterative algorithm

Heron's method (Babylonian algorithm, 1st century BCE) for $\sqrt c$:

$$a_1 = 1, \quad a_{n+1} = \frac{a_n + c/a_n}{2}$$

For $c = 5$: $a_1 = 1$, $a_2 = 3$, $a_3 = 2.333$, $a_4 = 2.2381$, $a_5 \approx 2.23607$ — already 5 correct digits of $\sqrt{5} \approx 2.23607$. **Quadratic convergence**: number of correct digits doubles each iteration. Limit computed from fixed point: $L = (L + c/L)/2 \Rightarrow L^2 = c \Rightarrow L = \sqrt c$.

## Worked examples

<Exemplo titulo="Limit of 1/n — the founding example" numero="1">

**Problem:** Compute $\lim_{n \to \infty} 1/n$.

**Strategy:** Observe the sequence numerically. The terms decrease indefinitely — the intuitive limit is $0$. To confirm: given any target $\varepsilon > 0$, just take $n > 1/\varepsilon$ so that $1/n < \varepsilon$.

**Solution:**

1. **List some terms.** $1,\ 1/2,\ 1/3,\ 1/4,\ \ldots,\ 1/100,\ \ldots,\ 1/10^6,\ \ldots$
2. **Observe the trend.** For any $\varepsilon > 0$, just take $n > 1/\varepsilon$ so that $1/n < \varepsilon$.
3. **Conclude.** $\lim_{n \to \infty} 1/n = 0$.

**Verification:** $1/10^6 = 10^{-6}$, $1/10^{12} = 10^{-12}$. Approaches zero without ever touching it.

**Source.** [Active Calculus §8.1 (Sequences)](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-8-3-geometric.html) — license CC-BY-NC-SA. Canonical opening example of convergence concept.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Limit of ratio of polynomials" numero="2">

**Problem:** Compute $\lim_{n \to \infty} \dfrac{3n + 5}{n + 2}$.

**Strategy:** Divide numerator and denominator by $n$ (the highest power present). This isolates the terms that tend to zero.

**Solution:**

1. **Divide everything by $n$.** $\dfrac{3n + 5}{n + 2} = \dfrac{3 + 5/n}{1 + 2/n}$.
2. **Take the limit.** Since $5/n \to 0$ and $2/n \to 0$: $\dfrac{3 + 0}{1 + 0} = 3$.
3. **Conclude.** $\lim_{n \to \infty} \dfrac{3n + 5}{n + 2} = 3$.

**Verification:** $n = 1\,000$: $(3\,005)/(1\,002) \approx 2.999$. $n = 10^6$: $\approx 2.999999$. Converges to $3$.

**Trick.** For $\lim P(n)/Q(n)$ with $\deg P = \deg Q$, the limit is the ratio of leading coefficients. Here: $3/1 = 3$.

**Source.** [Lebl — Basic Analysis I §2.2](https://www.jirka.org/ra/) — license CC-BY-SA. Theorem on sequence limits.

</Ejemplo>

<Ejemplo titulo="Difference of square roots — conjugate technique" numero="3">

**Problem:** Compute $\lim_{n \to \infty} (\sqrt{n+1} - \sqrt{n})$.

**Strategy:** Indeterminate form $\infty - \infty$. Multiply by the conjugate $\sqrt{n+1} + \sqrt{n}$ to rationalize.

**Solution:**

1. **Multiply by conjugate.** $\sqrt{n+1} - \sqrt{n} = \dfrac{(\sqrt{n+1} - \sqrt{n})(\sqrt{n+1} + \sqrt{n})}{\sqrt{n+1} + \sqrt{n}} = \dfrac{(n+1) - n}{\sqrt{n+1} + \sqrt{n}} = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{n+1} + \sqrt{n}}$.
2. **Analyze the denominator.** $\sqrt{n+1} + \sqrt{n} \to +\infty$.
3. **Conclude.** $\lim_{n \to \infty} \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{n+1} + \sqrt{n}} = 0$.

**Verification:** $n = 100$: $\sqrt{101} - 10 \approx 0.0499$. $n = 10\,000$: $\approx 0.005$. Tends to zero.

**Source.** [Lebl — Basic Analysis I §2.2](https://www.jirka.org/ra/) — license CC-BY-SA. Standard technique for $\infty - \infty$ forms with roots.

</Ejemplo>

<Ejemplo titulo="The number e via (1+1/n)^n" numero="4">

**Problem:** Show intuitively that $\lim_{n \to \infty} (1 + 1/n)^n = e \approx 2.71828$.

**Strategy:** Compute numerically for increasing $n$. The monotone bounded criterion guarantees the limit exists.

**Solution:**

1. **Calculate some terms.**

   | $n$ | $(1 + 1/n)^n$ |
   |---|---|
   | $1$ | $2$ |
   | $10$ | $2.5937$ |
   | $100$ | $2.7048$ |
   | $1\,000$ | $2.7169$ |
   | $10^6$ | $2.71828$ |

2. **Observe monotonicity and boundedness.** One can show (via Newton's binomial) that the sequence is increasing and bounded above by $3$. By the monotone bounded theorem, the limit exists.

3. **Identify the limit.** The numerical value $e \approx 2.71828\ldots$ is defined as this limit (equivalent to the series $\sum 1/k!$).

**Verification:** SymPy: `sp.limit((1 + 1/n)**n, n, sp.oo)` returns `E`.

**Why does it matter?** Generalization: $\lim_{n \to \infty} (1 + r/n)^n = e^r$. For continuous compounding at rate $r$, the annual factor is $e^r$. The connection to interest will be formalized in Trim 5.

**Source.** [Active Calculus §8.1](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-8-3-geometric.html) — license CC-BY-NC-SA. Rigorous proof in [Lebl §2.4](https://www.jirka.org/ra/).

</Ejemplo>

<Ejemplo titulo="Fixed point of recurrence — Heron's method" numero="5">

**Problem:** Consider $a_1 = 1$, $a_{n+1} = (a_n + 2/a_n)/2$. To what value does it converge?

**Strategy:** If $a_n \to L$, then $a_{n+1} \to L$ as well. Pass to the limit in the recurrence to get an algebraic equation in $L$ (fixed-point equation).

**Solution:**

1. **Assume the limit $L$ exists.** $a_{n+1} \to L$ (same sequence, shifted by 1).
2. **Pass to the limit.** $L = (L + 2/L)/2 \Rightarrow 2L = L + 2/L \Rightarrow L = 2/L \Rightarrow L^2 = 2$.
3. **Solve.** $L = \pm\sqrt{2}$. Since $a_1 = 1 > 0$ and the recurrence preserves positivity, $L = \sqrt{2} \approx 1.41421$.

**Numerical verification:**

| $n$ | $a_n$ |
|---|---|
| $1$ | $1.000000$ |
| $2$ | $1.500000$ |
| $3$ | $1.416667$ |
| $4$ | $1.414216$ |
| $5$ | $1.414214$ |

**Connection.** This is the Newton-Raphson iteration for $f(x) = x^2 - 2$. Quadratic convergence: correct digits double each iteration. Algorithm of Heron of Alexandria (1st century CE), rediscovered by Newton in 17th century.
