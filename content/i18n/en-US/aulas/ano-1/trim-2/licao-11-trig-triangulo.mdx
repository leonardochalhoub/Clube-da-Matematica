<Porta nivel="formal" titulo="Rigorous definition">

<Definicao titulo="Trigonometric ratios in the right triangle">

Let $\triangle ABC$ be right-angled at $C$, with hypotenuse $\overline{AB}$ of length $c$, leg $\overline{BC}$ (opposite to $\hat A$) of length $a$, and leg $\overline{CA}$ (adjacent to $\hat A$) of length $b$. For the angle $\hat A = \theta \in (0°, 90°)$:

$$\sin\theta = \frac{a}{c}, \quad \cos\theta = \frac{b}{c}, \quad \tan\theta = \frac{a}{b}$$

The reciprocal ratios $\sec\theta = 1/\cos\theta$, $\csc\theta = 1/\sin\theta$, and $\cot\theta = 1/\tan\theta$ are the reciprocals.

</Definicao>

### Why do the ratios depend only on the angle?

**Triangle similarity (Thales):** triangles with the same three angles are similar — all their sides are proportional. In a right triangle with acute angle $\theta$, any enlargement or reduction preserves the three angles (90°, $\theta$, and 90°−$\theta$). The ratio $a/c$ is therefore the same for all such triangles — it depends only on $\theta$.

> "If two right triangles have an acute angle of equal measure, the triangles are similar; therefore, the ratios of the corresponding sides will be equal." — [OpenStax Algebra and Trigonometry 2e, §5.2](https://openstax.org/books/algebra-and-trigonometry-2e/pages/5-2-right-triangle-trigonometry)

### Fundamental identity

<Equation

<Teorema titulo="Pythagorean identities">

Starting from $\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1$, dividing by $\cos^2\theta$ (with $\cos\theta \neq 0$):

$$\tan^2\theta + 1 = \sec^2\theta$$

Dividing by $\sin^2\theta$ (with $\sin\theta \neq 0$):

$$1 + \cot^2\theta = \csc^2\theta$$

</Teorema>

| $\theta$ | $\sin\theta$ | $\cos\theta$ | $\tan\theta$ |
|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| $30°$ | $\dfrac{1}{2}$ | $\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$ | $\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{3}$ |
| $45°$ | $\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$ | $\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$ | $1$ |
| $60°$ | $\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$ | $\dfrac{1}{2}$ | $\sqrt{3}$ |

**Derivation 30-60-90:** Take an equilateral triangle with side 2 and draw the height, bisecting the base. You get a right triangle with hypotenuse 2, base 1, and height $\sqrt{4-1} = \sqrt{3}$. The smaller angle is 30° (at the base), the larger is 60° (at the apex).

**Derivation 45-45-90:** An isosceles right triangle has equal legs $a = b$. By Pythagoras: $c = a\sqrt{2}$. Thus $\sin 45° = a/(a\sqrt{2}) = \sqrt{2}/2$.

<Insight>

In the special values table, sine of $30°$ = cosine of $60°$, and vice versa. This is no coincidence: $\cos\theta = \sin(90° - \theta)$ for every acute angle — this is why the function is called **co**-sine (complement of sine).

</Insight>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

Take a ladder and lean it against a wall.

If the ladder is **very upright** (almost vertical), the top reaches **very high**. If the ladder is **very slanted**, the top reaches **very low**.

The tilt of the ladder — the angle it makes with the ground — is what we call an **angle**. And the height it reaches depends **only on this angle**, not the size of the ladder. A small ladder and a huge ladder at the same tilt reach proportional heights: the same ratio between height and length.

Trigonometry is the way to calculate that height **without having to measure** with a ruler. You only need to know the angle and the length — the formulas give the height.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

Trigonometry is the way to **calculate distances** without measuring everything.

**Example:** you are in front of a building. You measure the distance from your position to the base of the building: 50 meters. You measure the angle between the ground and the top of the building as you look from where you stand: 60 degrees.

How tall is the building?

The formula is: $\text{height} = 50 \cdot \tan(60°) = 50 \cdot \sqrt{3} \approx 86.6$ meters.

Done — you discovered the building's height without climbing it.

This trick is used in:

- **Surveying:** measuring the size of mountains and the depth of valleys.
- **Astronomy:** calculating the distance to planets and stars (without going there).
- **GPS:** your phone does triangulation with 4 satellites all the time to know where you are.

Trigonometry was invented in **Babylon almost 4,000 years ago** to predict eclipses and is inside your phone today.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old">

Trigonometry starts simple: the ratio between sides of a right triangle.

The magic is that **the ratio depends ONLY on the angle, not the size**. If you draw any right triangle with a 30° angle, **always** $\sin 30° = 1/2$. Always — whether the triangle is tiny or gigantic.

**Why?** Because triangles with the same angles are **similar** — proportional. The ratios between sides are preserved.

**Trick to remember:** SOH-CAH-TOA.
- **S**in = **O**pposite / **H**ypotenuse
- **C**os = **A**djacent / **H**ypotenuse
- **T**an = **O**pposite / **A**djacent

And the special values (memorize them!):

| $\theta$ | $\sin$ | $\cos$ | $\tan$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| $30°$ | $1/2$ | $\sqrt{3}/2$ | $\sqrt{3}/3$ |
| $45°$ | $\sqrt{2}/2$ | $\sqrt{2}/2$ | $1$ |
| $60°$ | $\sqrt{3}/2$ | $1/2$ | $\sqrt{3}$ |

Where does this show up immediately? **Vector decomposition** in Physics: any vector tilted at angle $\theta$ on the horizontal breaks into horizontal component ($\cos\theta$) and vertical component ($\sin\theta$).

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

Right-triangle trigonometry is the **special case** — angle in the first quadrant — of general trigonometry via the unit circle (Lesson 12). But it is the **most direct case** in structural engineering, basic physics, and signal analysis.

### Auxiliary Pythagorean identities

Dividing $\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1$ by $\cos^2\theta$:

$$\tan^2\theta + 1 = \sec^2\theta$$

It shows up in trigonometric substitution in integration (Calculus I), in friction formulas in mechanics, and in optics (Snell's law in trigonometric form).

### Inverse functions and notation

The problem "given $\sin\theta = k$, what is $\theta$?" uses $\theta = \arcsin(k)$ (or $\sin^{-1}(k)$ on the calculator — **not** $1/\sin(k)$). Note that on a scientific calculator you must be in **DEG** mode to work in degrees or in **RAD** mode for radians (conversion: $1 \text{ rad} = 180°/\pi \approx 57.3°$).

### Direct applications

- **Forces on a ramp:** force $F$ on an incline $\theta$ — component parallel to the ramp is $F\sin\theta$ (tends to cause sliding), component normal is $F\cos\theta$ (compresses the surface).
- **Velocity decomposition:** projectile launched at $\theta$ with initial velocity $v_0$ — horizontal component $v_x = v_0\cos\theta$, vertical $v_y = v_0\sin\theta$.
- **Structures:** cable or bar inclined under tension $T$ at $\theta$ with horizontal — vertical component $T\sin\theta$, horizontal $T\cos\theta$.
- **Antennas:** downtilt angle of a cellular antenna beam calculated by $\theta = \arctan(h/d)$, where $h$ is antenna height and $d$ is coverage distance.

### Numerical example

Loading ramp inclined at $15°$, length $8$ m. Vertical height: $h = 8\sin 15° \approx 8 \times 0.259 \approx 2.07$ m. Horizontal projection: $8\cos 15° \approx 7.73$ m.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

### Historical origin

Trigonometry as the calculation of ratios in triangles traces back to the Babylonian cuneiform tablet **Plimpton 322** ($\approx 1800$ B.C.) — a table of Pythagorean triples that implicitly encodes values of $\cos^2\theta$ for varying angles. Hipparchus of Nicaea (2nd century B.C.) compiled the first table of chords (equivalent to $2R\sin\theta$). Ptolemy systematized it in the *Almagest* (2nd century A.D.).

Modern notation (sine as a dimensionless ratio) crystallized in 17th-century Europe via Viète and later Newton — before that, the "chord" of an arc was the operational concept.

### Computational trigonometry (IEEE 754)

`sin(x)` in modern floating-point hardware uses:

1. **Argument reduction:** $x \bmod 2\pi$ via the **Payne-Hanek** algorithm (guards against catastrophic cancellation for large $x$).
2. **Minimax polynomial (Remez)** on $[-\pi/4, \pi/4]$.
3. **Quadrant reconstruction** via symmetry.

Maximum error: $\approx 0.5$ ULP. On GPU/SIMD, `sincos.approx` returns both simultaneously with latency ~10 cycles. The unit of measure in symbolic/derivative calculus is **always radian**: $(d/dx)\sin x = \cos x$ is valid only with $x$ in rad.

### Fourier — where trigonometry explodes

Every smooth periodic function decomposes into sines and cosines:

$$f(t) = \frac{a_0}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left[a_n\cos(n\omega t) + b_n\sin(n\omega t)\right]$$

Foundation of DSP, JPEG (DCT), MP3, magnetic resonance, modal analysis in structural and mechanical engineering, and quantum mechanics (stationary states). The **FFT** of Cooley-Tukey (1965) computes the discrete version in $O(N\log N)$ and is inside all digital processing.

### Positional embeddings in Transformers

In *Attention Is All You Need* ([Vaswani et al., 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.03762)), each position $p$ in the input sequence receives the vector:

$$PE_{(p,2k)} = \sin(p/10000^{2k/d}), \quad PE_{(p,2k+1)} = \cos(p/10000^{2k/d})$$

This encodes position continuously without trainable parameters — pure trigonometry at the heart of LLMs. The same idea of "position as angle" returns to Babylonian tables 3,800 years old.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practice — 3 real scenarios">

### Scenario 1: Height of a building in São Paulo

You stand 80 meters from the base of the **Edifício Itália** (downtown SP). The angle of elevation to the top measured with a theodolite is $58°$. Estimated height:

$$h = 80 \cdot \tan 58° \approx 80 \times 1.600 \approx 128 \text{ m}$$

(The actual building is $\approx 165$ m — the difference comes from the observer's eye height and uncertainty in the reference point.)

### Scenario 2: Street slope (Lapa, Rio)

A street is 200 m long with a vertical rise of 18 m. Slope in degrees:

$$\theta = \arctan(18/200) = \arctan(0.09) \approx 5.14°$$

On roads, it is often expressed as a **percentage**: $9\%$. The legal urban limit in Brazil (NBR 9283): 12%. Ramps above $5°$ require attention to startup torque and brake system.

### Scenario 3: Downtilt of a 5G antenna (telecom)

A 5G antenna is installed at 60 m height. To cover a point on the ground 250 m away horizontally:

$$\theta = \arctan(60/250) = \arctan(0.24) \approx 13.5°$$

In practice, urban 5G antennas operate with downtilt of $5°$ to $15°$. Above that value, the beam aims too close and loses range; below that, it creates interference in adjacent sectors.

## Worked examples

<Exemplo titulo="Calculate sin, cos, tan in a 3-4-5 triangle (application)" numero="1">

**Problem:** In a right triangle, the leg opposite to $\theta$ measures 3 and the adjacent leg measures 4. Calculate $\sin\theta$, $\cos\theta$, and $\tan\theta$.

**Strategy:** Apply SOH-CAH-TOA directly. First, find the hypotenuse using Pythagoras.

**Solution:**

1. **Hypotenuse:** $c = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = \sqrt{25} = 5$.
2. **Ratios:**

$$\sin\theta = \frac{3}{5}, \quad \cos\theta = \frac{4}{5}, \quad \tan\theta = \frac{3}{4}$$

**Verification (Pythagorean identity):** $(3/5)^2 + (4/5)^2 = 9/25 + 16/25 = 1$. ✓

**Source.** [OpenStax — Algebra and Trigonometry 2e §5.2](https://openstax.org/books/algebra-and-trigonometry-2e/pages/5-2-right-triangle-trigonometry) — CC-BY 4.0.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Sides of a 30-60-90 triangle with given hypotenuse (application)" numero="2">

**Problem:** In a right triangle with acute angle $30°$ and hypotenuse $20$ cm, calculate the two legs.

**Strategy:** Use the special values $\sin 30° = 1/2$ and $\cos 30° = \sqrt{3}/2$.

**Solution:**

1. **Leg opposite 30°:** $\sin 30° = \text{opp}/20 \Rightarrow \text{opp} = 20 \times 1/2 = 10$ cm.
2. **Leg adjacent to 30°:** $\cos 30° = \text{adj}/20 \Rightarrow \text{adj} = 20 \times \sqrt{3}/2 = 10\sqrt{3} \approx 17.32$ cm.

**Verification (Pythagoras):** $10^2 + (10\sqrt{3})^2 = 100 + 300 = 400 = 20^2$. ✓

**Source.** [Stitz–Zeager Precalculus §10.2](https://www.stitz-zeager.com/szprecalculus07042013.pdf) — CC-BY-NC-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Building height via angle of elevation (intermediate)" numero="3">

**Problem:** An observer is 50 m from the base of a building. The angle of elevation to the top is $40°$. Determine the building's height. (Given: $\tan 40° \approx 0.839$.)

**Strategy:** The observer, the base of the building, and the top form a right triangle. The height is the leg opposite the $40°$ angle; the horizontal distance is the adjacent leg. The correct ratio is the tangent.

**Solution:**

1. $\tan 40° = h/50$
2. $h = 50 \times 0.839 \approx 41.95$ m.

**Verification:** Line of sight $\approx \sqrt{42^2 + 50^2} \approx 65.3$ m; $\sin 40° \approx 42/65.3 \approx 0.643$. Matches the table. ✓

**Source.** [OpenStax — Algebra and Trigonometry 2e §5.2 (Applications)](https://openstax.org/books/algebra-and-trigonometry-2e/pages/5-2-right-triangle-trigonometry) — CC-BY 4.0.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Horizontal distance via angle of depression (advanced)" numero="4">

**Problem:** A drone flies at $100$ m height and detects a person on the ground at an angle of depression of $25°$ (measured downward from the horizontal). What is the horizontal distance between the drone and the person? (Use $\tan 25° \approx 0.466$.)

**Strategy:** The angle of depression at the drone is **alternate interior** with the angle of elevation the person would see. So in the right triangle "drone–projection on ground–person," the acute angle at the top is $25°$, opposite is the height $100$ m, and adjacent is the distance $d$.

**Solution:**

1. $\tan 25° = 100/d$
2. $d = 100/0.466 \approx 214.6$ m.

**Verification:** $\arctan(100/214.6) \approx 25°$. ✓

**Source.** [OpenStax — Algebra and Trigonometry 2e §5.2 (Elevation/Depression)](https://openstax.org/books/algebra-and-trigonometry-2e/pages/5-2-right-triangle-trigonometry) — CC-BY 4.0.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Height of a hill via two angles of sight (real modeling)" numero="5">

**Problem:** A surveyor at point $A$ sees the top of a hill at an angle of elevation $25°$. They walk $100$ m toward the hill until reaching point $B$, and the angle becomes $40°$. Calculate the height $h$. (Use $\tan 25° \approx 0.466$ and $\tan 40° \approx 0.839$.)

**Strategy:** The foot of the hill is $x$ m from $B$. We obtain two equations in $h$ and $x$, which we solve by substitution.

**Solution:**

1. Triangle from $B$: $h = x\tan 40°$.
2. Triangle from $A$: $h = (x + 100)\tan 25°$.
3. Setting equal: $x(0.839 - 0.466) = 100 \times 0.466 \Rightarrow x \approx 46.6/0.373 \approx 124.9$ m.
4. $h = 124.9 \times 0.839 \approx 104.8$ m.

**Verification:** $\tan(\text{angle from }A) \approx 104.8/224.9 \approx 0.466$, corresponding to $25°$. ✓

**Source.** [Stitz–Zeager Precalculus §10.3](https://www.stitz-zeager.com/szprecalculus07042013.pdf) — CC-BY-NC-SA.

</Exemplo>
