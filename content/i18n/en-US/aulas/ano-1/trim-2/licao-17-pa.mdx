<Porta nivel="formal" titulo="Definition and formulas">

<Definicao titulo="Arithmetic Progression (AP)">
Sequence $(a_n)$ such that $a_{n+1} - a_n = r$ (constant) for all $n \in \mathbb{N}^*$. The scalar $r$ is called the **ratio**.
</Definicao>

### General term

<Equation numero="1" explicacao="General term of an AP. Between a₁ and aₙ there are exactly n−1 steps of size r." latex="a_n = a_1 + (n-1)\,r" />

Provable by induction on $n$. Equivalent to $a_n = a_p + (n-p)\,r$ for any index $p$.

> "An arithmetic sequence is a sequence in which the difference between any two consecutive terms is constant." — [OpenStax College Algebra 2e, §9.2](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/9-2-arithmetic-sequences)

### Sum of the first $n$ terms

<Equation numero="2" explicacao="Gauss's sum. Each pair of terms equidistant from the ends sums to a₁ + aₙ. With n/2 pairs, the total sum is n(a₁ + aₙ)/2." latex="S_n = \sum_{k=1}^n a_k = \frac{n\,(a_1 + a_n)}{2} = \frac{n\,[\,2a_1 + (n-1)r\,]}{2}" />

**Proof** (Gauss as a child, $\sim$1789): write $S_n$ twice—in increasing and decreasing order:

<Eq>{`\\begin{aligned} S_n &= a_1 + a_2 + \\ldots + a_{n-1} + a_n \\\\ S_n &= a_n + a_{n-1} + \\ldots + a_2 + a_1 \\end{aligned}`}</Eq>

Adding term by term: $2 S_n = n\,(a_1 + a_n)$, since each pair sums to $a_1 + a_n$. ∎

### Properties

- **Arithmetic mean**: three consecutive terms satisfy $a_n = (a_{n-1} + a_{n+1})/2$.
- **Increasing** if $r > 0$, **decreasing** if $r < 0$, **constant** if $r = 0$.
- **Sum of extremes = sum of equidistant terms**: $a_1 + a_n = a_2 + a_{n-1} = \ldots$

<Teorema titulo="Uniqueness of AP from two terms">
Given $a_p$ and $a_q$ with $p \neq q$, there exists a unique AP with these values at positions $p$ and $q$. The ratio is $r = (a_q - a_p)/(q - p)$.
</Teorema>

### Sum of powers (preview)

$$\sum_{k=1}^n k = \frac{n(n+1)}{2}, \quad \sum_{k=1}^n k^2 = \frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}{6}, \quad \sum_{k=1}^n k^3 = \left(\frac{n(n+1)}{2}\right)^2$$

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

Climbing stairs with **equal steps**: 1 step, 2 steps, 3 steps... Each step adds the **same height**.

An AP is like this: you start at a number and **always add the same amount** to get the next one. Like $2, 4, 6, 8, 10$ (always add 2). Or $5, 8, 11, 14$ (always add 3).

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

An AP is a list of numbers where, from the first to the second, from the second to the third, and so on, the difference is **always the same**. That difference is called the **ratio**.

- $1, 4, 7, 10, 13$ — ratio 3.
- $20, 17, 14, 11$ — ratio $-3$ (going down).
- $0, 5, 10, 15, 20$ — ratio 5.

**What is the $n$-th term?** $a_n = a_1 + (n-1)\,r$.

Example: $1, 4, 7, 10, \ldots$ What is the 100th? $a_{100} = 1 + 99 \cdot 3 = 298$.

**Sum of an AP**: there is a clever formula. $S_n = n(a_1 + a_n)/2$.

Funny story: **Carl Friedrich Gauss at age 8**—his teacher asked him to add 1 to 100 to keep the class busy. In 30 seconds he handed in: $5050$. He paired $1 + 100 = 101$, $2 + 99 = 101$, $\ldots$, 50 pairs of $101$. Total: $50 \cdot 101 = 5050$.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old">

An AP is a list where each number is the previous **plus a fixed value**. The fixed value is called the **ratio**.

- $1, 4, 7, 10, 13, \ldots$ — AP with ratio 3.
- $20, 17, 14, 11, 8, \ldots$ — AP with ratio $-3$ (decreasing).
- $0, 5, 10, 15, 20, \ldots$ — AP with ratio 5.

**General term**: $a_n = a_1 + (n-1)\,r$. Like "what is the 50th term?": take $a_1$, add 49 times the ratio.

**Sum of an AP**: $S_n = n(a_1 + a_n)/2$. **Gauss's brilliant trick at age 8**: to sum $1+2+3+\ldots+100$, he paired them ($1+100, 2+99, 3+98, \ldots$)—each pair gives $101$, and there are $50$ pairs. Total: $5\,050$. **Without adding everything one by one.**

**Where does it appear?** Shoe sizes 32, 33, 34, 35, ... (AP). Building floors. Calendar dates. Salary with a fixed yearly raise (3,000 dollars, 3,300 dollars, 3,600 dollars, ...).

**Useful trick**: three consecutive terms $a, b, c$ form an AP if and only if $b = (a+c)/2$ (arithmetic mean). That is why we call it "arithmetic"!

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

An AP is the simplest recurrence. It appears as a **foundation** in:

- **Riemann sum as a staircase**: approximating an integral via finite sums—the summation has indices in an AP.
- **Time discretization**: regular sampling $t_n = t_0 + n \Delta t$ is an AP.
- **Numerical methods**: regular grids are APs in coordinates.
- **Classical cryptography**: Caesar ciphers shift letters in an AP.

### Rigorous proof of the general term

By induction on $n$. Base case $n = 1$: $a_1 = a_1 + 0 \cdot r$. ✓ Step: suppose $a_n = a_1 + (n-1)\,r$. Since $a_{n+1} = a_n + r$, then $a_{n+1} = a_1 + (n-1)\,r + r = a_1 + n\,r = a_1 + ((n+1)-1)\,r$. ∎

### Proof of sum via summation

Gauss's proof is equivalent to:

$$S_n = \sum_{k=1}^n [a_1 + (k-1)r] = n\,a_1 + r \sum_{k=1}^n (k-1) = n\,a_1 + r \cdot \frac{n(n-1)}{2}$$

### Faulhaber and sums of powers

APs do not cover everything. For sums $\sum n^2$ or $\sum n^3$, use distinct formulas proved by induction:

$$\sum_{k=1}^n k = \frac{n(n+1)}{2}, \quad \sum_{k=1}^n k^2 = \frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}{6}$$

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

An AP is a special case of an **arithmetic series**—the sum of an AP is a degree-2 polynomial in $n$. Generalization: **polynomial summations** $\sum k^m$ are degree $m+1$ polynomials in $n$ (Faulhaber, Bernoulli numbers).

### Faulhaber and Bernoulli

$$\sum_{k=1}^n k^m = \frac{1}{m+1} \sum_{j=0}^m \binom{m+1}{j} B_j\, n^{m+1-j}$$

where $B_j$ are Bernoulli numbers. For $m = 1$ we recover Gauss.

### Modern applications

- **Combinatorics**: symbolic summation via Gosper/Zeilberger algorithm automates hypergeometric sums.
- **Number theory**: **Dirichlet's theorem**—there are infinitely many primes in any AP $\{a + nd : \gcd(a,d) = 1\}$. The proof uses Dirichlet L-functions and complex analysis.
- **Arithmetic progressions and primes**: Green–Tao (2004) proved that the primes contain arithmetic progressions of arbitrary length.

### Pitfalls

- **Off-by-one**: $\sum_{k=1}^n$ vs $\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}$—they differ by whether we include $a_n$.
- **Empty sum**: $\sum_{k=a}^{a-1}$ equals $0$ by convention.
- **Numerical accumulation**: summing $10^6$ terms in float can accumulate error $\sim 10^{-10}$. Use Kahan's algorithm if precision is critical.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practical application">

**Recipe: given an AP, find everything.**

1. **Identify $a_1$ and $r$**.
2. **General term**: $a_n = a_1 + (n-1)\,r$.
3. **Sum**: $S_n = n(a_1 + a_n)/2$.
4. **Check** with 2 terms computed by hand.

### When to worry?

- **"Decreasing" AP**: $r < 0$. Do not confuse with "decreasing" in the sense of tending to zero—a non-zero AP never converges.
- **Negative sum**: can happen if $a_n < 0$ for several terms. Check signs.
- **In real problems**: salaries with fixed annual raises, simple interest, distance accumulated at constant velocity. AP models **linear** scenarios well.

</Porta>

</DuasPortas>

## Worked examples

<Exemplo titulo="General term of an AP" numero="1">

**Problem:** Given the AP $4, 7, 10, 13, \ldots$, compute the 25th term.

**Strategy:** identify $a_1$ and $r$, then apply $a_n = a_1 + (n-1)\,r$.

**Solution:**

1. **Identify $a_1$ and $r$.** The first term is $a_1 = 4$. The difference between consecutive terms is $r = 7 - 4 = 3$.
2. **Apply the general-term formula.** $a_{25} = 4 + (25 - 1) \cdot 3 = 4 + 72 = 76$.

**Check:** four terms by hand: $4, 7, 10, 13$—difference always $3$. For $n = 25$: we climb $24$ steps of height $3$ from $4$, total $76$. ✓

**Source.** [OpenStax College Algebra 2e §9.2](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/9-2-arithmetic-sequences) — CC-BY 4.0 license.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Find a₁ and r from two terms" numero="2">

**Problem:** In an AP, $a_5 = 17$ and $a_{10} = 32$. Find $a_1$ and $r$.

**Strategy:** set up a system of two equations in two unknowns using the general term. Subtraction eliminates $a_1$.

**Solution:**

1. **Write the general term at both points.**
   - $a_5 = a_1 + 4r = 17$
   - $a_{10} = a_1 + 9r = 32$
2. **Subtract the first from the second.** $5r = 15 \Rightarrow r = 3$.
3. **Substitute to find $a_1$.** $a_1 + 4 \cdot 3 = 17 \Rightarrow a_1 = 5$.

**Check:** AP $5, 8, 11, 14, 17, \ldots$—$a_5 = 17$ ✓ and $a_{10} = 5 + 9 \cdot 3 = 32$ ✓.

**Source.** [Stitz–Zeager Precalculus §9.2](https://www.stitz-zeager.com/szprecalculus07042013.pdf) — CC-BY-NC-SA license.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Sum using Gauss's formula" numero="3">

**Problem:** Compute $S = 7 + 11 + 15 + 19 + \ldots + 99$.

**Strategy:** identify the AP, find $n$ (number of terms) using the general term, then apply Gauss's formula.

**Solution:**

1. **Identify $a_1$ and $r$.** $a_1 = 7$, $r = 11 - 7 = 4$.
2. **Find $n$ using $a_n = 99$.** $99 = 7 + (n-1) \cdot 4 \Rightarrow 92 = 4(n-1) \Rightarrow n = 24$.
3. **Apply Gauss's formula.** $S_{24} = \dfrac{24 \cdot (7 + 99)}{2} = \dfrac{24 \cdot 106}{2} = 12 \cdot 106 = 1\,272$.

**Check:** pair the extremes $7 + 99 = 106$; $12$ pairs give $12 \cdot 106 = 1\,272$. ✓ Alternative: average $= 106/2 = 53$, total $= 53 \cdot 24 = 1\,272$. ✓

**Source.** [OpenStax College Algebra 2e §9.4](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/9-4-series-and-their-notations) — CC-BY 4.0 license.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="How many terms to exceed 1000?" numero="4">

**Problem:** How many terms of the AP $1, 4, 7, 10, \ldots$ must be summed so that $S_n \geq 1\,000$?

**Strategy:** $a_1 = 1$, $r = 3$, so $S_n = n(2 + (n-1) \cdot 3)/2 = n(3n - 1)/2$. Solve $n(3n-1)/2 \geq 1\,000$.

**Solution:**

1. **Set up the inequality.** $\dfrac{n(3n - 1)}{2} \geq 1\,000 \iff 3n^2 - n - 2\,000 \geq 0$.
2. **Solve the equation $3n^2 - n - 2\,000 = 0$.** By the quadratic formula: $n = \dfrac{1 \pm \sqrt{1 + 24\,000}}{6} = \dfrac{1 \pm \sqrt{24\,001}}{6}$.
3. **Approximate $\sqrt{24\,001} \approx 154.92$.** Positive root: $n \approx (1 + 154.92)/6 \approx 25.99$.
4. **Since $n$ is an integer, take $n = 26$.** Verify: $S_{26} = 26 \cdot (3 \cdot 26 - 1)/2 = 26 \cdot 77/2 = 1\,001 \geq 1\,000$. ✓ And $S_{25} = 25 \cdot 74/2 = 925 < 1\,000$.

**Check:** $a_{26} = 1 + 25 \cdot 3 = 76$ and $S_{26} = 26 \cdot (1 + 76)/2 = 26 \cdot 38.5 = 1\,001$. ✓

**Source.** [Stitz–Zeager Precalculus §9.2](https://www.stitz-zeager.com/szprecalculus07042013.pdf), exercises 9.2 — CC-BY-NC-SA license.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Free fall—distance in the n-th second (real modeling)" numero="5">

**Problem:** In free fall from rest, the distance traveled in the $n$-th second is $d_n = g(2n - 1)/2$, with $g = 9.81 \text{ m/s}^2$. (a) Show that $(d_n)$ is an AP. (b) Compute the total distance traveled in 5 seconds.

**Strategy:** verify that $d_{n+1} - d_n$ is constant (AP definition). Then sum the first 5 terms.

**Solution:**

1. **Compute the difference $d_{n+1} - d_n$.** $d_{n+1} - d_n = \dfrac{g[2(n+1) - 1]}{2} - \dfrac{g(2n - 1)}{2} = \dfrac{g \cdot 2}{2} = g$. Constant $= g$, so it is an AP with ratio $r = g$. ✓
2. **Identify $a_1 = d_1$.** $d_1 = g \cdot 1/2 = 4.905$ m.
3. **Compute $a_5 = d_5$.** $d_5 = g \cdot 9/2 = 44.145$ m.
4. **Sum using Gauss's formula.** $S_5 = 5 \cdot (d_1 + d_5)/2 = 5 \cdot (4.905 + 44.145)/2 = 5 \cdot 24.525 = 122.625$ m.

**Physical check**: in free fall, $S(t) = g t^2/2$. With $t = 5$: $S(5) = 9.81 \cdot 25/2 = 122.625$ m. ✓ Matches Galileo's rule of odd numbers (distances per second form $1, 3, 5, 7, 9, \ldots$ times $g/2$).

**Source.** [OpenStax College Physics 2e §2.7 (Falling Objects)](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/2-7-falling-objects) — CC-BY 4.0 license.

</Exemplo>
