<Porta nivel="formal" titulo="Definition via the unit circle">

<Definicao titulo="Trigonometric functions on the unit circle">
Let $C$ be the unit circle centered at $O = (0,0)$. For $\theta \in \mathbb{R}$ measured in radians from the positive x-axis, counterclockwise, the point $P(\theta) \in C$ is uniquely determined. We define:
$$\cos\theta = x_{P(\theta)}, \qquad \sin\theta = y_{P(\theta)}, \qquad \tan\theta = \frac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta}\ (\cos\theta \neq 0)$$
</Definicao>

> "The unit circle is a circle of radius 1 centered at the origin. The (x, y) coordinates of a point on this circle, where the angle in standard position is t, are (cos t, sin t)." — [OpenStax Algebra and Trigonometry 2e, §5.3](https://openstax.org/books/algebra-and-trigonometry-2e/pages/5-3-unit-circle-sine-and-cosine-functions)

  audioTexto="Cosine squared of theta plus sine squared of theta equals one. It holds for every real angle."

### Periodicity

$$\sin(\theta + 2\pi) = \sin\theta \qquad \cos(\theta + 2\pi) = \cos\theta \qquad \forall\,\theta \in \mathbb{R}$$

Angles that differ by multiples of $2\pi$ determine the **same** point on the circle: they are called **coterminal angles**.

### Signs by quadrant

| Quadrant | $\theta$ (rad) | $\sin\theta$ | $\cos\theta$ | $\tan\theta$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| I | $(0,\, \pi/2)$ | $+$ | $+$ | $+$ |
| II | $(\pi/2,\, \pi)$ | $+$ | $-$ | $-$ |
| III | $(\pi,\, 3\pi/2)$ | $-$ | $-$ | $+$ |
| IV | $(3\pi/2,\, 2\pi)$ | $-$ | $+$ | $-$ |

### Special angles

| $\theta$ | $0$ | $\pi/6$ | $\pi/4$ | $\pi/3$ | $\pi/2$ | $\pi$ | $3\pi/2$ | $2\pi$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $\sin$ | $0$ | $1/2$ | $\sqrt{2}/2$ | $\sqrt{3}/2$ | $1$ | $0$ | $-1$ | $0$ |
| $\cos$ | $1$ | $\sqrt{3}/2$ | $\sqrt{2}/2$ | $1/2$ | $0$ | $-1$ | $0$ | $1$ |

### Symmetry identities

<Definicao titulo="Parity and fundamental reductions">

$$\sin(-\theta) = -\sin\theta \quad (\sin \text{ é função ímpar})$$
$$\cos(-\theta) = \cos\theta \quad (\cos \text{ é função par})$$
$$\sin(\pi - \theta) = \sin\theta, \quad \cos(\pi - \theta) = -\cos\theta$$
$$\sin(\pi + \theta) = -\sin\theta, \quad \cos(\pi + \theta) = -\cos\theta$$

</Definicao>

<Teorema titulo="Sum formulas">
For any $\alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{R}$:
$$\sin(\alpha \pm \beta) = \sin\alpha\cos\beta \pm \cos\alpha\sin\beta$$
$$\cos(\alpha \pm \beta) = \cos\alpha\cos\beta \mp \sin\alpha\sin\beta$$

**Proof idea:** rotating by $\alpha + \beta$ is the composition of two rotations. In coordinates, this is the product of rotation matrices $R(\alpha)R(\beta) = R(\alpha+\beta)$.
</Teorema>

<Insight>
Every reduction to a quadrant results from applying the identities above. Memorize the signs by quadrant and the first-quadrant table — the rest follows.
</Insight>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="The Ferris wheel">

Imagine a **Ferris wheel** at an amusement park. You get into the little seat at the very bottom. The wheel starts turning slowly. You go up, then you're at the top, then you come down, then you're at the bottom again.

Your **height** changes like a wave: it rises slowly, reaches the top, comes down slowly, reaches the bottom, and starts over. Always the same wave. Always in the same way.

That wave of the height is what mathematicians call the **sine**. And the "sideways position" (how close you are to the right side or the left side) is the **cosine**. The two waves return to the same place after one full turn of the wheel.

The trigonometric circle is exactly the drawing of that Ferris wheel seen from above, with radius equal to 1.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="Beyond the right triangle">

Right-triangle trigonometry only works for angles between 0° and 90°. But what about larger angles? And negative ones?

The solution is **a circle**. A circle of radius 1 drawn in the Cartesian plane, centered at the origin.

For each angle $\theta$ — measured from the x-axis, rotating upward — you mark the corresponding point **on the circle**. That point has two coordinates: $x$ and $y$. And then: $x = \cos\theta$ and $y = \sin\theta$. There you go, the definition extended to any angle!

**Radians**: instead of measuring an angle in degrees (0° to 360°), mathematicians use radians. One full turn = $2\pi$ radians. Why? Because it is natural for the circle of radius 1: the entire perimeter is $2\pi$, so rotating $2\pi$ radians traces exactly the perimeter.

Conversion: $180° = \pi$ rad. From there $90° = \pi/2$, $60° = \pi/3$, $45° = \pi/4$, $30° = \pi/6$.

**Periodicity**: after rotating one full turn, you return to the same point. That is why $\sin(\theta + 2\pi) = \sin\theta$ for every angle.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="Generalizing the triangle to the plane">

In Lesson 11 we saw trigonometry in the **right triangle** — it only works for acute angles (0° to 90°).

And for larger angles, such as 120°, 270°, or negative ones?

The solution is the **trigonometric circle**: a circle of radius 1 centered at the origin. For each angle $\theta$ (measured from the x-axis, counterclockwise), the corresponding point on the circle has coordinates $(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$. **Definition extended to every $\theta \in \mathbb{R}$**.

**Why radians?** One full turn = $2\pi$ radians = $360°$. The unit is "natural" for circles: the arc of radius 1 swept by the angle $\theta$ has length exactly $\theta$ (in radians). In degrees it would be $\theta\pi/180$ — an inconvenient factor. More important: in calculus, $(\sin x)' = \cos x$ holds only with $x$ in radians.

**Pythagorean identity**: for any $\theta$, the point $P(\theta)$ lies on the circle of radius 1, so $\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1$.

**Reducing to the first quadrant**: to compute $\sin(5\pi/3)$, identify the quadrant (IV), find the reference angle ($2\pi - 5\pi/3 = \pi/3$), apply the table ($\sin(\pi/3) = \sqrt{3}/2$), and adjust the sign ($\sin$ negative in IV). Result: $\sin(5\pi/3) = -\sqrt{3}/2$.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="Precalculus and foundations of Fourier analysis">

The unit circle is the central object of harmonic analysis — any periodic signal can be decomposed into sines and cosines.

### Double-angle formulas

$$\sin 2\theta = 2\sin\theta\cos\theta$$
$$\cos 2\theta = \cos^2\theta - \sin^2\theta = 1 - 2\sin^2\theta = 2\cos^2\theta - 1$$

They derive from the sum formulas with $\alpha = \beta = \theta$. The second form $1 - 2\sin^2\theta$ is used to reduce powers before integrating ($\int \sin^2 x\,dx$).

### Euler's formula

$$e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta$$

Derived from the Taylor series of $e^x$, $\sin x$, and $\cos x$. For $\theta = \pi$: $e^{i\pi} + 1 = 0$. In electrical engineering, impedances are $Z = |Z|e^{i\varphi}$ — magnitude and phase on the unit circle of the complex plane.

### Direct applications

- **AC signal**: $V(t) = V_0\sin(\omega t + \varphi)$ — $\omega = 2\pi f$, phase $\varphi$ as an angle on the circle.
- **Vector rotation**: <Eq>{`R(\\theta) = \\begin{pmatrix}\\cos\\theta & -\\sin\\theta \\\\ \\sin\\theta & \\cos\\theta\\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq> — every rotation of $\mathbb{R}^2$ is determined by an angle on the circle.
- **Arc length**: $s = r\theta$ — clean only in radians, which is why the unit is "natural".

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="The SO(2) group, Fourier analysis, and rotary encoding">

The unit circle $S^1 = \{z \in \mathbb{C} : |z| = 1\}$ is the fundamental object of:

**Fourier analysis:** every function $f \in L^2([0, 2\pi])$ decomposes as $\hat{f}(n) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi} f(\theta)e^{-in\theta}\,d\theta$ — the Fourier coefficients are inner products with the characters $e^{in\theta}$ of the group $U(1) \cong S^1$.

**The group $SO(2) \cong U(1)$:** the rotations of the plane form the compact Lie group $SO(2)$, isomorphic to the multiplicative group $U(1) = \{e^{i\theta} : \theta \in \mathbb{R}\}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}$. The representation theory of this group classifies all periodic symmetries in dimension 2.

**Rotary embeddings (RoPE):** in LLM architectures such as GPT, the position of token $k$ is encoded as $e^{ik\theta_p}$ applied to pairs of coordinates of the embedding vector — exactly rotation on the unit circle of the complex plane. The attention dot product between tokens $m$ and $n$ captures only the relative difference $m - n$.

**`atan2` vs. `atan`:** in production code, `atan(y/x)` loses quadrant information and divides by zero at $x = 0$. Always use `atan2(y, x)`, which returns $\theta \in (-\pi, \pi]$ correctly in all quadrants.

**Roots of unity:** $e^{2\pi ik/N}$ for $k = 0, \ldots, N-1$ are the $N$ equally spaced points on $S^1$ — the basis of the FFT (Fast Fourier Transform), which reduces the DFT from $O(N^2)$ to $O(N\log N)$.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Recipe and classic bug">

**Recipe: computing any trigonometric value.**

1. **Reduce** the angle to the interval $[0, 2\pi)$ via $\theta \leftarrow \theta \bmod 2\pi$.
2. **Identify the quadrant** — it determines the sign of $\sin$, $\cos$, $\tan$.
3. **Compute the reference angle** (acute, in the first quadrant): Q1: $\theta$; Q2: $\pi - \theta$; Q3: $\theta - \pi$; Q4: $2\pi - \theta$.
4. **Apply the table** of special angles and adjust the sign.

**Concrete example:** $\cos(13\pi/4)$?
- Reduce: $13\pi/4 - 8\pi/4 = 5\pi/4$ (subtract $2\pi$).
- Quadrant III ($\pi < 5\pi/4 < 3\pi/2$). Reference: $5\pi/4 - \pi = \pi/4$.
- Table: $\cos(\pi/4) = \sqrt{2}/2$. Sign in III: $\cos < 0$.
- Result: $\cos(13\pi/4) = -\sqrt{2}/2$.

**Classic bug — DEG vs. RAD:** a scientific calculator in the wrong mode. On an entrance exam, $\sin(90)$ in degree mode = 1; in radian mode $\approx 0.894$. Always check the mode before computing.

**Arc length**: $s = r\theta$ holds only with $\theta$ in radians. In degrees it would be $s = r\theta\pi/180$.

```python
import sympy as sp
theta = sp.Rational(13, 4) * sp.pi
print(sp.cos(theta))         # -sqrt(2)/2

# Wolfram Alpha: cos(13 pi / 4) exact
```

## Worked examples

<Exemplo titulo="Converting degrees to radians (direct application)" numero="1">

**Problem:** Convert $225°$ to radians.

**Strategy:** Multiply by $\pi/180$.

**Solution:**

$$225° \times \frac{\pi}{180°} = \frac{225\pi}{180} = \frac{5\pi}{4} \text{ rad}$$

(Simplification: $\gcd(225, 180) = 45$; $225/45 = 5$, $180/45 = 4$.)

**Check:** $\frac{5\pi}{4} \times \frac{180°}{\pi} = \frac{5 \times 180°}{4} = 225°$. ✓

**Source.** [OpenStax — Algebra and Trigonometry 2e §5.1](https://openstax.org/books/algebra-and-trigonometry-2e/pages/5-1-angles) — license CC-BY 4.0.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Sine and cosine at a second-quadrant angle" numero="2">

**Problem:** Compute $\sin(2\pi/3)$ and $\cos(2\pi/3)$.

**Strategy:** Identify the quadrant, compute the reference angle, apply the table, adjust the sign.

**Solution:**

1. **Quadrant:** $\pi/2 < 2\pi/3 < \pi$ — quadrant II.
2. **Reference angle:** $\pi - 2\pi/3 = \pi/3$.
3. **Table:** $\sin(\pi/3) = \sqrt{3}/2$, $\cos(\pi/3) = 1/2$.
4. **Signs (Q2):** $\sin > 0$, $\cos < 0$. Hence $\sin(2\pi/3) = \sqrt{3}/2$, $\cos(2\pi/3) = -1/2$.

**Check:** $(\sqrt{3}/2)^2 + (-1/2)^2 = 3/4 + 1/4 = 1$. ✓

**Source.** [Stitz–Zeager Precalculus §10.3](https://www.stitz-zeager.com/szprecalculus07042013.pdf) — license CC-BY-NC-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Reducing an angle by periodicity (intermediate)" numero="3">

**Problem:** Compute $\cos(13\pi/4)$.

**Strategy:** Reduce modulo $2\pi$, then identify the quadrant and reference angle.

**Solution:**

1. **Reduce:** $13\pi/4 - 2\pi = 5\pi/4$. (Since $5\pi/4 < 2\pi$, we stop.)
2. **Quadrant:** $\pi < 5\pi/4 < 3\pi/2$ — quadrant III.
3. **Reference:** $5\pi/4 - \pi = \pi/4$.
4. **Table and sign (Q3, $\cos < 0$):** $\cos(13\pi/4) = -\sqrt{2}/2$.

**Check:** $\cos(13\pi/4) = \cos(13\pi/4 - 2 \times 2\pi) = \cos(5\pi/4)$, and numerically $-\sqrt{2}/2 \approx -0{,}7071$. ✓

**Source.** [OpenStax — Algebra and Trigonometry 2e §5.3](https://openstax.org/books/algebra-and-trigonometry-2e/pages/5-3-unit-circle-sine-and-cosine-functions) — license CC-BY 4.0.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Verifying a reflection identity (proof)" numero="4">

**Problem:** Prove that $\cos(\theta + \pi) = -\cos\theta$ for every $\theta \in \mathbb{R}$.

**Strategy:** Use the cosine sum formula with $\cos\pi = -1$, $\sin\pi = 0$.

**Solution:**

$$\cos(\theta + \pi) = \cos\theta\cos\pi - \sin\theta\sin\pi = \cos\theta \cdot (-1) - \sin\theta \cdot 0 = -\cos\theta.$$

**Geometric interpretation:** adding $\pi$ to the angle is equivalent to reflecting the point $P(\theta)$ through the origin — both coordinates change sign: $(\cos\theta, \sin\theta) \to (-\cos\theta, -\sin\theta)$.

**Numerical check:** $\cos(\pi/4 + \pi) = \cos(5\pi/4) = -\sqrt{2}/2 = -\cos(\pi/4)$. ✓

**Source.** [Stitz–Zeager Precalculus §10.4](https://www.stitz-zeager.com/szprecalculus07042013.pdf) — license CC-BY-NC-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Computing sin 75° via the sum of angles (advanced)" numero="5">

**Problem:** Compute $\sin(75°)$ exactly, without a calculator, using $75° = 45° + 30°$.

**Strategy:** Apply $\sin(\alpha + \beta) = \sin\alpha\cos\beta + \cos\alpha\sin\beta$.

**Solution:**

$$\sin(45° + 30°) = \sin 45°\cos 30° + \cos 45°\sin 30° = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} + \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{2} = \frac{\sqrt{6}}{4} + \frac{\sqrt{2}}{4} = \frac{\sqrt{6}+\sqrt{2}}{4}.$$

**Numerical check:** $(\sqrt{6}+\sqrt{2})/4 \approx (2{,}449 + 1{,}414)/4 \approx 0{,}9659$, and $\sin 75° \approx 0{,}9659$. ✓

**Source.** [OpenStax — Algebra and Trigonometry 2e §9.2](https://openstax.org/books/algebra-and-trigonometry-2e/pages/9-2-sum-and-difference-identities) — license CC-BY 4.0.

</Exemplo>
