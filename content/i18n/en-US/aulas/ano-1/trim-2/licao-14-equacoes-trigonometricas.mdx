<Porta nivel="formal" titulo="Structure of solutions">

<Definicao titulo="General solutions of basic trigonometric equations">

Let $a \in [-1, 1]$.

- **Sine:** $\sin x = a \iff x = \arcsin a + 2k\pi \quad \text{or} \quad x = \pi - \arcsin a + 2k\pi,\quad k \in \mathbb{Z}$
  where $\arcsin: [-1, 1] \to [-\pi/2, \pi/2]$ is the principal inverse (range in Q1 or Q4).
- **Cosine:** $\cos x = a \iff x = \pm\arccos a + 2k\pi,\quad k \in \mathbb{Z}$
  where $\arccos: [-1, 1] \to [0, \pi]$.
- **Tangent:** $\tan x = a \iff x = \arctan a + k\pi,\quad k \in \mathbb{Z}$
  with $\arctan: \mathbb{R} \to (-\pi/2, \pi/2)$. **The period of tangent is $\pi$**, not $2\pi$.

</Definicao>

> "We close this section with one final example of a trigonometric inequality. To solve such an inequality, we begin by replacing the inequality with the corresponding equation, then check the resulting intervals." — [Stitz–Zeager, Precalculus §10.7](https://www.stitz-zeager.com/szprecalculus07042013.pdf)

> "Solving a trigonometric equation requires the same techniques as solving any equation: isolate the variable, use factoring, use the quadratic formula, use identities, and set each factor equal to zero." — [OpenStax Algebra and Trigonometry 2e §9.5](https://openstax.org/books/algebra-and-trigonometry-2e/pages/9-5-solving-trigonometric-equations)

<Teorema titulo="Number of solutions in an interval of length $2\pi$">
The equation $\sin x = a$ with $a \in (-1, 1)$ has exactly **2 solutions** in any interval $[\theta, \theta + 2\pi)$. For $a = \pm 1$, exactly **1 solution**. For $|a| > 1$, **no real** solutions.

The equation $\tan x = a$ has exactly **1 solution** in any interval of length $\pi$ that does not contain a vertical asymptote.
</Teorema>

### Strategy of reduction

<Definicao titulo="Types of reducible equations">

| Type | Example | Strategy |
|---|---|---|
| Polynomial in $\sin$ | $2\sin^2 x - \sin x - 1 = 0$ | $u = \sin x$, quadratic formula |
| Polynomial in $\cos$ | $\cos^2 x = 1/4$ | $u = \cos x$, square root |
| $\sin x \pm \cos x = c$ | $\sin x + \cos x = 1$ | $R\sin(x + \varphi)$ with $R = \sqrt{a^2+b^2}$ |
| Double angle | $\sin 2x = \sin x$ | $2\sin x\cos x = \sin x$; factor |
| Sum-to-product | $\sin x + \sin 3x = 0$ | $2\sin 2x\cos x = 0$ |

</Definicao>

<Insight>

**Why does tangent have period $\pi$ and not $2\pi$?** Because $\tan x = \sin x / \cos x$, and both sine and cosine change sign between $[0, \pi)$ and $[\pi, 2\pi)$ — the ratio stays the same. Geometrically: the angle $x$ and the angle $x + \pi$ point in opposite directions on the circle, but the slope of the line through the origin is the same.

</Insight>

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

Imagine a clock. At which times of the day does the hour hand point to the **6** (down there)? Answer: **6:00 and 6:30 PM** (and also at 30:00 if you want more cycles, but there is no 30:00 on a normal day).

A trigonometric equation is like this: you want to know **at which angles** sine (or cosine) has a certain value. And the answer is a **list** of angles, because the wheel spins forever.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

The equation $\sin x = 1/2$ is not like $x + 3 = 5$ (one answer only). It has **infinitely many answers** because sine repeats.

Imagine the unit circle. Which points have $y = 1/2$? **Two points**: one in the first quadrant (angle $\pi/6$) and one in the second (angle $5\pi/6$). And since you can spin the circle as many times as you want, each of those two points becomes **infinite**: $\pi/6, \pi/6 + 2\pi, \pi/6 + 4\pi, \ldots$

That's why the answer to a trigonometric equation comes in **set form**:
$$x = \pi/6 + 2k\pi \quad \text{or} \quad x = 5\pi/6 + 2k\pi$$

for any integer $k$.

Usually the teacher asks for the answer in $[0, 2\pi)$ — just one spin. Then it's easy: list the solutions in that interval and done.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old">

Solving $\sin x = 1/2$ is not as simple as solving $x + 3 = 5$.

Why? Because sine repeats all the time. If $x = \pi/6$ is a solution, $x = \pi/6 + 2\pi$ is too. And $x = \pi/6 + 4\pi$. And... infinitely many.

Plus, $\sin(\pi - \pi/6) = \sin(5\pi/6) = 1/2$ also. So in $[0, 2\pi)$ there are **two** solutions: $\pi/6$ and $5\pi/6$.

**Recipe:**
1. Find the solutions on the unit circle, in $[0, 2\pi)$.
2. Add $2k\pi$ (all full rotations).

For tangent it is different: period of $\tan$ is $\pi$ (not $2\pi$), because $\tan(x + \pi) = \tan x$.

**More elaborate equations** reduce to basics via identities:
- $\cos 2x = \cos^2 x - \sin^2 x = 1 - 2 \sin^2 x$.
- $\sin 2x = 2 \sin x \cos x$.
- $\sin x + \cos x = \sqrt 2 \sin(x + \pi/4)$.

**Inequalities**: same but with an interval. $\sin x > 1/2$ on $[0, 2\pi)$? The $x$ where $\sin x$ is above $1/2$ — from the graph, that is $(\pi/6, 5\pi/6)$.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

In practice, **trigonometry is not solved by hand** after high school. Use a calculator or solver. But the structure of the solutions is important because it controls the form of answers in engineering problems.

In control, **phase coupling** between two signals is given by $\arctan$ of the imaginary-over-real ratio of the transfer quotient. In mechanics, angle of equilibrium is the root of a trigonometric equation.

### Numerical methods

For trigonometric equations that do not reduce to basics (e.g., $x = \cos x$), use **Newton-Raphson** or **bisection**. **There is no closed form**.

```python
from scipy.optimize import brentq
import numpy as np
sol = brentq(lambda x: x - np.cos(x), 0, 1)
print(sol)  # ~0.739085
```

### Complex solutions (preview)

For $\sin z = 5$ (impossible in reals), there is a solution in $\mathbb{C}$ using Euler:
$$\sin z = (e^{iz} - e^{-iz})/(2i) = 5 \Rightarrow z = \pi/2 - i \ln(5 + 2\sqrt 6)$$

### Applications in engineering

- **Force equilibrium**: $\sum F_i \cos \theta_i = 0$ — a trig equation in $\theta$.
- **Inverse kinematics**: robot arm, joint angle solves $a \cos\theta + b \sin\theta = c$.
- **Acoustics**: room resonance when $\sin(k_x L_x) = 0$ — natural modes.
- **Control systems**: zeros and poles on the unit circle — where $z = e^{j\omega T}$.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

Trigonometric equations are a special case of **transcendental equations**. In general they do not admit closed form — only formulas for cases polynomial in $\sin, \cos, \tan$.

### Algebraic structure

The **ring of trigonometric functions** $\mathbb{R}[\sin x, \cos x]$, modulo $\sin^2 x + \cos^2 x = 1$, is isomorphic to $\mathbb{R}[t, t^{-1}]/(t \bar t = 1)$ via Euler $t = e^{ix}$. This reduces every polynomial equation in $\sin x, \cos x$ to an algebraic equation in $t$ on the unit circle in $\mathbb{C}$.

```python
import sympy as sp
x = sp.symbols('x', real=True)

# General symbolic solution:
sp.solveset(sp.sin(x) - sp.Rational(1,2), x, sp.S.Reals)
# Union(ImageSet({2*pi*n + pi/6, n in Z}),
#       ImageSet({2*pi*n + 5*pi/6, n in Z}))

# On an interval:
sp.solveset(sp.sin(x) - sp.Rational(1,2), x, sp.Interval(0, 2*sp.pi))
```

### Advanced patterns

- **Kepler equation**: $M = E - e \sin E$ — solved for elliptic orbit. Newton converges in ~5 iterations for $e < 0.9$.
- **Bessel equation**: non-closed zeros, but tabulated. Appears in vibration modes of a drum.
- **Chebyshev identities**: $\cos(n\theta) = T_n(\cos\theta)$ with $T_n$ a degree-$n$ polynomial — basis of Gauss-Chebyshev quadrature.

### Pitfalls

- **`np.arcsin` returns in $[-\pi/2, \pi/2]$**: to get a solution in quadrant II, use $\pi - \arcsin a$.
- **Calculator mode**: deg vs rad in CFD/FEA — classic source of validation bugs.
- **Lost solutions** when multiplying/dividing by $\sin x$ or $\cos x$ (which may be zero): always check whether an extra solution exists where that function vanishes.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practical application">

**Master recipe for solving a trigonometric equation:**

1. **Try to reduce to basic** ($\sin x = a$, $\cos x = a$, $\tan x = a$).
2. **Use identities** (Pythagorean, sum, double angle) if needed.
3. **Substitute** $u = \sin x$ or $u = \cos x$ if it is polynomial.
4. **Solve** $u$ first, then return to $x$.
5. **List** solutions in the requested interval.
6. **Check** one of the solutions in the original equation.

### Wolfram / SymPy workflow

```python
import sympy as sp
x = sp.symbols('x', real=True)

# Simple equation:
sp.solveset(sp.sin(x) - sp.cos(x), x, sp.Interval(0, 2*sp.pi))
# {pi/4, 5*pi/4}

# Equation with identity:
eq = sp.sin(2*x) - sp.sin(x)   # = 2 sin(x) cos(x) - sin(x)
sp.solveset(eq, x, sp.Interval(0, 2*sp.pi))

# Inequality:
sp.reduce_inequalities(sp.sin(x) > sp.Rational(1, 2))
```

Wolfram Alpha: `solve sin(x) + cos(x) = 1, x in [0, 2pi]` returns $\{0, \pi/2\}$.

### When to worry?

- **Extraneous solutions** when squaring: $\sin x = -\cos x$ has a solution, but $\sin^2 x = \cos^2 x$ has **more** solutions (including $\sin x = +\cos x$). Always check.
- **When dividing by $\cos x$**: you lose solutions where $\cos x = 0$. Treat that case separately.
- **`tan` at a critical point**: $\tan x$ goes to $\pm \infty$ at $x = \pi/2$ — in the inequality, that point is **not** in the domain.
- **In physical problems**, restrict the domain: negative angles may not make sense.

</Porta>

## Worked examples

> Five examples with increasing difficulty — from the most direct (basic equation on an interval) to real-world modeling (finding when tide wave crests occur). Each example cites its source: the original problem always comes from an open book.

<Exemplo titulo="Basic sine equation on an interval (application)" numero="1">

**Problem:** Solve $\sin x = \tfrac{1}{2}$ on $[0, 2\pi)$.

**Strategy:** The equation is of basic type. Use the unit circle to identify which angles of the first rotation have sine equal to $1/2$ — exactly two, symmetric about the vertical axis.

**Solution:**

1. **Identify the reference angle.** The value $1/2$ is tabulated: $\sin(\pi/6) = 1/2$. This is the reference angle in the first quadrant.
2. **Use sine symmetry.** On the circle, sine has the same value at $\pi/6$ and at $\pi - \pi/6 = 5\pi/6$ (second quadrant). In quadrants 3 and 4 sine is negative.
3. **List the solutions in $[0, 2\pi)$.**
$$x = \frac{\pi}{6} \quad \text{or} \quad x = \frac{5\pi}{6}$$

**Check:** $\sin(\pi/6) = 0.5$ and $\sin(5\pi/6) = \sin(\pi - \pi/6) = \sin(\pi/6) = 0.5$. Correct.

**Source.** [OpenStax — Algebra and Trigonometry 2e §9.5](https://openstax.org/books/algebra-and-trigonometry-2e/pages/9-5-solving-trigonometric-equations) — CC-BY 4.0 license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="General solution in ℝ (application)" numero="2">

**Problem:** Find all real solutions to $\cos x = -\tfrac{\sqrt 2}{2}$.

**Strategy:** Identify the basic solution in the interval $[0, \pi]$ via $\arccos$, then apply the symmetry $\cos(-x) = \cos x$ and periodicity $2\pi$ to generate all solutions.

**Solution:**

1. **Principal solution.** Since $\arccos(-\sqrt 2/2) = 3\pi/4$, this is the angle in the second quadrant.
2. **Symmetry.** By the principle $\cos(-x) = \cos x$, the angle $-3\pi/4$ (or equivalent $5\pi/4$) also works.
3. **Generalize.** Adding $2k\pi$ (period of cosine):
$$x = \tfrac{3\pi}{4} + 2k\pi \quad \text{or} \quad x = -\tfrac{3\pi}{4} + 2k\pi, \quad k \in \mathbb{Z}.$$

**Check:** For $k = 0$: $\cos(3\pi/4) = -\sqrt 2/2$ and $\cos(-3\pi/4) = \cos(3\pi/4) = -\sqrt 2/2$. Correct.

**Source.** [Stitz–Zeager — Precalculus §10.7](https://www.stitz-zeager.com/szprecalculus07042013.pdf) — CC-BY-NC-SA license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Quadratic equation in sin x (intermediate)" numero="3">

**Problem:** Solve $2\sin^2 x - \sin x - 1 = 0$ on $[0, 2\pi)$.

**Strategy:** Substitute $u = \sin x$ and solve the quadratic $2u^2 - u - 1 = 0$. Then return to the angles corresponding to each root.

**Solution:**

1. **Substitution.** Let $u = \sin x$. The equation becomes $2u^2 - u - 1 = 0$.
2. **Quadratic formula.** $u = \dfrac{1 \pm \sqrt{1 + 8}}{4} = \dfrac{1 \pm 3}{4}$. So $u = 1$ or $u = -1/2$.
3. **Return to $x$.**
   - $\sin x = 1 \Rightarrow x = \pi/2$.
   - $\sin x = -1/2 \Rightarrow x = 7\pi/6$ or $x = 11\pi/6$ (third and fourth quadrants).
4. **Solution set on $[0, 2\pi)$:** $\{\pi/2,\ 7\pi/6,\ 11\pi/6\}$.

**Check:** $2\sin^2(\pi/2) - \sin(\pi/2) - 1 = 2 - 1 - 1 = 0$. $2\sin^2(7\pi/6) - \sin(7\pi/6) - 1 = 2(1/4) - (-1/2) - 1 = 0$. Correct.

**Source.** [OpenStax — Algebra and Trigonometry 2e §9.5](https://openstax.org/books/algebra-and-trigonometry-2e/pages/9-5-solving-trigonometric-equations) — CC-BY 4.0 license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Trigonometric inequality (advanced)" numero="4">

**Problem:** Solve $\sin x > \tfrac{\sqrt 3}{2}$ on $[0, 2\pi)$.

**Strategy:** Convert the inequality to an equation to find boundary points; then inspect, on the unit circle, which arc has sine above $\sqrt 3/2$.

**Solution:**

1. **Associated equation.** $\sin x = \sqrt 3/2$ has solutions $x = \pi/3$ and $x = 2\pi/3$ on $[0, 2\pi)$.
2. **Graph analysis.** The graph of $\sin x$ on $[0, 2\pi)$ is a curve that reaches its maximum at $\pi/2$. Above $\sqrt 3/2$, it lies only between $\pi/3$ and $2\pi/3$.
3. **Solution set.** Since the inequality is strict, the endpoints do not enter:
$$x \in \left(\tfrac{\pi}{3},\ \tfrac{2\pi}{3}\right).$$

**Check:** At $x = \pi/2$ (middle of the interval): $\sin(\pi/2) = 1 > \sqrt 3/2$. At $x = 0$ (outside): $\sin(0) = 0 < \sqrt 3/2$. Correct.

**Source.** [Stitz–Zeager — Precalculus §10.7](https://www.stitz-zeager.com/szprecalculus07042013.pdf) — CC-BY-NC-SA license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Tides as a sine wave (real-world modeling)" numero="5">

**Problem:** The height of the tide at a port is modeled by $h(t) = 3 \sin\!\bigl(\tfrac{\pi t}{6}\bigr)$ meters, with $t$ in hours from 6 AM. At which times in the interval $[0, 12]$ hours does the tide reach $h = 1.5$ m?

**Strategy:** Equating the model to the desired value reduces the problem to a basic sine equation. Restricting to $[0, 12]$ corresponds to one full cycle of the argument.

**Solution:**

1. **Equation.** $3 \sin(\pi t/6) = 1.5 \Leftrightarrow \sin(\pi t/6) = 1/2$.
2. **Solve $\sin u = 1/2$** with $u = \pi t/6$. The basic solutions on $[0, 2\pi)$ are $u = \pi/6$ and $u = 5\pi/6$.
3. **Return to $t$.** $\pi t/6 = \pi/6 \Rightarrow t = 1$. $\pi t/6 = 5\pi/6 \Rightarrow t = 5$.
4. **Check the interval.** Both $t = 1$ and $t = 5$ are in $[0, 12]$.

$$t = 1\ \text{h} \quad \text{or} \quad t = 5\ \text{h}.$$

**Check:** $h(1) = 3 \sin(\pi/6) = 3 \cdot 0.5 = 1.5$. $h(5) = 3 \sin(5\pi/6) = 3 \cdot 0.5 = 1.5$. Correct.

**Source.** [Active Calculus §0.7](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-0-7-trig.html) — Boelkins · CC-BY-NC-SA.
</Exemplo>
