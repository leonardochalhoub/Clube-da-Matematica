### Trigonometry — structure

<Definicao titulo="Fundamental identities of Trim 2">

Let $\theta \in \mathbb{R}$ (angle in radians). The three central identities are:

$$\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1 \quad \text{(Pythagorean)}$$

$$\sin(A \pm B) = \sin A \cos B \pm \cos A \sin B \quad \text{(addition)}$$

$$\sin A + \sin B = 2\sin\!\frac{A+B}{2}\cos\!\frac{A-B}{2} \quad \text{(sum-to-product)}$$

For any triangles ($ABC$ with sides $a, b, c$ opposite angles $A, B, C$):

$$\frac{\sin A}{a} = \frac{\sin B}{b} = \frac{\sin C}{c} \quad \text{(law of sines)}$$

$$c^2 = a^2 + b^2 - 2ab\cos C \quad \text{(law of cosines)}$$

</Definicao>

> "Sum-to-product transforms the addition of sines into a product of sines and cosines — essential for simplifying equations with multiple angles." — [OpenStax Algebra and Trigonometry 2e, §9.4](https://openstax.org/books/algebra-and-trigonometry-2e/pages/9-4-sum-to-product-and-product-to-sum-formulas)

<Teorema titulo="Law of sines — elementary proof">

In any triangle $ABC$, draw the altitude $h$ from vertex $C$ to side $AB$. Then:
- In the left sub-triangle: $h = b \sin A$.
- In the right sub-triangle: $h = a \sin B$.

Equating: $b \sin A = a \sin B$, hence $\dfrac{\sin A}{a} = \dfrac{\sin B}{b}$. Repeating with the altitude from $A$ yields $\dfrac{\sin C}{c}$. $\square$

</Teorema>

### Sequences — structure

<Definicao titulo="AP, GP, and intuitive limit">

**Arithmetic progression (AP):** $a_n = a_1 + (n-1)r$. The sum of the first $n$ terms is:

$$S_n = \frac{n(a_1 + a_n)}{2}$$

**Geometric progression (GP):** $a_n = a_1 q^{n-1}$. For $q \neq 1$, the partial sum is:

$$S_n = a_1\frac{q^n - 1}{q - 1}$$

**Infinite geometric series:** if $|q| < 1$, the series converges:

$$S_\infty = \frac{a_1}{1 - q}$$

**Intuitive limit:** the sequence $(a_n)$ has limit $L$ if, from some index onward, all terms are as close to $L$ as we wish. Notation: $\lim_{n\to\infty} a_n = L$.

</Definicao>

> "The key idea: if $|r| < 1$, the geometric series converges to $\dfrac{a}{1-r}$." — [OpenStax Algebra and Trigonometry 2e, §11.4](https://openstax.org/books/algebra-and-trigonometry-2e/pages/11-4-series-and-their-notations)

> "A sequence &#123;a_n&#125; converges to $L$ if for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists an index $N$ such that $|a_n - L| < \varepsilon$ for all $n \geq N$." — [Active Calculus §8.2](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-8-2-sequences.html)

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

You spent Trim 2 learning three "games":

**Game 1 — the spinning clock** (trigonometry). Imagine a clock hand that spins. Its position up-and-down is the **sine**, and left-and-right is the **cosine**. Any time you ask, the sum of the two positions squared is always 1. Always!

**Game 2 — the ruler** (AP and GP). You make a line of numbers following a rule. In an AP the rule is "always add the same thing" (3, 7, 11, 15...). In a GP it is "always multiply by the same thing" (2, 6, 18, 54...).

**Game 3 — where does it go?** (limit). If you sum increasingly smaller numbers from a GP, the sum stops at some place. The sequence $1, 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, \ldots$ keeps getting closer and closer to **2** — and stays there.

Today you play all three games at the same time!

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

In Trim 2 you learned three big tools:

**1. Trigonometry** — sines, cosines, the unit circle. It is used to measure angles, calculate distances in triangles, and model waves (sound, light, tide).

**2. Sequences** — AP and GP. An AP adds the same thing at each step (salary with a fixed raise of US$ 200). A GP multiplies by the same factor (bank balance at 5% per month).

**3. Limit** — where does a sequence that continues forever go? The GP with ratio $1/2$: $1 + 1/2 + 1/4 + 1/8 + \ldots$ keeps getting closer to $2$, and never passes it.

The workshop problems mix all three:
- "A sine wave is dampened each cycle — when does it reach zero?" (trig + GP + limit)
- "A ball bounces, losing 30% at each bounce — total distance?" (infinite GP)
- "Tide in Salvador reaches 2.5 m at what time?" (periodic function + trig equation)

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old teen">

This is the review before Trim 3. The topics you must master:

**Trigonometry:**
- Exact values: $\sin 30° = 1/2$, $\sin 45° = \sqrt 2/2$, $\sin 60° = \sqrt 3/2$ (and symmetric cosines).
- Pythagorean identity: $\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1$ — appears in every problem.
- Equations: $\sin\theta = k$ has two solutions per period. Always reduce to the interval $[0, 2\pi)$.
- Law of sines when you have "angle + opposite side + another element". Law of cosines when you have "three sides" or "two sides + included angle".

**Sequences:**
- AP: general term $a_1 + (n-1)r$, sum $n(a_1 + a_n)/2$.
- GP: general term $a_1 q^{n-1}$, partial sum $a_1(q^n-1)/(q-1)$, infinite sum (if $|q| < 1$): $a_1/(1-q)$.
- Always check: in a GP, $|q| < 1$ is the convergence condition — without it there is no infinite sum.

**Intuitive limit:**
- $\lim a_n = L$: the terms approach $L$ without ever "stopping".
- Fixed point: if $a_{n+1} = f(a_n)$ converges to $L$, then $L = f(L)$.
- Squeeze theorem: if $b_n \leq a_n \leq c_n$ and $b_n, c_n \to L$, then $a_n \to L$.

**Strategy for mixed problems:**
1. Identify the type (triangle, wave, number list, limit).
2. List data and unknowns.
3. Choose the right formula.
4. Check the answer by substitution.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

This consolidation covers the **periodic** (trigonometry) and **discrete** (sequences) base that scaffolds Year 2.

### Connections to Year 2

| Trim 2 | Year 2 | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| Unit circle | $\frac{d}{dx}\sin x = \cos x$ | Taylor series of $\sin$ and $\cos$ at zero |
| Trig equations | $f'(x) = 0$ | Critical points of $\sin$ and $\cos$ |
| Infinite GP $a_1/(1-q)$ | Taylor series $1/(1-x) = \sum x^n$ | Generalized geometric sum |
| Intuitive limit | Formal limit $\varepsilon$-$\delta$ (Lesson 41) | Formalization of "approaches" |
| Recurrence fixed point | Newton's method, iteration | Roots via functional iteration |

### Cross-domain applications

- **Electrical engineering**: AC voltage = sinusoidal ($V(t) = V_0\sin\omega t$); pole analysis = discrete-time GP.
- **Finance**: compound interest = GP ($F = P(1+i)^n$); present value = discounted geometric series.
- **Signal processing**: Fourier series = sum of sines/cosines — foundation of JPEG, MP3, communications.
- **Mechanics**: damped fall = sine with decreasing exponential envelope.

### Most common pitfalls in this block

- **Deg vs. rad**: numeric code and calculator diverge if you mix them.
- **Condition $|q| < 1$**: infinite geometric series exists only if this is respected.
- **Bounded sequence $\neq$ convergent**: $(-1)^n$ is bounded but does not converge.
- **SSA ambiguous**: law of sines with two sides + opposite angle can give 0, 1, or 2 triangles — always check the discriminant.
- **Spurious solutions**: when squaring or dividing in a trig equation, verify in the original equation.

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

Trim 2 forms the **continuous + discrete vocabulary** that is essential. From here on the program bifurcates:

- **Continuous calculus** (Trim 5+): derivative and integral generalize rate of change and sums.
- **Discrete analysis** (Trim 8+): combinatorics, probability, series.

### Structural generalizations

**Sinusoidal → Fourier analysis.** Any function $f$ that is periodic and square-integrable decomposes as $f(x) = a_0 + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} (a_n \cos nx + b_n \sin nx)$. It is the direct generalization of the sum-to-product identity appearing in today's exercises. Universal tool in DSP, compression, partial differential equations.

**GP → Z-transform.** The Z-transform generalizes the geometric series: $X(z) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} x[n]\,z^{-n}$ converges if $|z| > R$ (analogous to $|q| < 1$). Used in digital filter design (IIR, FIR) and discrete control.

**Limit → general topology.** The intuitive notion of "approaches" formalizes in the $\varepsilon$-$\delta$ definition (Lesson 41) and later generalizes to metric spaces, Banach spaces, and Hilbert spaces — foundation of functional analysis and finite element methods.

### Historical context

The law of sines was known to Islamic mathematicians of the 10th century (Al-Battani, Ibn Muadh) before being systematized in Europe. The infinite geometric series appears in Archimedes (3rd century BC) as a method of exhaustion — precursor to the integral. The idea of a rigorous limit came only with Cauchy and Weierstrass in the 19th century.

## Worked examples

<Exemplo titulo="AP: general term and Gauss sum" numero="1">

**Problem.** AP with $a_1 = 3$ and $r = 5$. Calculate $a_{20}$ and $S_{20}$.

**Strategy.** Apply directly $a_n = a_1 + (n-1)r$ and $S_n = n(a_1 + a_n)/2$.

**Solution.**

1. **General term.** $a_{20} = 3 + 19 \cdot 5 = 3 + 95 = 98$.
2. **Sum.** $S_{20} = 20(3 + 98)/2 = 10 \cdot 101 = 1,010$.

**Verification.** Mean of terms: $(3 + 98)/2 = 50.5$. Sum $= 50.5 \times 20 = 1,010$. ✓

**Source.** [OpenStax Algebra and Trigonometry 2e, §11.2](https://openstax.org/books/algebra-and-trigonometry-2e/pages/11-2-arithmetic-sequences) — CC-BY 4.0.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Trig equation with multiple angle argument" numero="2">

**Problem.** Solve $\sin(2x) = 1/2$ in $[0, 2\pi)$.

**Strategy.** Substitute $u = 2x$, solve $\sin u = 1/2$ on the expanded interval $[0, 4\pi)$, then divide by 2.

**Solution.**

1. **Substitute $u = 2x$.** For $x \in [0, 2\pi)$, we have $u \in [0, 4\pi)$.
2. **Solve $\sin u = 1/2$.** In the 1st quadrant: $u_0 = \pi/6$. In the 2nd: $u_1 = 5\pi/6$. Adding $2\pi$: $u_2 = \pi/6 + 2\pi = 13\pi/6$ and $u_3 = 5\pi/6 + 2\pi = 17\pi/6$.
3. **Return to $x = u/2$.** $x = \pi/12,\; 5\pi/12,\; 13\pi/12,\; 17\pi/12$.

**Verification.** $\sin(2 \cdot \pi/12) = \sin(\pi/6) = 1/2$ ✓. $\sin(2 \cdot 13\pi/12) = \sin(13\pi/6) = \sin(\pi/6) = 1/2$ ✓.

**Source.** [OpenStax Algebra and Trigonometry 2e, §9.5](https://openstax.org/books/algebra-and-trigonometry-2e/pages/9-5-solving-trigonometric-equations) — CC-BY 4.0.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Infinite GP — bouncing ball" numero="3">

**Problem.** A ball drops from 10 m and at each bounce rises to 70% of the previous height. What is the total distance traveled (falling and rising)?

**Strategy.** Separate into two infinite GPs — the falls and the rises. Use $S_\infty = a_1/(1-q)$ for each.

**Solution.**

1. **Falls.** $10 + 7 + 4.9 + \ldots = 10/(1-0.7) = 10/0.3 \approx 33.33$ m.
2. **Rises.** $7 + 4.9 + \ldots = 7/(1-0.7) = 7/0.3 \approx 23.33$ m.
3. **Total.** $33.33 + 23.33 \approx 56.67$ m.

**Verification.** Consolidated formula: total $= h \cdot (1+q)/(1-q) = 10 \cdot 1.7/0.3 \approx 56.67$ m. ✓

**Source.** [Active Calculus §8.3](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-8-3-geometric.html) — CC-BY-NC-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Tide and trig equation" numero="4">

**Problem.** The tide height in Salvador is $h(t) = 1.5 + \sin(\pi t/6)$ (in meters, $t$ in hours). (a) When is $h = 1.5$? (b) When is $h$ maximum?

**Strategy.** (a) Solve $\sin(\pi t/6) = 0$. (b) Solve $\sin(\pi t/6) = 1$.

**Solution.**

1. **(a)** $\sin(\pi t/6) = 0 \Rightarrow \pi t/6 = k\pi \Rightarrow t = 6k$. In $0 \leq t \leq 24$: $t = 0, 6, 12, 18, 24$ hours.
2. **(b)** $\sin(\pi t/6) = 1 \Rightarrow \pi t/6 = \pi/2 + 2k\pi \Rightarrow t = 3 + 12k$. In one day: $t = 3$ h and $t = 15$ h.
3. **Maximum height.** $h_{\max} = 1.5 + 1 = 2.5$ m.

**Verification.** Period $T = 2\pi/(\pi/6) = 12$ h — two high tides per day, consistent with reality. ✓

**Source.** [Stitz–Zeager Precalculus §11.1](https://www.stitz-zeager.com/szprecalculus07042013.pdf) — CC-BY-NC-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Trig sum via sum-to-product identity" numero="5">

**Problem.** Solve $\sin x + \sin 2x + \sin 3x = 0$ in $[0, 2\pi)$.

**Strategy.** Group $\sin x + \sin 3x$ and apply the sum-to-product identity: $\sin A + \sin B = 2\sin\frac{A+B}{2}\cos\frac{A-B}{2}$.

**Solution.**

1. **Group.** $\sin x + \sin 3x = 2\sin 2x \cos x$ (with $A = 3x$, $B = x$, mean $= 2x$, semi-difference $= x$).
2. **Substitute.** $2\sin 2x \cos x + \sin 2x = \sin 2x(2\cos x + 1) = 0$.
3. **Case 1: $\sin 2x = 0$.** $2x = k\pi \Rightarrow x = k\pi/2$. In $[0, 2\pi)$: $x = 0, \pi/2, \pi, 3\pi/2$.
4. **Case 2: $2\cos x + 1 = 0$.** $\cos x = -1/2 \Rightarrow x = 2\pi/3$ or $x = 4\pi/3$.
5. **Solution set.** $x \in \{0,\; \pi/2,\; 2\pi/3,\; \pi,\; 4\pi/3,\; 3\pi/2\}$.

**Verification.** $x = 2\pi/3$: $\sin(2\pi/3) + \sin(4\pi/3) + \sin(2\pi) = \frac{\sqrt 3}{2} - \frac{\sqrt 3}{2} + 0 = 0$. ✓

**Source.** [OpenStax Algebra and Trigonometry 2e, §9.4](https://openstax.org/books/algebra-and-trigonometry-2e/pages/9-4-sum-to-product-and-product-to-sum-formulas) — CC-BY 4.0.

</Exemplo>
