<Definicao titulo="Law of Sines">

In any triangle $ABC$ with sides $a, b, c$ opposite angles $A, B, C$ respectively:

$$\frac{a}{\sin A} = \frac{b}{\sin B} = \frac{c}{\sin C} = 2R$$

where $R$ is the radius of the **circumscribed circle** to the triangle.

</Definicao>

<Definicao titulo="Law of Cosines">

In any triangle $ABC$:

$$c^2 = a^2 + b^2 - 2ab\cos C$$

and similarly for the other sides. When $C = 90°$, $\cos C = 0$ and we recover **Pythagoras**.

</Definicao>

> "The Law of Sines can be used to solve oblique triangles, which are non-right triangles." — [OpenStax Algebra and Trigonometry 2e §10.1](https://openstax.org/books/algebra-and-trigonometry-2e/pages/10-1-non-right-triangles-law-of-sines)

> "The Law of Cosines is a generalization of the Pythagorean Theorem. We can use it to find a missing side when two sides and the included angle are known." — [Stitz–Zeager, Precalculus §11.3](https://www.stitz-zeager.com/szprecalculus07042013.pdf)

<Teorema titulo="Proof of the Law of Sines (acute triangle)">
Draw the altitude $h$ from $C$ to side $\overline{AB}$. In the right triangle $ACH$: $h = b \sin A$. In the right triangle $BCH$: $h = a \sin B$. Equating:

$$b \sin A = a \sin B \implies \frac{a}{\sin A} = \frac{b}{\sin B}$$

Repeating with another altitude proves that this ratio also equals $c/\sin C$. For $C = 90°$: $\sin C = 1$, so $c = 2R$ — the hypotenuse is a diameter of the circumscribed circle (**Thales' Theorem**). ∎
</Teorema>

<Teorema titulo="Proof of the Law of Cosines (via dot product)">
Place the origin at $C$. Define $\vec{u} = \vec{CA}$ and $\vec{v} = \vec{CB}$, with $|\vec{u}| = b$, $|\vec{v}| = a$. Then $\vec{AB} = \vec{v} - \vec{u}$ and $|\vec{AB}| = c$.

$$c^2 = |\vec{v} - \vec{u}|^2 = |\vec{v}|^2 + |\vec{u}|^2 - 2\,\vec{u}\cdot\vec{v} = a^2 + b^2 - 2ab\cos C \qquad \square$$

This proof works for any triangle — acute, obtuse, or right. ∎
</Teorema>

### When to use each law

<Definicao titulo="Law selection guide">

| Known data | Unknown | Law to use |
|---|---|---|
| 2 angles + 1 side (AAS, ASA) | remaining sides | **Sines** |
| 2 sides + opposite angle (SSA) | remaining (may be ambiguous) | **Sines** |
| 2 sides + included angle (SAS) | third side | **Cosines** |
| 3 sides (SSS) | some angle | **Cosines** inverted |

**Ambiguous case SSA** (given $a$, $b$, $A$ with $A < 90°$): compute $h = b\sin A$.

- $a < h$: no triangle
- $a = h$ or $a \geq b$: exactly 1 triangle
- $h < a < b$: 2 triangles

</Definicao>

### Area of a triangle

<Equation explicacao="Heron's formula: area from just three sides, without needing angles. Semiperimeter: s = (a+b+c)/2." latex="\text{Area} = \sqrt{s(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)}" />

<Insight>

**The Law of Cosines is the dot product.** The identity $|\vec{v} - \vec{u}|^2 = |\vec{v}|^2 + |\vec{u}|^2 - 2\,\vec{u}\cdot\vec{v}$ is exactly the Law of Cosines when $\vec{u}\cdot\vec{v} = |\vec{u}||\vec{v}|\cos C$. In linear algebra, this is the *definition* of the dot product via norm and angle — and it is also the formula that generalizes "distance" to high-dimensional spaces (Mahalanobis distance, RBF kernel, etc.).

</Insight>

You want to know **how far away** that tree is over there, but you can't walk to it (there's a river). You walk partway to one side and mark it, then walk to another side and mark that. You look at the tree from both spots and measure the angles.

**With triangles**, you can find the distance without crossing the river. Mathematicians have two rules for this: **Law of Sines** and **Law of Cosines**. Triangulation. That's how GPS works!

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

Pythagoras only works in a right triangle. But what if the triangle is "any"? Like acute (all angles less than 90°) or obtuse (one angle bigger)?

Then you use the **Law of Sines** or **Law of Cosines**.

**Law of Sines**: for any triangle, the ratio $\dfrac{\text{side}}{\text{sine of opposite angle}}$ is the same. Like, if one side is 10 and the opposite angle is 30°, and another side is 14 and the opposite angle is $B$, then $14/\sin B = 10/\sin 30°$. Solve for the unknown.

**Law of Cosines**: generalization of Pythagoras. Instead of $c^2 = a^2 + b^2$, you have $c^2 = a^2 + b^2 - 2ab \cos C$. When $C = 90°$, $\cos 90° = 0$ and the last term vanishes — Pythagoras comes back.

**Where does it show up?** Land surveying (measuring property), navigation (GPS does triangulation), astronomy (calculating distances to stars), engineering (vectors and forces).

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old">

In Lesson 11 we learned to "solve" a right triangle: given one side and one angle, find the other sides.

But what if the triangle **doesn't have a 90° angle**? Pythagoras doesn't work, and right-triangle sine/cosine don't work either.

The solution: **Law of Sines** and **Law of Cosines**. They work in **any** triangle.

**Law of Sines**: the ratio of each side to the sine of its opposite angle is constant.
$$\frac{a}{\sin A} = \frac{b}{\sin B} = \frac{c}{\sin C}$$

**Law of Cosines**: $c^2 = a^2 + b^2 - 2ab\cos C$. It's Pythagoras "modified" by the cosine of the angle between sides $a$ and $b$.

In practice: you have 3 measurements (3 sides, or 2 sides and 1 angle, etc.) and want to find the rest. Apply the right law, do the computation.

**Example**: you walk 5 km due north, then turn 60° toward the east and walk another 3 km. How far are you from your starting point?

Using the Law of Cosines: $d^2 = 5^2 + 3^2 - 2 \cdot 5 \cdot 3 \cdot \cos(120°) = 25 + 9 + 15 = 49$. So $d = 7$ km.

(The interior angle of the triangle is $180° - 60° = 120°$ from your heading change.)

**Quick decision**: is there an angle between two sides? Use cosines. Are there two angles? Use sines.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

Law of Sines and Cosines are fundamental in:

- **Surveying / Cadastral mapping**: triangulation of inaccessible points.
- **Navigation**: GPS performs 3D triangulation combining 4+ satellites — a multidimensional version of the Law of Cosines.
- **Astronomy**: stellar parallax uses the Law of Sines.
- **Robotics**: inverse kinematics of 2-segment robot arms solves via Law of Cosines.

### Area of a triangle

$$\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2} ab \sin C$$

It comes from "base times height divided by 2" with height $= a \sin C$. When $C = 90°$, we recover "leg times leg divided by 2".

### Heron's formula

Given 3 sides $a, b, c$, with semiperimeter $s = (a+b+c)/2$:
$$\text{Area} = \sqrt{s(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)}$$

Provable by combining the Law of Cosines with the identity $\sin^2 + \cos^2 = 1$.

### Connection to vectors

The Law of Cosines is equivalent to the expansion $|\vec u - \vec v|^2 = |\vec u|^2 + |\vec v|^2 - 2 \vec u \cdot \vec v$ — in linear algebra, it is the very definition of the dot product via norm and angle.

### Solution by matrix (preview)

Given a triangle defined by vectors $\vec u, \vec v$:
- Opposite side: $|\vec u - \vec v|$
- Angle between: $\arccos(\vec u \cdot \vec v / (|\vec u| |\vec v|))$
- Area: $|\vec u \times \vec v|/2$ (in 3D), or $|u_1 v_2 - u_2 v_1|/2$ (in 2D).

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

Law of Sines and Cosines are a special case of the **generalized Pythagorean theorem** on a Riemannian manifold, or of the **parallelogram law** in spaces with an inner product.

### Generalizations in non-Euclidean geometry

In spherical geometry (radius $R$):
$$\cos(c/R) = \cos(a/R)\cos(b/R) + \sin(a/R)\sin(b/R) \cos C$$

It approaches the plane Law of Cosines as $R \to \infty$ (Taylor to 2nd order).

In hyperbolic geometry:
$$\cosh c = \cosh a \cosh b - \sinh a \sinh b \cos C$$

### Application in GNSS / GPS

A receiver measures signal arrival time from each satellite (pseudorange). Trilateration solves the system of equations $|\vec p - \vec s_i| = c (t_i - \delta)$ with $\vec p$ position, $\vec s_i$ satellites, $\delta$ clock offset. Linearizing around an initial guess via gradient of the norm — exactly the Law of Cosines differentiated.

### Numerical

```python
import numpy as np
import sympy as sp

# Law of Cosines numerically:
a, b, c = 7, 8, 9
C = np.arccos((a**2 + b**2 - c**2) / (2*a*b))
print(np.degrees(C))   # ~73.4°

# Heron:
s = (a + b + c) / 2
area = np.sqrt(s*(s-a)*(s-b)*(s-c))
print(area)   # ~26.83

# Symbolic:
A, B, c = sp.symbols('A B c', positive=True)
sp.solve(sp.Eq(c**2, A**2 + B**2 - 2*A*B*sp.cos(sp.pi/3)), c)
```

### Pitfalls

- **SSA ambiguity**: can give 0, 1, or 2 solutions — a classic source of error in navigation problems.
- **Heron with "skinny" triangle**: if $a + b \approx c$, then $s - c$ is small and there is numerical cancellation. Use **numerically stable formula** by Kahan: order $a \geq b \geq c$ and compute $\sqrt{(a + (b+c))(c - (a-b))(c + (a-b))(a + (b-c))}/4$.
- **Calculator in deg vs rad**: as always.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practical application">

**Recipe: given any triangle, find all sides/angles.**

1. **List what you have** (angles, sides).
2. **Count the data**: 3 independent pieces of information define the triangle (up to SSA ambiguity).
3. **Choose the law**:
   - 2 angles? → Law of Sines.
   - 2 sides + angle between them? → Law of Cosines.
   - 3 sides? → Law of Cosines inverted.
4. **Apply it**.
5. **Use the angle sum** $A + B + C = \pi$ to close.

### Workflow Wolfram / SymPy / NumPy

```python
import numpy as np
import sympy as sp

# Example: a = 8, A = 30°, B = 45°, find b
A_rad = np.radians(30)
B_rad = np.radians(45)
a = 8
b = a * np.sin(B_rad) / np.sin(A_rad)
print(b)   # ~11.3137

# Law of Cosines with SymPy:
a, b, c, C = sp.symbols('a b c C', positive=True)
expr = sp.sqrt(a**2 + b**2 - 2*a*b*sp.cos(C))
print(expr.subs({a: 5, b: 7, C: sp.pi/3}))   # sqrt(39)
```

### When to worry?

- **Ambiguous SSA case**: always check whether there are 0, 1, or 2 triangles before proceeding.
- **Degenerate triangle**: if $a + b = c$, it is a line segment (not a triangle). $a + b < c$ is impossible.
- **Angle sum $\neq 180°$** after calculation: you have numerical error (likely rounding) or a sign error in cosine.
- **In field triangulation**: measure with a nominal error of 1°. Error propagation can grow to 5° at large distances.

## Worked examples

> Five examples with increasing difficulty — from the most direct (Law of Sines AAS) to real modeling (inverse kinematics of a robot arm). Each example cites its source: the original problem always comes from an open book.

<Exemplo titulo="Law of Sines AAS (application)" numero="1">

**Problem:** In triangle $ABC$, $A = 30°$, $B = 45°$ and side $a = 8$. Calculate side $b$.

**Strategy:** We have two angles and one side opposite one of them (case AAS). The Law of Sines solves directly: set up the ratio and isolate $b$.

**Solution:**

1. **Apply the Law of Sines.** $\dfrac{a}{\sin A} = \dfrac{b}{\sin B}$, or $\dfrac{8}{\sin 30°} = \dfrac{b}{\sin 45°}$.
2. **Substitute known values.** $\sin 30° = 1/2$, $\sin 45° = \sqrt 2 / 2$.
3. **Isolate $b$.** $b = 8 \cdot \dfrac{\sin 45°}{\sin 30°} = 8 \cdot \dfrac{\sqrt 2 / 2}{1/2} = 8\sqrt 2 \approx 11.31$.

$$b = 8\sqrt 2 \approx 11.31.$$

**Verification:** Since $B > A$, we must have $b > a$. Indeed $11.31 > 8$. It checks out.

**Source.** [OpenStax — Algebra and Trigonometry 2e §10.1](https://openstax.org/books/algebra-and-trigonometry-2e/pages/10-1-non-right-triangles-law-of-sines) — CC-BY 4.0 license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Law of Cosines SAS (application)" numero="2">

**Problem:** A triangle has sides $a = 5$, $b = 7$ and the angle between them $C = 60°$. Calculate the third side $c$.

**Strategy:** We have two sides and the angle between them (SAS). This is exactly the case for the Law of Cosines to find the side opposite the given angle.

**Solution:**

1. **Apply the law.** $c^2 = a^2 + b^2 - 2ab \cos C$.
2. **Substitute.** $c^2 = 25 + 49 - 2 \cdot 5 \cdot 7 \cdot \cos 60° = 74 - 70 \cdot \tfrac{1}{2} = 74 - 35 = 39$.
3. **Take the square root.** $c = \sqrt{39} \approx 6.24$.

**Verification:** $c$ must lie between $|a - b| = 2$ and $a + b = 12$ (triangle inequality). $6.24 \in (2, 12)$. It checks out.

**Source.** [Stitz–Zeager — Precalculus §11.3](https://www.stitz-zeager.com/szprecalculus07042013.pdf) — CC-BY-NC-SA license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Law of Cosines inverted — find angle from 3 sides (intermediate)" numero="3">

**Problem:** A triangle has sides $a = 5$, $b = 6$, $c = 7$. Calculate angle $C$.

**Strategy:** SSS — three sides known. Use the Law of Cosines solved for $\cos C$ and apply $\arccos$.

**Solution:**

1. **Rewrite the Law of Cosines.** $\cos C = \dfrac{a^2 + b^2 - c^2}{2ab}$.
2. **Substitute.** $\cos C = \dfrac{25 + 36 - 49}{2 \cdot 5 \cdot 6} = \dfrac{12}{60} = \dfrac{1}{5} = 0.2$.
3. **Apply arccos.** $C = \arccos(0.2) \approx 78.46°$.

$$C \approx 78.46°.$$

**Verification:** Since the triangle is "almost" isosceles and $c$ is the longest side, we expect $C$ to be the largest angle. By the Law of Cosines: $A = \arccos((36+49-25)/(2\cdot 6 \cdot 7)) = \arccos(60/84) \approx 44.42°$, $B \approx 57.12°$. Sum: $44.42 + 57.12 + 78.46 \approx 180°$. It checks out.

**Source.** [OpenStax — Algebra and Trigonometry 2e §10.2](https://openstax.org/books/algebra-and-trigonometry-2e/pages/10-2-non-right-triangles-law-of-cosines) — CC-BY 4.0 license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Ambiguous case SSA (advanced)" numero="4">

**Problem:** A triangle has $a = 5$, $b = 8$, $A = 30°$. How many triangles satisfy these conditions? Find them all.

**Strategy:** SSA is the ambiguous case. Calculate $h = b \sin A$ and compare with $a$ to decide among 0, 1, or 2 triangles. Then use the Law of Sines for each possibility.

**Solution:**

1. **Calculate $h$.** $h = b \sin A = 8 \cdot \sin 30° = 8 \cdot 0.5 = 4$.
2. **Decide the number of solutions.** Since $h = 4 < a = 5 < b = 8$, there are **two triangles**.
3. **Apply the Law of Sines.** $\dfrac{\sin B}{b} = \dfrac{\sin A}{a} \Rightarrow \sin B = \dfrac{8 \cdot 0.5}{5} = 0.8$.
4. **Find the two angles $B$.** $B_1 = \arcsin(0.8) \approx 53.13°$ and $B_2 = 180° - 53.13° \approx 126.87°$.
5. **Calculate $C$ in each case.** $C_1 = 180° - 30° - 53.13° = 96.87°$. $C_2 = 180° - 30° - 126.87° = 23.13°$.
6. **Calculate $c$ in each case.** $c_1 = a \cdot \sin C_1 / \sin A = 5 \cdot \sin 96.87° / 0.5 \approx 9.93$. $c_2 = 5 \cdot \sin 23.13° / 0.5 \approx 3.93$.

**Verification:** In each triangle, $A + B + C = 180°$ and the triangle inequality holds (all sides positive with $|a-b| < c < a+b$). It checks out for both.

**Source.** [Stitz–Zeager — Precalculus §11.2](https://www.stitz-zeager.com/szprecalculus07042013.pdf) — CC-BY-NC-SA license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Surveying — distance across the third vertex (real modeling)" numero="5">

**Problem:** To measure the distance $AB$ between two points separated by a river, a surveyor chooses a point $C$ on his bank with $AC = 50$ m, $BC = 70$ m and measures the angle $\hat{ACB} = 60°$. Calculate $AB$.

**Strategy:** Classic SAS scenario — two sides $AC$ and $BC$ known and the angle between them. The Law of Cosines solves directly for the opposite side $AB$.

**Solution:**

1. **Identify the configuration.** Triangle $ABC$ with sides $b = AC = 50$ m, $a = BC = 70$ m and angle $C = 60°$ between them. I want the third side $c = AB$.
2. **Apply the Law of Cosines.** $c^2 = a^2 + b^2 - 2ab\cos C = 70^2 + 50^2 - 2 \cdot 70 \cdot 50 \cdot \cos 60°$.
3. **Calculate.** $c^2 = 4900 + 2500 - 7000 \cdot 0.5 = 7400 - 3500 = 3900$.
4. **Take the square root.** $c = \sqrt{3900} = 10\sqrt{39} \approx 62.45$ m.

$$AB \approx 62.45 \text{ m}.$$

**Verification:** Since angle $C$ is acute and sides $a, b$ are not too different, the opposite side should be comparable to them. $62.45$ lies between $|70-50| = 20$ and $70+50 = 120$, within the triangle inequality. It checks out.

**Source.** [OpenStax — University Physics Vol. 1, ch. 2](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/2-introduction) — CC-BY 4.0 license.
</Exemplo>
