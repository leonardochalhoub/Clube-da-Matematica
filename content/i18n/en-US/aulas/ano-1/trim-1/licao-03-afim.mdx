<Porta nivel="formal" titulo="Definition and properties">

<Definicao titulo="Affine function">
$f: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ is **affine** if there exist $a, b \in \mathbb{R}$ such that $f(x) = ax + b$.
</Definicao>

- $a$: **slope coefficient** (or angle of inclination)
- $b$: **vertical intercept** (y-intercept)
- Graph: a straight line. $a > 0$: increasing. $a < 0$: decreasing. $a = 0$: constant.

<Equation numero="1"
  audioTexto="Equation one. F of x two minus f of x one, divided by x two minus x one, equals a. The rate of change between any two points of an affine function is constant and equal to the slope coefficient."
  explicacao="Rate of change between two points on the line. For an affine function, this value is CONSTANT — it does not depend on which pair of points you choose. This constancy is what distinguishes the affine function among all others." latex="\frac{f(x_2) - f(x_1)}{x_2 - x_1} = a" />

> "The slope of a line passing through two points $(x_1, y_1)$ and $(x_2, y_2)$ is $m = (y_2 - y_1)/(x_2 - x_1)$." — [OpenStax College Algebra 2e, §2.2](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/2-2-linear-equations-in-one-variable)

### Zero of the function and intercepts

$f(x) = 0 \iff x = -b/a$ (when $a \neq 0$). The pair $(0, b)$ is the **vertical intercept**. The pair $(-b/a, 0)$ is the **zero** (or horizontal intercept).

### Uniqueness theorem for two points

<Teorema titulo="Two points determine a unique affine function">
Given two distinct points $(x_1, y_1)$ and $(x_2, y_2)$ with $x_1 \neq x_2$, there exists a unique affine function $f(x) = ax + b$ passing through both. The coefficients are:
$$a = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1}, \quad b = y_1 - a x_1$$
</Teorema>

**Proof (sketch).** Existence: define $a$ by the formula above and $b = y_1 - a x_1$. It is verified that $f(x_1) = y_1$ by construction, and $f(x_2) = a(x_2 - x_1) + y_1 = (y_2 - y_1) + y_1 = y_2$. Uniqueness: if $g(x) = a' x + b'$ also satisfies $g(x_i) = y_i$, then $a' = (y_2 - y_1)/(x_2 - x_1) = a$ and $b' = y_1 - a' x_1 = b$. ∎

### Composition and operations

Let $f(x) = a_1 x + b_1$ and $g(x) = a_2 x + b_2$. Then:

- **Sum**: $(f + g)(x) = (a_1 + a_2) x + (b_1 + b_2)$ — affine, with slopes added.
- **Composition**: $(f \circ g)(x) = a_1 (a_2 x + b_2) + b_1 = a_1 a_2 x + (a_1 b_2 + b_1)$ — affine, with slopes multiplied.
- **Inverse** (if $a_1 \neq 0$): $f^{-1}(y) = (y - b_1)/a_1$ — also affine, with slope $1/a_1$.

The set of invertible affine functions ($a \neq 0$) under composition forms a group — the structure $(\mathbb{R}^* \times \mathbb{R}, \circ)$. This observation will be used in linear algebra (Lesson 31+) and affine geometry.

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

Imagine a staircase. Each step has **the same height**.

- If each step is 20 cm high, after 1 step you've climbed 20 cm.
- After 2 steps, you've climbed 40 cm.
- After 5 steps, you've climbed 100 cm = 1 meter.

The height you're at depends **on how many steps you've climbed**. And it always grows **the same amount** per step.

That's an affine function. The height is $f(n) = 20 \cdot n$ where $n$ is the number of steps.

If the staircase started on a platform 50 cm above the ground (like a small stage), then it would be $f(n) = 20n + 50$. The 50 is the **starting point** (where you are before climbing anything). The 20 is **how much you climb per step**.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

You go to the movies. Entry costs **$30 fixed**. Each bag of popcorn costs **$8**. If you buy $n$ bags:

$$\text{total spending} = 30 + 8n$$

That's an affine function! Where:
- The **30** is the spending **even if you buy 0 popcorn** (entry). That's the **b** in the formula $f(x) = ax + b$.
- The **8** is how much your bill **increases with each extra bag of popcorn**. That's the **a** in the formula.

Whenever you have a function with "a fixed part + a part that grows at a constant rate," you have an affine function. Other examples:

- **Uber fare**: flat fee ($6 fixed) + $2 per km. ⟹ $f(d) = 6 + 2d$.
- **Electric bill**: availability fee ($25) + $0.80 per kWh. ⟹ $f(k) = 25 + 0.80\,k$.
- **Scooter rental**: $3 to unlock + $0.80 per minute.

The line on the graph **represents** this formula visually. The steeper the line, the faster your bill grows.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old">

The affine function is the simplest type of "interesting" function that exists — and it's the one that opens the door to calculus.

The rule is: $f(x) = ax + b$. You put in a value of $x$, multiply by $a$, add $b$, and out comes $f(x)$. That simple.

**Why does it matter?** Because the graph is a **straight line**, and the slope of that line is exactly $a$. When you move **1 step to the right** on the $x$-axis, you move **a steps up on the y-axis**. Always. At any point on the line. This **constancy** is what makes the affine function "linear" in behavior.

In a few more lessons you'll study **instantaneous rate of change** (the derivative). And you'll see that: for an affine function, the derivative is constant and equal to $a$. For other functions (parabolas, exponentials), the "slope" changes with $x$ — and that's exactly where calculus shows up. But for a line, it's easy: the slope is $a$, end of story.

**Concrete example.** You drive a car at a constant speed of 60 km/h. The distance covered is $d(t) = 60t$ (with $t$ in hours). The "slope" $a = 60$ is the speed. It makes sense — the steeper the line, the faster you go.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

The affine function is the point where most physical models are **linearized locally**. Even nonlinear phenomena (free fall with air resistance, non-Newtonian dynamics) are, to first order, approximated by affine functions — this is what the derivative formalizes.

### Slope-intercept form vs. general form

- Slope-intercept: $y = ax + b$
- General: $Ax + By + C = 0$
- Parametric: $\{(x_0 + t\vec{u}_x, y_0 + t\vec{u}_y) : t \in \mathbb{R}\}$

Each is useful in different contexts. In CAD, parametric. In linear optimization, general form. In statistics (regression), slope-intercept.

### Fitting lines (preview of linear regression)

Given $(x_1, y_1), \ldots, (x_n, y_n)$, which line best fits through them? **Least squares**:

$$a = \frac{n \sum x_i y_i - \sum x_i \sum y_i}{n \sum x_i^2 - (\sum x_i)^2}, \quad b = \bar{y} - a \bar{x}$$

You'll see this in detail in Trimester 11 (inferential statistics).

### Applications (partial list)

- **Hooke's law**: $F = kx$ — elastic force as a function of stretch (affine with $b = 0$, i.e., linear)
- **Ohm's law**: $V = RI$ — voltage as a function of current (linear)
- **Celsius to Fahrenheit conversion**: $F = 1.8C + 32$ (affine, $a = 1.8$, $b = 32$)
- **Equation of motion for uniform motion**: $s(t) = s_0 + vt$ (affine in $t$, $b = s_0$, $a = v$)

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

In practice, **"linear is local."** Most of the technical work in engineering involving models boils down to, at the end of the day, fitting lines:

- **Parameter estimation** in control engineering: plant identification via sinusoidal excitation and linear fitting in the Laplace domain.
- **Taylor linearization**: any smooth function near $x_0$ is locally affine — $f(x) \approx f(x_0) + f'(x_0)(x - x_0)$. All stability analysis of dynamical systems depends on this.
- **Ordinary least squares (OLS) regression**: the line minimizing squared error for $n$ points is the backbone of econometrics (GDP ~ investment), pharmacology (dose ~ response), agronomy (rainfall ~ harvest).
- **Logistic regression**: the "linearization" of log-odds — the logarithm of the odds ratio is an affine function of the covariates. Every AI system that classifies (spam filter, fraud detection) has logistic regression at its heart.

### Caution: heteroscedasticity and spurious linearity

In production, fitting $y = ax + b$ to data without checking residuals is a recipe for disaster. The prerequisites of regression (Gauss–Markov):

1. The relationship is actually linear — not just the data.
2. Residuals have constant variance (homoscedasticity).
3. Residuals are uncorrelated.
4. Residuals have mean zero.

When these fail, $\hat{a}$ is still an estimator, but loses efficiency or consistency. **OLS without checks is cargo cult**.

### Linearization vs. transformation

When the relationship is NOT affine, there are two ways out: (a) fit with a nonlinear model, (b) **transform variables** until it becomes affine. Classical examples:

- $y = a x^b$ ⟹ $\ln y = \ln a + b \ln x$ — log-log linear (power law)
- $y = a e^{bx}$ ⟹ $\ln y = \ln a + bx$ — semi-log linear (exponential)
- $y = a/x$ ⟹ $y = a \cdot (1/x)$ — affine in $1/x$

Plotting on log-log or semi-log scale and checking if it becomes a line is the first diagnostic tool.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practice — 3 real scenarios">

### Scenario 1: CEMIG electric bill (Minas Gerais, April/2026)

Residential tariff class B1 (estimated): $0.82/kWh + yellow flag $0.02/kWh + 25% ICMS on consumption + fixed availability fee $35.40 (three-phase residence). For consumption $k$ (kWh):

$$C(k) = (0.82 + 0.02) \cdot 1.25 \cdot k + 35.40 = 1.05 k + 35.40$$

**Affine function**, with $a = 1.05$ $/kWh and $b = 35.40$ $. For $k = 200$ kWh, the bill is $C(200) = 245.40$ dollars.

**Marginal analysis**: each additional kWh costs exactly $1.05. The "fixed portion" of $35.40 does not depend on consumption — it's what you pay **even if you travel the whole month with the breaker off**.

### Scenario 2: Crack propagation velocity (fracture mechanics)

In fatigue testing (Paris curve), the propagation of a crack in metal can be linearized as:

$$\log(da/dN) = \log C + m \log(\Delta K)$$

where $da/dN$ is the crack propagation speed per cycle, $\Delta K$ is the stress intensity factor range, and $C, m$ are material constants. On a log-log scale this is a straight line with slope $m$ — called the **Paris exponent**.

For typical structural steel, $m \approx 3$. This means: doubling $\Delta K$ multiplies the propagation speed by $2^3 = 8$ — drastically nonlinear behavior that becomes linear on the log-log scale.

### Scenario 3: Aggregate demand curve (microeconomics)

The demand for a good is often modeled as an affine function: $Q(p) = \alpha - \beta p$ where $\alpha, \beta > 0$ and $p$ is price. The price elasticity of demand is $\epsilon = -\beta p / Q$ — it varies along the curve, even though the function is affine.

Numerical example: $Q(p) = 1000 - 25 p$. For $p = 20$, $Q = 500$, elasticity $|\epsilon| = 25 \cdot 20 / 500 = 1$ (unit elastic). For $p = 30$, $Q = 250$, elasticity $|\epsilon| = 3$ (elastic). For $p = 10$, $Q = 750$, $|\epsilon| = 0.33$ (inelastic). **Operational note**: a company that raises the price of an inelastic good gains revenue; for an elastic good, it loses revenue.

<Exemplo titulo="Example 1 — Identify coefficients and zero (application)" numero="1">

**Problem:** For $f(x) = -\dfrac{2}{3} x + 4$, determine: slope coefficient $a$, vertical intercept $b$, zero of the function, and the value of $f(6)$.

**Strategy:** Direct comparison with slope-intercept form $y = ax + b$. The zero is $x_0 = -b/a$; $f(6)$ is direct numerical evaluation.

**Solution:**

1.  **Coefficients.** Comparing: $a = -2/3$, $b = 4$.
2.  **Zero.** $f(x) = 0 \iff -\tfrac{2}{3}x + 4 = 0 \iff x = 6$.
3.  **$f(6)$.** $f(6) = -\tfrac{2}{3}(6) + 4 = -4 + 4 = 0$. This agrees with item 2 — $x = 6$ is the zero.

**Verification:** Since $a < 0$, the function is decreasing. At $x = 0$, $f(0) = 4$ (vertical intercept). At $x = 6$, it crosses the x-axis. ✓

**Source.** [OpenStax — College Algebra 2e §2.2](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/2-2-linear-equations-in-one-variable) — CC-BY 4.0 license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Example 2 — Line through two points (intermediate)" numero="2">

**Problem:** Determine the equation of the affine function whose graph passes through $(2, -1)$ and $(5, 8)$.

**Strategy:** Apply the formula $a = (y_2 - y_1)/(x_2 - x_1)$ for the slope, then use one of the points to find $b$.

**Solution:**

1.  **Slope.** $a = \dfrac{8 - (-1)}{5 - 2} = \dfrac{9}{3} = 3$.
2.  **Intercept.** Using point $(2, -1)$: $-1 = 3 \cdot 2 + b \Rightarrow b = -7$.
3.  **Equation.** $f(x) = 3x - 7$.

**Verification:** $f(2) = 6 - 7 = -1$ ✓ and $f(5) = 15 - 7 = 8$ ✓.

**Source.** [OpenStax — College Algebra 2e §2.2](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/2-2-linear-equations-in-one-variable) — CC-BY 4.0 license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Example 3 — Parallel and perpendicular lines (intermediate)" numero="3">

**Problem:** Determine the equation of the line parallel to $y = -2x + 7$ that passes through $(3, 1)$, and the equation of the line perpendicular to this same line passing through the same point.

**Strategy:** Parallel lines have the **same** slope ($a' = a$). Perpendicular lines have slopes whose product is $-1$ ($a' = -1/a$). In both cases, find $b'$ using the given point.

**Solution:**

1.  **Parallel line.** Slope $a' = -2$. Using $(3, 1)$: $1 = -2(3) + b' \Rightarrow b' = 7$. Equation: $y = -2x + 7$. (Wait — this coincides with the original line. This happens because $(3, 1)$ lies on the original line.)
2.  **Verification.** $-2(3) + 7 = 1$. ✓ In fact, $(3, 1)$ belongs to the original line; the only parallel line is itself.
3.  **Perpendicular line.** Slope $a' = -1/(-2) = 1/2$. Using $(3, 1)$: $1 = (1/2)(3) + b' \Rightarrow b' = -1/2$. Equation: $y = \tfrac{1}{2}x - \tfrac{1}{2}$.

**Verification:** At $x = 3$: $\tfrac{1}{2}(3) - \tfrac{1}{2} = 1$. ✓ Product of slopes: $(-2) \cdot (1/2) = -1$. ✓

**Source.** [Stitz–Zeager — Precalculus §2.1](https://www.stitz-zeager.com/szprecalculus07042013.pdf) — CC-BY-NC-SA license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Example 4 — Composition of affine functions is affine (advanced)" numero="4">

**Problem:** Let $f(x) = 2x + 1$ and $g(x) = -3x + 5$. Calculate $f \circ g$ and $g \circ f$ and show that both are affine. Determine the resulting coefficients in each case.

**Strategy:** Substitute one function into the other and simplify to slope-intercept form. The composition of two affine functions is always affine — a key property that allows us to construct groups of transformations.

**Solution:**

1.  **$f \circ g$.** $f(g(x)) = f(-3x + 5) = 2(-3x + 5) + 1 = -6x + 10 + 1 = -6x + 11$.
    Coefficients: $a = -6$, $b = 11$. Note that the resulting $a$ is the product $2 \cdot (-3) = -6$.
2.  **$g \circ f$.** $g(f(x)) = g(2x + 1) = -3(2x + 1) + 5 = -6x - 3 + 5 = -6x + 2$.
    Coefficients: $a = -6$, $b = 2$.
3.  **Observation.** Both have the same slope $-6$ (product of the individual $a$'s — commutative) but different intercepts (not commutative in the constant term).

**Verification:** At $x = 0$: $(f \circ g)(0) = f(5) = 11$ ✓; $(g \circ f)(0) = g(1) = 2$ ✓.

**Source.** [OpenStax — College Algebra 2e §5.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/5-1-linear-functions) — CC-BY 4.0 license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Example 5 — Real-world modeling: comparing taxi plans (real modeling)" numero="5">

**Problem:** Two taxi companies compete in the same city. **Company X**: $6.00 flat fee + $2.80/km. **Company Y**: $4.00 flat fee + $3.50/km. Starting from what distance is Company X cheaper?

**Strategy:** Model each cost as an affine function, set them equal to find the break-even point, and analyze the comparison for larger and smaller distances.

**Solution:**

1.  **Models.** $C_X(d) = 6 + 2.80\,d$ and $C_Y(d) = 4 + 3.50\,d$.
2.  **Break-even point.** Set equal: $6 + 2.80\,d = 4 + 3.50\,d \Rightarrow 2 = 0.70\,d \Rightarrow d = 2/0.70 \approx 2.86$ km.
3.  **Comparison.** For $d < 2.86$ km, Company Y is cheaper (lower flat fee makes up for it). For $d > 2.86$ km, Company X is cheaper (lower rate per km makes up for it).

**Verification:** At $d = 5$ km: $C_X = 6 + 14 = 20.00$ dollars; $C_Y = 4 + 17.50 = 21.50$ dollars. ✓ X is cheaper. At $d = 1$ km: $C_X = 8.80$, $C_Y = 7.50$. ✓ Y is cheaper.

**Source.** [Yoshiwara — Modeling, Functions, and Graphs ch. 1](https://textbooks.aimath.org/textbooks/approved-textbooks/yoshiwara/) — free license.
</Exemplo>
