<Porta nivel="formal" titulo="Definition and properties">

<Definicao titulo="Linear (affine) function">
$f: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ is **affine** if there exist $a, b \in \mathbb{R}$ such that $f(x) = ax + b$.
</Definicao>

- $a$: **slope** (angular coefficient, inclination)
- $b$: **y-intercept** (linear coefficient)
- Graph: a straight line. $a > 0$: increasing. $a < 0$: decreasing. $a = 0$: constant.

<Equation numero="1"
  audioTexto="Equation one. f of x two minus f of x one, divided by x two minus x one, equals a. The rate of change between any two points of an affine function is constant and equal to the slope."
  explicacao="Rate of change between two points on the line. For an affine function, this value is CONSTANT — it does not depend on which pair of points you choose. It is this constancy that characterizes the affine function among all others." latex="\frac{f(x_2) - f(x_1)}{x_2 - x_1} = a" />

> "The slope of a line that passes through two points $(x_1, y_1)$ and $(x_2, y_2)$ is $m = (y_2 - y_1)/(x_2 - x_1)$." — [OpenStax College Algebra 2e, §2.2](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/2-2-linear-equations-in-one-variable)

### Zero of the function and intercepts

$f(x) = 0 \iff x = -b/a$ (when $a \neq 0$). The pair $(0, b)$ is the **vertical intercept**. The pair $(-b/a, 0)$ is the **zero** (or horizontal intercept).

### Uniqueness theorem: two points determine a line

<Teorema titulo="Two points determine an affine function">
Given two distinct points $(x_1, y_1)$ and $(x_2, y_2)$ with $x_1 \neq x_2$, there exists a unique affine function $f(x) = ax + b$ passing through both. The coefficients are:
$$a = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1}, \quad b = y_1 - a x_1$$
</Teorema>

**Proof (sketch).** Existence: define $a$ by the formula above and $b = y_1 - a x_1$. One verifies $f(x_1) = y_1$ by construction, and $f(x_2) = a(x_2 - x_1) + y_1 = (y_2 - y_1) + y_1 = y_2$. Uniqueness: if $g(x) = a' x + b'$ also satisfies $g(x_i) = y_i$, then $a' = (y_2 - y_1)/(x_2 - x_1) = a$ and $b' = y_1 - a' x_1 = b$. ∎

### Composition and operations

Let $f(x) = a_1 x + b_1$ and $g(x) = a_2 x + b_2$. Then:

- **Sum**: $(f + g)(x) = (a_1 + a_2) x + (b_1 + b_2)$ — affine, with slopes added.
- **Composition**: $(f \circ g)(x) = a_1 (a_2 x + b_2) + b_1 = a_1 a_2 x + (a_1 b_2 + b_1)$ — affine, with slopes multiplied.
- **Inverse** (if $a_1 \neq 0$): $f^{-1}(y) = (y - b_1)/a_1$ — also affine, with slope $1/a_1$.

The set of **invertible** affine functions ($a \neq 0$) under composition forms a group — the structure $(\mathbb{R}^* \times \mathbb{R}, \circ)$. This observation will be used in linear algebra (Lesson 31+) and in affine geometry.

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

Imagine a staircase. Each step has **the same height**.

- If each step is 20 cm, after 1 step you climbed 20 cm.
- After 2 steps, you climbed 40 cm.
- After 5 steps, you climbed 100 cm = 1 meter.

The height you are at depends **on how many steps you climbed**. And it always grows **by the same amount** per step.

That is a linear function. The height is $f(n) = 20 \cdot n$ where $n$ is the number of steps.

If the staircase started on a platform 50 cm above the floor, then it would be $f(n) = 20n + 50$. The 50 is the **starting point** (where you are before climbing anything). The 20 is **how much you climb per step**.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

You go to the movies. The ticket costs **$30 flat**. Each bag of popcorn costs **$8**. If you buy $n$ bags:

$$\text{total cost} = 30 + 8n$$

That is a linear function! Where:
- The **30** is the cost **even if you buy 0 bags of popcorn** (the ticket). That is the **b** in the formula $f(x) = ax + b$.
- The **8** is how much the bill **increases with each extra bag of popcorn**. That is the **a** in the formula.

In any function of the type "there is a fixed part + a part that grows at the same rate", you have an affine function. Other examples:

- **Rideshare fare**: base fee ($6 flat) + $2 per km. $f(d) = 6 + 2d$.
- **Electricity bill**: availability fee ($25) + $0.80 per kWh. $f(k) = 25 + 0{,}80\,k$.
- **Scooter rental**: $3 to unlock + $0.80 per minute.

The straight line on the graph **represents** this formula visually. The steeper the line, the faster your bill grows.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old">

The affine function is the simplest "interesting" type of function that exists — and it is the one that opens the door to calculus.

The rule is: $f(x) = ax + b$. Plug in a value of $x$, multiply by $a$, add $b$, and out comes $f(x)$. Simple as that.

**Why does it matter?** Because the graph is a **straight line**, and the slope of that line is exactly $a$. When you move **1 step to the right** on the $x$-axis, you go up **$a$ steps on the y-axis**. Always. At any point on the line. This **constancy** is what makes the affine function "linear" in behavior.

In a few lessons you will study the **instantaneous rate of change** (the derivative). You will see that: for an affine function, the derivative is constant and equal to $a$. For other functions (parabolas, exponentials), the "slope" changes with $x$ — and that is exactly where calculus appears. But for the straight line, it is easy: the slope is $a$, end of story.

**Concrete example.** You drive at a constant speed of 60 km/h. The distance traveled is $d(t) = 60t$ (with $t$ in hours). The "slope" $a = 60$ is the speed. Makes sense — the steeper the line, the faster you go.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

The affine function is the point where most physical models are **locally linearized**. Even non-linear phenomena (free fall with air resistance, non-Newtonian dynamics) are, to first order, approximated by affine functions — that is what the derivative formalizes.

### Slope-intercept vs. general form

- Slope-intercept: $y = ax + b$
- General: $Ax + By + C = 0$
- Parametric: $\{(x_0 + t\vec{u}_x, y_0 + t\vec{u}_y) : t \in \mathbb{R}\}$

Each is useful in different contexts. In CAD, parametric. In linear optimization, general. In statistics (regression), slope-intercept.

### Line fitting (linear regression preview)

Given $(x_1, y_1), \ldots, (x_n, y_n)$, which line best fits them? **Least squares**:

$$a = \frac{n \sum x_i y_i - \sum x_i \sum y_i}{n \sum x_i^2 - (\sum x_i)^2}, \quad b = \bar{y} - a \bar{x}$$

You will see this in detail in Trimester 11 (inferential statistics).

### Applications (partial list)

- **Hooke's law**: $F = kx$ — elastic force as a function of elongation (affine with $b = 0$, i.e., linear)
- **Ohm's law**: $V = RI$ — voltage as a function of current (linear)
- **Celsius to Fahrenheit conversion**: $F = 1{,}8C + 32$ (affine, $a = 1{,}8$, $b = 32$)
- **Uniform motion equation**: $s(t) = s_0 + vt$ (affine in $t$, $b = s_0$, $a = v$)

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

In practice, "**linear is local**". Most technical engineering activity involving models is, at the end of the day, line fitting:

- **Parameter estimation** in control engineering: plant identification via sinusoidal excitation and linear fitting in the Laplace domain.
- **Taylor linearization**: any smooth function near $x_0$ is locally affine — $f(x) \approx f(x_0) + f'(x_0)(x - x_0)$. All stability analysis of dynamical systems depends on this.
- **Linear OLS regression**: the line that minimizes squared error for $n$ points is the foundation of the economist (GDP ~ investment), the pharmacist (dose ~ response), the agronomist (rain ~ harvest).
- **Logistic regression**: the "linearization" of the log-odds — the log of the probability ratio is an affine function of the covariates. Every AI that classifies (spam filter, fraud detection) has logistic regression at its core.

### Watch out: heteroskedasticity and spurious linearity

In production, fitting $y = ax + b$ to data without checking residuals is a recipe for disaster. The prerequisites of regression (Gauss-Markov):

1. True linearity of the relationship — not just of the data.
2. Residuals with constant variance (homoskedasticity).
3. Uncorrelated residuals.
4. Zero-mean residuals.

When these fail, $\hat a$ remains an estimator, but loses efficiency or consistency properties. **OLS without checking is cargo cult**.

### Linearization vs. transformation

When the relationship is NOT affine, there are two options: (a) fit with a non-linear model, (b) **transform variables** until it becomes affine. Classic examples:

- $y = a x^b$ means $\ln y = \ln a + b \ln x$ — log-log linear (power law)
- $y = a e^{bx}$ means $\ln y = \ln a + bx$ — semi-log linear (exponential)
- $y = a/x$ means $y = a \cdot (1/x)$ — affine in $1/x$

Plotting on log-log or semi-log scale and checking if it becomes a straight line is the first diagnostic tool.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practice — 3 real-world scenarios">

### Scenario 1: Electricity bill (April 2026)

Estimated residential rate: $0.82/kWh + yellow flag surcharge $0.02/kWh + 25% tax on consumption + fixed availability fee of $35.40 (three-phase residential). For a consumption of $k$ (kWh):

$$C(k) = (0{,}82 + 0{,}02) \cdot 1{,}25 \cdot k + 35{,}40 = 1{,}05 k + 35{,}40$$

**Affine function**, with $a = 1{,}05$ per kWh and $b = 35{,}40$. For $k = 200$ kWh, the bill is $C(200) = 245{,}40$.

**Marginal analysis**: each additional kWh costs exactly $1.05. The "fixed portion" of $35.40 does not depend on consumption — it is what you pay **even if you travel the whole month with the breaker off**.

### Scenario 2: Crack propagation velocity (fracture mechanics)

In fatigue tests (Paris curve), the propagation of a crack in metal can be linearized as:

$$\log(da/dN) = \log C + m \log(\Delta K)$$

where $da/dN$ is the crack velocity per cycle, $\Delta K$ is the variation in the stress intensity factor, and $C, m$ are material constants. On a log-log scale this is a straight line with slope $m$ — called the **Paris exponent**.

For typical structural steel, $m \approx 3$. This means: doubling $\Delta K$ multiplies the propagation velocity by $2^3 = 8$ — dramatically non-linear behavior that becomes linear on a log-log scale.

### Scenario 3: Aggregate demand curve (microeconomics)

The demand for a good is often modeled as an affine function: $Q(p) = \alpha - \beta p$ where $\alpha, \beta > 0$ and $p$ is the price. The price elasticity of demand is $\epsilon = -\beta p / Q$ — it varies along the curve, even though the function is affine.

Numerical example: $Q(p) = 1000 - 25 p$. At $p = 20$, $Q = 500$, elasticity $|\epsilon| = 25 \cdot 20 / 500 = 1$ (unit elastic). At $p = 30$, $Q = 250$, elasticity $|\epsilon| = 3$ (elastic). At $p = 10$, $Q = 750$, $|\epsilon| = 0{,}33$ (inelastic). **Operational observation**: a firm that raises the price of an inelastic good gains revenue; for an elastic good, it loses revenue.

## Worked examples

> Five examples with increasing difficulty — from direct evaluation of a given line to modeling a break-even point for transport plans. Each example cites its source: the original problem always comes from an open-licensed book.

<Exemplo titulo="Example 1 — Identify coefficients and zero (application)" numero="1">

**Problem:** For $f(x) = -\dfrac{2}{3} x + 4$, determine: slope $a$, y-intercept $b$, zero of the function, and the value of $f(6)$.

**Strategy:** Direct comparison with the slope-intercept form $y = ax + b$. The zero is $x_0 = -b/a$; $f(6)$ is straightforward numerical evaluation.

**Solution:**

1.  **Coefficients.** By comparison: $a = -2/3$, $b = 4$.
2.  **Zero.** $f(x) = 0 \iff -\tfrac{2}{3}x + 4 = 0 \iff x = 6$.
3.  **$f(6)$.** $f(6) = -\tfrac{2}{3}(6) + 4 = -4 + 4 = 0$. Consistent with item 2 — $x = 6$ is the zero.

**Verification:** Since $a < 0$, the function is decreasing. At $x = 0$, $f(0) = 4$ (vertical intercept). At $x = 6$, it crosses the $x$-axis. ✓

**Source.** [OpenStax — College Algebra 2e §2.2](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/2-2-linear-equations-in-one-variable) — CC-BY 4.0 license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Example 2 — Line through two points (intermediate)" numero="2">

**Problem:** Determine the equation of the affine function whose graph passes through $(2, -1)$ and $(5, 8)$.

**Strategy:** Apply the formula $a = (y_2 - y_1)/(x_2 - x_1)$ for the slope, then use one of the points to find $b$.

**Solution:**

1.  **Slope.** $a = \dfrac{8 - (-1)}{5 - 2} = \dfrac{9}{3} = 3$.
2.  **Intercept.** Using the point $(2, -1)$: $-1 = 3 \cdot 2 + b \Rightarrow b = -7$.
3.  **Equation.** $f(x) = 3x - 7$.

**Verification:** $f(2) = 6 - 7 = -1$ ✓ and $f(5) = 15 - 7 = 8$ ✓.

**Source.** [OpenStax — College Algebra 2e §2.2](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/2-2-linear-equations-in-one-variable) — CC-BY 4.0 license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Example 3 — Parallel and perpendicular lines (intermediate)" numero="3">

**Problem:** Find the equation of the line parallel to $y = -2x + 7$ passing through $(3, 1)$, and the equation of the line perpendicular to that same line passing through the same point.

**Strategy:** Parallel lines have the **same** slope ($a' = a$). Perpendicular lines have slopes whose product is $-1$ ($a' = -1/a$). In both cases, find $b'$ using the given point.

**Solution:**

1.  **Parallel line.** Slope $a' = -2$. Using $(3, 1)$: $1 = -2(3) + b' \Rightarrow b' = 7$. Equation: $y = -2x + 7$. (Note — it coincides with the original line. This happens because $(3, 1)$ lies on the original line.)
2.  **Verification.** $-2(3) + 7 = 1$. ✓ Indeed, $(3, 1)$ belongs to the original line; the only parallel line is itself.
3.  **Perpendicular line.** Slope $a' = -1/(-2) = 1/2$. Using $(3, 1)$: $1 = (1/2)(3) + b' \Rightarrow b' = -1/2$. Equation: $y = \tfrac{1}{2}x - \tfrac{1}{2}$.

**Verification:** At $x = 3$: $\tfrac{1}{2}(3) - \tfrac{1}{2} = 1$. ✓ Product of slopes: $(-2) \cdot (1/2) = -1$. ✓

**Source.** [Stitz–Zeager — Precalculus §2.1](https://www.stitz-zeager.com/szprecalculus07042013.pdf) — CC-BY-NC-SA license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Example 4 — Composition of affine functions is affine (advanced)" numero="4">

**Problem:** Let $f(x) = 2x + 1$ and $g(x) = -3x + 5$. Compute $f \circ g$, $g \circ f$ and show that both are affine. Determine the resulting coefficients in each case.

**Strategy:** Substitute one function into the other and simplify to slope-intercept form. The composition of two affine functions is always affine — a key property that allows building groups of transformations.

**Solution:**

1.  **$f \circ g$.** $f(g(x)) = f(-3x + 5) = 2(-3x + 5) + 1 = -6x + 10 + 1 = -6x + 11$.
    Coefficients: $a = -6$, $b = 11$. Note that the resulting $a$ is the product $2 \cdot (-3) = -6$.
2.  **$g \circ f$.** $g(f(x)) = g(2x + 1) = -3(2x + 1) + 5 = -6x - 3 + 5 = -6x + 2$.
    Coefficients: $a = -6$, $b = 2$.
3.  **Observation.** Both have the same slope $-6$ (multiplication of the individual slopes — commutative) but different intercepts (not commutative in the constant term).

**Verification:** At $x = 0$: $(f \circ g)(0) = f(5) = 11$ ✓; $(g \circ f)(0) = g(1) = 2$ ✓.

**Source.** [OpenStax — College Algebra 2e §5.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/5-1-linear-functions) — CC-BY 4.0 license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Example 5 — Real-world modeling: comparing taxi plans (real modeling)" numero="5">

**Problem:** Two taxi companies compete in the same city. **Company X**: base fare $6.00 + $2.80/km. **Company Y**: base fare $4.00 + $3.50/km. Above what distance is Company X cheaper?

**Strategy:** Model each cost as an affine function, set them equal to find the break-even point, and analyze the comparison for longer and shorter distances.

**Solution:**

1.  **Models.** $C_X(d) = 6 + 2{,}80\,d$ and $C_Y(d) = 4 + 3{,}50\,d$.
2.  **Break-even point.** Set equal: $6 + 2{,}80\,d = 4 + 3{,}50\,d \Rightarrow 2 = 0{,}70\,d \Rightarrow d = 2/0{,}70 \approx 2{,}86$ km.
3.  **Comparison.** For $d < 2{,}86$ km, Company Y is cheaper (smaller base fare wins). For $d > 2{,}86$ km, Company X is cheaper (lower per-km rate wins).

**Verification:** At $d = 5$ km: $C_X = 6 + 14 = 20{,}00$; $C_Y = 4 + 17{,}50 = 21{,}50$. ✓ X is cheaper. At $d = 1$ km: $C_X = 8{,}80$, $C_Y = 7{,}50$. ✓ Y is cheaper.

**Source.** [Yoshiwara — Modeling, Functions, and Graphs ch. 1](https://textbooks.aimath.org/textbooks/approved-textbooks/yoshiwara/) — free license.
</Exemplo>
