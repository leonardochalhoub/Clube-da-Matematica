### Comparison of growth rates

<Definicao titulo="Growth hierarchy">

For fixed $a > 1$ and $p > 0$, as $x \to +\infty$:

$$\ln x \;\ll\; x^p \;\ll\; a^x$$

The symbol $f \ll g$ means $f(x)/g(x) \to 0$.

- **Logarithmic** $\ln x$: grows without bound, but more slowly than any power $x^p$ with $p > 0$.
- **Polynomial** $x^p$: grows without bound, but more slowly than any exponential $a^x$ with $a > 1$.
- **Exponential** $a^x$ ($a > 1$): dominates all polynomials and logarithms.

</Definicao>

> "There is a hierarchy of functions based on how quickly they grow. Exponentials grow faster than powers, which grow faster than logarithms." — [OpenStax College Algebra 2e §6.2](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/6-2-graphs-of-exponential-functions)

### The fundamental exponential model

<Definicao titulo="Exponential model">

$$N(t) = N_0 \cdot e^{kt}$$

- $N_0 = N(0)$: initial value.
- $k > 0$: growth. $k < 0$: decay.
- **Characteristic property**: the relative rate of change is constant — $N'(t)/N(t) = k$.

</Definicao>

<Equation latex="\frac{dN}{dt} = kN" explicacao="Separable ODE: the rate of change of N is proportional to N itself. This equation is central to Trim 10 — the rigorous justification of the model appears when you learn derivatives and ODEs." />

| Phenomenon | Equation | Parameter |
|---|---|---|
| Population growth | $P(t) = P_0 e^{rt}$ | $r > 0$ intrinsic rate |
| Continuous compound interest | $S(t) = S_0 e^{it}$ | $i$ nominal rate |
| Radioactive decay | $N(t) = N_0 e^{-\lambda t}$ | $\lambda > 0$ decay constant |
| Newton's cooling | $T(t) - T_a = (T_0 - T_a) e^{-kt}$ | $k > 0$ depends on material |

### Half-life and doubling time

<Equation latex="\tau_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{|k|} \approx \frac{0{,}693}{|k|}" explicacao="Half-life: time to reduce to half (when k is negative). Doubling time: time to double (when k is positive). The relationship is symmetric." />

### Logistic model

Pure exponential growth is physically unsustainable: it implies $N \to \infty$. The logistic model incorporates saturation at a carrying capacity $K$:

<Equation latex="\dot N = r N \!\left(1 - \frac{N}{K}\right), \qquad N(t) = \frac{K}{1 + A e^{-rt}}" explicacao="When N is small compared to K, the factor (1 - N/K) is approximately 1 and growth is nearly exponential. As N approaches K, growth slows and saturates at K. The resulting curve has an S-shape (sigmoid)." />

> "The logistic model is commonly used to model population growth. Growth starts slowly, reaches a maximum, and then decelerates as the population approaches the environmental limit." — [OpenStax College Algebra 2e §6.7](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/6-7-exponential-and-logarithmic-models)

### Linearization via log

Plotting $N(t)$ vs $t$ on a **log-y scale** transforms the exponential into a straight line:

$$\ln N = \ln N_0 + kt$$

The slope of the line is $k$. This is the basis of linear regression on exponential data.

<Insight>

**Why $e^x$ and not $2^x$?** Any $a^x$ can be written as $e^{x \ln a}$. The base $e \approx 2{,}718$ is the unique one such that the derivative of $e^x$ is itself: $\frac{d}{dx}e^x = e^x$. This makes the algebra of derivatives and integrals minimal — zero correction factors. It is pure convenience, not magic.

</Insight>

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

Imagine you fold a piece of paper in half. Now you have 2 parts. Fold again: 4 parts. One more fold: 8 parts. Each fold **doubles** the total.

In 10 folds you would have **1,024 parts**. In 20 folds, more than **1 million**.

Things that grow like this include bacteria, viruses, and rumors spreading at school.

But some things grow **very slowly**. Count how many pages you have read from your favorite series. You read 1 book (300 pages). 10 books. 100 books. 1,000 books. The number of books races far ahead of the satisfaction of having read a lot — you always want more. That is a bit like the logarithm: it rises, but much more slowly.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

**Three different ways to grow:**

| Type | Rule | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Linear | Adds the same each period | Savings without interest ($100 per month) |
| Exponential | Multiplies by the same each period | Compound interest, doubling bacteria |
| Logarithmic | Grows, but more and more slowly | Sound decibels, Richter scale |

**Numerical example — bacteria doubling every hour:**

| Hour | Linear (+100/h) | Exponential (x2/h) |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 0 | 100 | 100 |
| 6 | 700 | 6,400 |
| 12 | 1,300 | 409,600 |
| 24 | 2,500 | ~1.7 billion |

In 24 hours: linear growth goes from 100 to 2,500 (25x). Exponential growth goes from 100 to **1.7 billion** (17 million x).

**Half-life** is the time for something to fall by half. Carbon-14 has a half-life of 5,730 years — which is why fossils up to ~50,000 years old can be dated.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old">

**Exponential growth is alarming.** It goes slowly at first, then explodes.

The king who lost at chess: the prize was 1 grain on square 1, 2 on square 2, 4 on square 3, $\ldots$, $2^{63}$ on square 64. Total: $2^{64} - 1 \approx 1{,}8 \times 10^{19}$ grains. **More rice than all global production accumulated over centuries.**

**Three growth rates — compare visually at $x = 10$:**

- $\ln 10 \approx 2{,}3$ (logarithmic)
- $10^1 = 10$ (linear)
- $10^2 = 100$ (quadratic)
- $2^{10} = 1{,}024$ (exponential)
- $10^{10} = 10$ billion (much stronger exponential)

**The central rule:** if the rate of growth is proportional to the quantity ($\dot N = kN$), the function is exponential. If it grows at a constant absolute rate ($\dot N = k$), it is linear. If it grows at a decreasing rate, it may be logarithmic.

**Moore's Law (Gordon Moore, 1965):** the number of transistors on a chip roughly doubles every 2 years. From 1971 (Intel 4004: 2,300 transistors) to 2023 (Apple M2 Ultra: $\sim 134$ billion). Over 52 years, 26 doublings: $2{,}300 \times 2^{26} \approx 154$ billion. Confirmed.

**Logistic model:** pure exponential growth cannot last forever. The S-curve (sigmoid) models growth that starts exponential and saturates at a ceiling $K$: smartphone adoption, vaccine diffusion, mature startup growth.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

The model $\dot N = kN$ is ubiquitous. In **chemical engineering** (first-order reaction kinetics), **electrical engineering** (RC and RL circuits), **biomedical engineering** (compartmental pharmacokinetics), and **finance** (continuous pricing).

### Asymptotic dominance

The result $e^x / x^n \to \infty$ for any fixed $n$ is proved by applying L'Hopital $n$ times:

$$\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{e^x}{x^n} = \lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{e^x}{n!} = \infty$$

And for the logarithm:

$$\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{\ln x}{x^p} = \lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{1/x}{p x^{p-1}} = \lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{1}{p x^p} = 0 \quad (p > 0)$$

### Logistic model — qualitative analysis

$\dot N = rN(1 - N/K)$: unstable equilibrium at $N = 0$, stable equilibrium at $N = K$. Inflection point at $N = K/2$ — there the growth rate is maximum ($\dot N = rK/4$). The closed-form solution is:

$$N(t) = \frac{K}{1 + A e^{-rt}}, \quad A = \frac{K - N_0}{N_0}$$

### SIR model (Kermack-McKendrick, 1927)

System of ODEs for epidemics:

$$\dot S = -\beta SI, \quad \dot I = \beta SI - \gamma I, \quad \dot R = \gamma I$$

- $S$ = susceptible, $I$ = infected, $R$ = recovered. $S + I + R = N$ constant.
- Basic reproduction number: $R_0 = \beta N / \gamma$. If $R_0 > 1$, the epidemic spreads.
- For small $t$ and $I \ll N$: $I(t) \approx I_0 e^{(\beta N - \gamma)t}$ — initial exponential phase.

### Linearization via log

Plot data $(t_i, \ln N_i)$. If it falls on a line, growth is exponential. The slope is $k$ by least squares.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

In practice, $\dot N = rN$ is the **null model** — the first approximation. Every serious analysis starts there and adds correction terms.

### Moore's Law: exponential with an expiration date

Gordon Moore published in 1965 in *Electronics Magazine*. The observation concerned circuit density, not speed. **Dennard scaling** (power scaling) was the second pillar — which collapsed around 2005 with the heat dissipation problem. That is why CPUs stopped growing in clock frequency and started growing in core count. Moore's Law continues nominally, but with increasing resistance: sub-10nm dimensions encounter quantum effects, current leakage, and fabrication costs in the range of US\$ 20 billion. TSMC, Intel, and Samsung compete for 2nm.

### Logistic market growth

Bass diffusion model ($\dot N = (p + qN/K)(K - N)$) — Frank Bass 1969 — generalizes the logistic with 2 parameters (innovation $p$ + imitation $q$). Used to forecast product adoption (smartphones, EVs, vaccines). Fitted to 100+ products historically.

### Model identification from data

1. Plot on **log-y scale**. If it becomes a line, growth is exponential. Slope = $r$.
2. If it curves downward, growth is logistic. Use nonlinear fitting.
3. If it becomes a line with a **break** (slope change), it is a regime-shift.

### GBM and Black-Scholes (preview of Trim 12)

In implied volatility, the temporal evolution of assets follows Geometric Brownian Motion: $dS = \mu S\,dt + \sigma S\,dW_t$. Solution: $S_t = S_0 \exp((\mu - \sigma^2/2)t + \sigma W_t)$. **Exponential motion with Gaussian noise in the exponent.** The $-\sigma^2/2$ factor (Ito correction) distinguishes the log-normal model from simple $\mu t$.

### Log-returns in finance

**Log-return** $r_t = \log(P_t/P_{t-1}) \approx \Delta P/P$ for small changes. Returns add up: $\log(P_T/P_0) = \sum r_t$. Sums of random variables are tractable by the CLT; products are not. Foundation of Markowitz's risk model and Value-at-Risk.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practice — 4 real scenarios with Brazilian data">

### Scenario 1: Inflation and purchasing power (IPCA 2019–2025)

Cumulative IPCA 2019–2025: ~36% (average ~5.3% p.a.). For a salary of R\$ 5,000 in 2019:

$$\text{real purchasing power}(t) = 5{,}000 \cdot e^{-0{,}053\,t}$$

In 2026 ($t = 7$): purchasing power approximately R\$ 3,450. **A real loss of 31% in 7 years without an adjustment.** To maintain R\$ 5,000 in real terms, the nominal salary would need to be approximately R\$ 7,246.

### Scenario 2: COVID-19 in Brazil — SIR-like model

Brazilian cases from March/2020 to May/2020 grew at $r \approx 0{,}28$/day. Doubling time: $\ln 2/0{,}28 \approx 2{,}5$ days.

Linear projection (incorrect): 1 case to 1 + 0.28 x 60 approximately 18 in 60 days.
Exponential projection: $e^{0{,}28 \times 60} \approx 18$ million — clearly impossible, signaling that the pure exponential model saturated. After May/2020, growth became logistic, then oscillatory.

**Lesson**: the exponential model **only holds while $N \ll K$**. For COVID, $K$ is the susceptible population, which decreases as immunization and recovery occur (SIR model).

### Scenario 3: Asset depreciation (new vehicle)

Declining balance rule with $r = 20\%$/year:

$$V(t) = 80{,}000 \cdot (0{,}80)^t$$

After 1 year: R\$ 64,000. After 5 years: approximately R\$ 26,214. After 10 years: approximately R\$ 8,590. Loss of ~89% in 10 years. Compare with linear depreciation (R\$ 16,000/year): after 5 years, R\$ 0 remains — the declining balance is a more realistic picture of market value.

### Scenario 4: Moore's Law in the Brazilian context (ENEM TIC)

In 2010, the best consumer GPU had ~3 billion transistors. In 2024, ~90 billion. Annual growth rate: $90/3 = 30$ times in 14 years.

$$30 = e^{14r} \Rightarrow r = \ln 30/14 \approx 0{,}24 \text{ (24\% per year)}$$

Doubling time: $\ln 2/0{,}24 \approx 2{,}9$ years — slightly below the classic Moore "2 years", consistent with the slowdown of the 2010s.

</Porta>

## Worked examples

<Exemplo titulo="Bacteria doubling — base-2 model (application)" numero="1">

**Problem:** A bacterial colony starts with 500 individuals and doubles every hour. How many bacteria will there be after 6 hours?

**Strategy:** Recognize the "doubles every $T$ hours" model as $N(t) = N_0 \cdot 2^{t/T}$. Here $N_0 = 500$, $T = 1$.

**Solution:**

1. Write the model: $N(t) = 500 \cdot 2^{t}$, with $t$ in hours.
2. Substitute $t = 6$: $N(6) = 500 \cdot 2^6 = 500 \cdot 64 = 32\,000$.
3. Answer: **32,000 bacteria**.

**Check:** $N(1) = 1\,000$; $N(2) = 2\,000$; $N(3) = 4\,000$. Each hour doubles, consistent pattern. $500 \times 64 = 32\,000$ ✓.

**Source.** [OpenStax College Algebra 2e §6.7](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/6-7-exponential-and-logarithmic-models) — CC-BY 4.0 license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Continuous vs simple compound interest (intermediate)" numero="2">

**Problem:** R\$ 5,000 are invested at 8% p.a. under two schemes: (a) simple interest; (b) continuous compounding. Calculate the balance after 10 years in each case and compare.

**Strategy:** (a) Simple interest: $S = S_0(1 + rt)$. (b) Continuous: $S = S_0 e^{rt}$, with $r = 0{,}08$, $t = 10$.

**Solution:**

(a) Simple: $S = 5\,000 \cdot (1 + 0{,}08 \cdot 10) = 5\,000 \cdot 1{,}8 = 9\,000$.

(b) Continuous: $S = 5\,000 \cdot e^{0{,}08 \times 10} = 5\,000 \cdot e^{0{,}8} \approx 5\,000 \cdot 2{,}2255 = 11\,127{,}70$.

Difference: R\$ 2,127.70 in favor of continuous compounding — 24% more.

**Check:** Rule of 70: $T_\text{double} \approx 70/8 = 8{,}75$ years. In 10 years we expect a little more than doubling under continuous compounding ($11\,127/5\,000 = 2{,}23\times$) ✓. Under simple interest, $1{,}8\times$ with no interest-on-interest ✓.

**Source.** [OpenStax College Algebra 2e §6.7](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/6-7-exponential-and-logarithmic-models) — CC-BY 4.0 license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Carbon-14 dating (intermediate)" numero="3">

**Problem:** A fossil bone contains 25% of its original carbon-14. Half-life of C-14: 5,730 years. What is the approximate age?

**Strategy:** $N(t)/N_0 = (1/2)^{t/\tau_{1/2}}$. Since $0{,}25 = 1/4 = (1/2)^2$, two half-lives have passed.

**Solution:**

1. $(1/2)^{t/5730} = 1/4 = (1/2)^2$.
2. Equating exponents: $t/5730 = 2 \Rightarrow t = 11\,460$ years.

**Check:** 25% = $(1/2)^2$, 2 half-lives. $2 \times 5{,}730 = 11{,}460$ ✓.

**Source.** [OpenStax College Algebra 2e §6.7](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/6-7-exponential-and-logarithmic-models) — CC-BY 4.0 license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Moore's Law — continuous rate and doubling time (advanced)" numero="4">

**Problem:** The number of transistors on a chip goes from 2,300 (Intel 4004, 1971) to 2.6 billion (Intel Core i7, 2011) in 40 years. (a) Calculate the continuous annual rate $r$. (b) Verify consistency with "doubles every 2 years".

**Strategy:** $N(t) = N_0 e^{rt}$. Apply $\ln$ to isolate $r$. Then calculate $T_\text{double} = \ln 2/r$.

**Solution:**

(a) $N(40)/N_0 = 2{,}6 \times 10^9 / 2{,}300 \approx 1{,}130{,}435$. Therefore $e^{40r} = 1{,}130{,}435$.
$$40r = \ln(1{,}130{,}435) \approx 13{,}94 \Rightarrow r \approx 0{,}348 \text{ per year} \approx 34{,}8\%\text{ p.a.}$$

(b) Doubling time: $T = \ln 2 / 0{,}348 \approx 1{,}99$ years. Consistent with Moore's Law. ✓

**Check:** In 40 years, 20 doublings: $2{,}300 \times 2^{20} = 2{,}300 \times 1{,}048{,}576 \approx 2{,}4 \times 10^9$ — very close to the real $2{,}6 \times 10^9$ ✓.

**Source.** [Yoshiwara — Modeling, Functions, and Graphs ch. 6](https://textbooks.aimath.org/textbooks/approved-textbooks/yoshiwara/) — open license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Newton's Law of Cooling — parameter identification (modeling)" numero="5">

**Problem:** A cup of coffee at $90\,°C$ is placed in a room at $20\,°C$. After 5 min it is at $70\,°C$. (a) Model $T(t)$. (b) Calculate $T(15)$. (c) How long until it reaches $30\,°C$?

**Strategy:** $T(t) - T_a = (T_0 - T_a) e^{-kt}$ with $T_a = 20$, $T_0 = 90$.

**Solution:**

(a) $T(t) = 20 + 70\,e^{-kt}$. Use $T(5) = 70$: $50 = 70\,e^{-5k} \Rightarrow e^{-5k} = 5/7 \Rightarrow k = \ln(7/5)/5 \approx 0{,}0673$/min.

(b) $T(15) = 20 + 70\,e^{-0{,}0673 \times 15} = 20 + 70 \times 0{,}364 \approx 45{,}5\,°C$.

(c) $30 = 20 + 70\,e^{-kt} \Rightarrow e^{-kt} = 1/7 \Rightarrow t = \ln 7/k \approx 1{,}946/0{,}0673 \approx 28{,}9$ min.

**Check:** The difference $T - T_a$ drops from $70$ to $50$ in 5 min (factor $5/7$). In 15 min ($= 3 \times 5$): $(5/7)^3 = 125/343 \approx 0{,}364$, so $T - 20 \approx 25{,}5$, $T \approx 45{,}5\,°C$ ✓.

**Source.** [OpenStax College Algebra 2e §6.7](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/6-7-exponential-and-logarithmic-models) — CC-BY 4.0 license.
</Exemplo>
