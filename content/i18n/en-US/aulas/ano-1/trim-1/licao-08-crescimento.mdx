### Comparison of growth rates

<Definicao titulo="Hierarchy of growth">

For $a > 1$ and $p > 0$ fixed, when $x \to +\infty$:

$$\ln x \;\ll\; x^p \;\ll\; a^x$$

The symbol $f \ll g$ means $f(x)/g(x) \to 0$.

- **Logarithmic** $\ln x$: grows without limit, but slower than any power $x^p$ with $p > 0$.
- **Polynomial** $x^p$: grows without limit, but slower than any exponential $a^x$ with $a > 1$.
- **Exponential** $a^x$ ($a > 1$): dominates all polynomials and logarithms.

</Definicao>

> "There is a hierarchy of functions based on how fast they grow. Exponentials grow faster than powers, which grow faster than logarithms." — [OpenStax College Algebra 2e §6.2](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/6-2-graphs-of-exponential-functions)

### The fundamental exponential model

<Definicao titulo="Exponential model">

$$N(t) = N_0 \cdot e^{kt}$$

- $N_0 = N(0)$: initial value.
- $k > 0$: growth. $k < 0$: decay.
- **Characteristic property**: the relative rate of change is constant — $N'(t)/N(t) = k$.

</Definicao>

<Equation latex="\frac{dN}{dt} = kN" explicacao="Separable ODE: the rate of change of N is proportional to N itself. This equation is central in Term 10 — the rigorous justification of the model appears when you learn derivatives and ODEs." />

| Phenomenon | Equation | Parameter |
|---|---|---|
| Population growth | $P(t) = P_0 e^{rt}$ | $r > 0$ intrinsic rate |
| Continuous compound interest | $S(t) = S_0 e^{it}$ | $i$ nominal rate |
| Radioactive decay | $N(t) = N_0 e^{-\lambda t}$ | $\lambda > 0$ decay constant |
| Cooling (Newton) | $T(t) - T_a = (T_0 - T_a) e^{-kt}$ | $k > 0$ depends on material |

### Half-life and doubling time

<Equation latex="\tau_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{|k|} \approx \frac{0.693}{|k|}" explicacao="Half-life: time to reduce to half (when k negative). Doubling time: time to double (when k positive). The relationship is symmetric." />

### Logistic model

Pure exponential growth is physically unsustainable: it implies $N \to \infty$. The logistic model incorporates saturation at a carrying capacity $K$:

<Equation latex="\dot N = r N \!\left(1 - \frac{N}{K}\right), \qquad N(t) = \frac{K}{1 + A e^{-rt}}" explicacao="When N is small compared to K, the factor (1 - N/K) ≈ 1 and growth is nearly exponential. As N approaches K, growth slows and saturates at K. The resulting curve has S shape (sigmoid)." />

> "The logistic model is commonly used to model population growth. Growth begins slowly, reaches a maximum, and then slows as the population approaches the environmental limit." — [OpenStax College Algebra 2e §6.7](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/6-7-exponential-and-logarithmic-models)

### Linearization via logarithm

Plotting $N(t)$ vs $t$ on a **log-y scale** transforms the exponential into a line:

$$\ln N = \ln N_0 + kt$$

The slope of the line is $k$. This is the basis of linear regression on exponential data.

<Insight>

**Why $e^x$ and not $2^x$?** Any $a^x$ can be written as $e^{x \ln a}$. The base $e \approx 2.718$ is the unique one such that the derivative of $e^x$ is itself: $\frac{d}{dx}e^x = e^x$. This makes the algebra of derivatives and integrals minimal — zero correction factors. It's purely convenience, not magic.

</Insight>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

Imagine you fold a piece of paper in half. Now you have 2 parts. Fold again: 4 parts. One more fold: 8 parts. Each fold **doubles** the total.

In 10 folds you'd have **1,024 parts**. In 20 folds, more than **1 million**.

Something that grows like that is like bacteria, viruses, or a rumor at school.

But there's something that grows **way too slowly**. Count how many pages you've read from your favorite collection. You read 1 book (300 pages). 10 books. 100 books. 1,000 books. The number of books grows much faster than your satisfaction from having read enough — you always want more. That's a bit like the logarithm: it goes up, but much more slowly.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

**Three different ways to grow:**

| Type | Rule | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Linear | Add the same each period | Savings without interest (US$ 100 per month) |
| Exponential | Multiply the same each period | Compound interest, bacteria doubling |
| Logarithmic | Grows, but slower and slower | Sound decibels, Richter scale |

**Numerical example — bacteria doubling every hour:**

| Hour | Linear (+ 100/h) | Exponential (× 2/h) |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 0 | 100 | 100 |
| 6 | 700 | 6,400 |
| 12 | 1,300 | 409,600 |
| 24 | 2,500 | ~1.7 billion |

In 24 hours: linear growth goes from 100 to 2,500 (25×). Exponential growth goes from 100 to **1.7 billion** (17 million ×).

**Half-life** is the time to fall to half. Carbon-14 has a half-life of 5,730 years — that's why we can date fossils up to ~50,000 years old.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old">

**Exponential growth is scary.** It goes slowly at first, then explodes.

The king who lost at chess: prize = 1 grain on square 1, 2 on square 2, 4 on square 3, $\ldots$, $2^{63}$ on square 64. Total: $2^{64} - 1 \approx 1.8 \times 10^{19}$ grains. **More rice than the entire world's accumulated production over centuries.**

**Three growth rates — compare visually for $x = 10$:**

- $\ln 10 \approx 2.3$ (logarithmic)
- $10^1 = 10$ (linear)
- $10^2 = 100$ (quadratic)
- $2^{10} = 1,024$ (exponential)
- $10^{10} = 10$ billion (much stronger exponential)

**The central rule:** if the rate of growth is proportional to the quantity ($\dot N = kN$), the function is exponential. If it grows with a constant absolute rate ($\dot N = k$), it's linear. If it grows with decreasing rate, it may be logarithmic.

**Moore's Law (Gordon Moore, 1965):** the number of transistors on a chip approximately doubles every 2 years. From 1971 (Intel 4004: 2,300 transistors) to 2023 (Apple M2 Ultra: $\sim 134$ billion). In 52 years, 26 doublings: $2,300 \times 2^{26} \approx 154$ billion. Confirmed.

**Logistic model:** pure exponential growth cannot last forever. The S curve (sigmoid) models growth that starts exponential and saturates at a ceiling $K$: smartphone adoption, vaccine diffusion, mature startup growth.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

The model $\dot N = kN$ is omnipresent. In **chemical engineering** (first-order reaction kinetics), **electrical engineering** (RC, RL circuits), **biomedical** (compartmental pharmacokinetics), **finance** (continuous pricing).

### Asymptotic dominance

The result $e^x / x^n \to \infty$ for any fixed $n$ is proved by L'Hôpital applied $n$ times:

$$\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{e^x}{x^n} = \lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{e^x}{n!} = \infty$$

And for the logarithm:

$$\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{\ln x}{x^p} = \lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{1/x}{p x^{p-1}} = \lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{1}{p x^p} = 0 \quad (p > 0)$$

### Logistic model — qualitative analysis

$\dot N = rN(1 - N/K)$: unstable equilibrium at $N = 0$, stable equilibrium at $N = K$. Inflection point at $N = K/2$ — there the growth rate is maximum ($\dot N = rK/4$). The closed-form solution is:

$$N(t) = \frac{K}{1 + A e^{-rt}}, \quad A = \frac{K - N_0}{N_0}$$

### SIR model (Kermack-McKendrick, 1927)

System of ODEs for epidemics:

$$\dot S = -\beta SI, \quad \dot I = \beta SI - \gamma I, \quad \dot R = \gamma I$$

- $S$ = susceptible, $I$ = infected, $R$ = recovered. $S + I + R = N$ constant.
- Basic reproduction number: $R_0 = \beta N / \gamma$. If $R_0 > 1$, epidemic spreads.
- For small $t$ and $I \ll N$: $I(t) \approx I_0 e^{(\beta N - \gamma)t}$ — initial exponential phase.

### Linearization via logarithm

Plot data $(t_i, \ln N_i)$. If it falls on a line, growth is exponential. Slope = $k$ by least squares.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

In practice, $\dot N = rN$ is the **null model** — first approximation. Every serious analysis starts there and adds correction terms.

### Moore's Law: exponential with an expiration date

Gordon Moore published in 1965 in *Electronics Magazine*. The observation was about circuit density, not speed. **Dennard's Law** (power scaling) was the second pillar — which collapsed ~2005 with the heat dissipation problem. That's why CPUs stopped growing in frequency and started growing in cores. Moore's Law continues nominally, but with increasing resistance: sub-10nm dimensions encounter quantum effects, current leakage, and fab costs on the scale of US$20 billion. TSMC, Intel, Samsung compete for 2nm.

### Logistic market growth

Bass diffusion model ($\dot N = (p + qN/K)(K - N)$) — Frank Bass 1969 — generalizes logistic with 2 parameters (innovation $p$ + imitation $q$). Used in product adoption forecasting (smartphone, EV, vaccine). Fitted to 100+ products historically.

### Model identification from data

1. Plot on **log-y scale**. If it becomes a line → exponential. Slope = $r$.
2. If curve bends down → logistic. Use nonlinear fitting.
3. If it becomes a line with **break** (change of slope) → regime-shift.

### GBM and Black-Scholes (preview of Term 12)

Under implied volatility, asset evolution follows Geometric Brownian Motion: $dS = \mu S\,dt + \sigma S\,dW_t$. Solution: $S_t = S_0 \exp((\mu - \sigma^2/2)t + \sigma W_t)$. **Exponential movement with Gaussian noise in the exponent.** The factor $-\sigma^2/2$ (Itô correction) distinguishes the log-normal model from simple $\mu t$.

### Log-returns in finance

**Log-return** $r_t = \log(P_t/P_{t-1}) \approx \Delta P/P$ for small variations. Returns sum: $\log(P_T/P_0) = \sum r_t$. Sum of random variables is tractable by CLT; product is not. Basis of Markowitz risk model and Value-at-Risk.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practice — 4 real scenarios with Brazilian data">

### Scenario 1: Inflation and purchasing power (IPCA 2019–2025)

Cumulative IPCA 2019–2025: ~36% (average ~5.3% p.a.). For a salary of R$ 5,000 in 2019:

$$\text{real purchasing power}(t) = 5{,}000 \cdot e^{-0.053\,t}$$

In 2026 ($t = 7$): purchasing power $\approx$ R$ 3,450. **Real loss of 31% in 7 years without adjustment.** To maintain R$ 5,000 real, the nominal salary would need to be $5{,}000 \cdot e^{0.053 \cdot 7} \approx$ R$ 7,246.

### Scenario 2: COVID-19 in Brazil — SIR-like model

Brazilian cases from March/2020 to May/2020 grew with $r \approx 0.28$/day. Doubling time: $\ln 2/0.28 \approx 2.5$ days.

Linear projection (incorrect): 1 case → 1 + 0.28×60 ≈ 18 in 60 days.
Exponential projection: $e^{0.28 \times 60} \approx 18$ million — clearly impossible, signaling that pure exponential model saturated. After May/2020, growth became logistic, then oscillatory.

**Lesson**: the exponential model **only holds while $N \ll K$**. For COVID, $K$ is the susceptible population, which declines as immunization and recovery occur (SIR model).

### Scenario 3: Asset depreciation (new vehicle)

Declining balance rule with $r = 20\%$/year:

$$V(t) = 80{,}000 \cdot (0.80)^t$$

In 1 year: R$ 64,000. In 5 years: $80{,}000 \cdot (0.80)^5 \approx$ R$ 26,214. In 10 years: $\approx$ R$ 8,590. Loss of ~89% in 10 years. Compare with linear depreciation (16,000/year): in 5 years remainder is R$ 0 — declining balance is more faithful to market reality.

### Scenario 4: Moore's Law in Brazilian context (ENEM TIC)

In 2010, the best consumer GPU had ~3 billion transistors. In 2024, ~90 billion. Growth rate per year: $90/3 = 30$ times in 14 years.

$$30 = e^{14r} \Rightarrow r = \ln 30/14 \approx 0.24 \text{ (24% per year)}$$

Doubling time: $\ln 2/0.24 \approx 2.9$ years — slightly below the "2 years" classic Moore's Law, consistent with slowdown in the 2010s.

</Porta>

## Worked examples

<Exemplo titulo="Bacteria doubling — base 2 model (application)" numero="1">

**Problem:** A bacterial colony starts with 500 individuals and doubles every hour. How many bacteria will there be after 6 hours?

**Strategy:** Recognize the "doubles every $T$ hours" model as $N(t) = N_0 \cdot 2^{t/T}$. Here $N_0 = 500$, $T = 1$.

**Solution:**

1. Write the model: $N(t) = 500 \cdot 2^{t}$, with $t$ in hours.
2. Substitute $t = 6$: $N(6) = 500 \cdot 2^6 = 500 \cdot 64 = 32,000$.
3. Answer: **32,000 bacteria**.

**Verification:** $N(1) = 1,000$; $N(2) = 2,000$; $N(3) = 4,000$. Each hour doubles, pattern consistent. $500 \times 64 = 32,000$ ✓.

**Source.** [OpenStax College Algebra 2e §6.7](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/6-7-exponential-and-logarithmic-models) — CC-BY 4.0 license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Compound interest continuous vs simple (intermediate)" numero="2">

**Problem:** US$ 5,000 is invested at 8% p.a. in two modes: (a) simple capitalization; (b) continuous capitalization. Calculate the balance after 10 years in each case and compare.

**Strategy:** (a) Simple interest: $S = S_0(1 + rt)$. (b) Continuous interest: $S = S_0 e^{rt}$, with $r = 0.08$, $t = 10$.

**Solution:**

(a) Simple: $S = 5,000 \cdot (1 + 0.08 \cdot 10) = 5,000 \cdot 1.8 = 9,000$.

(b) Continuous: $S = 5,000 \cdot e^{0.08 \times 10} = 5,000 \cdot e^{0.8} \approx 5,000 \cdot 2.2255 = 11,127.70$.

Difference: US$ 2,127.70 in favor of continuous capitalization — 24% more.

**Verification:** Rule of 70: $T_\text{dupl} \approx 70/8 = 8.75$ years. In 10 years we expect slightly more than doubling in continuous ($11,127/5,000 = 2.23\times$) ✓. In simple, $1.8\times$ with no effect of interest on interest ✓.

**Source.** [OpenStax College Algebra 2e §6.7](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/6-7-exponential-and-logarithmic-models) — CC-BY 4.0 license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Carbon-14 dating (intermediate)" numero="3">

**Problem:** A fossil bone contains 25% of the original carbon-14. Half-life of C-14: 5,730 years. What is the approximate age?

**Strategy:** $N(t)/N_0 = (1/2)^{t/\tau_{1/2}}$. Since $0.25 = 1/4 = (1/2)^2$, two half-lives have passed.

**Solution:**

1. $(1/2)^{t/5730} = 1/4 = (1/2)^2$.
2. Equating exponents: $t/5730 = 2 \Rightarrow t = 11,460$ years.

**Verification:** 25% = $(1/2)^2$, 2 half-lives. $2 \times 5,730 = 11,460$ ✓.

**Source.** [OpenStax College Algebra 2e §6.7](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/6-7-exponential-and-logarithmic-models) — CC-BY 4.0 license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Moore's Law — continuous rate and doubling time (advanced)" numero="4">

**Problem:** The number of transistors on a chip goes from 2,300 (Intel 4004, 1971) to 2.6 billion (Intel Core i7, 2011) in 40 years. (a) Calculate the annual continuous rate $r$. (b) Check if it is consistent with "doubles every 2 years".

**Strategy:** $N(t) = N_0 e^{rt}$. Apply $\ln$ to isolate $r$. Then calculate $T_\text{dupl} = \ln 2/r$.

**Solution:**

(a) $N(40)/N_0 = 2.6 \times 10^9 / 2,300 \approx 1,130,435$. Therefore $e^{40r} = 1,130,435$.
$$40r = \ln(1,130,435) \approx 13.94 \Rightarrow r \approx 0.348 \text{ per year} \approx 34.8\%\text{ p.a.}$$

(b) Doubling time: $T = \ln 2 / 0.348 \approx 1.99$ years. Consistent with Moore's Law. ✓

**Verification:** In 40 years, 20 doublings: $2,300 \times 2^{20} = 2,300 \times 1,048,576 \approx 2.4 \times 10^9$ — very close to the actual $2.6 \times 10^9$ ✓.

**Source.** [Yoshiwara — Modeling, Functions, and Graphs ch. 6](https://textbooks.aimath.org/textbooks/approved-textbooks/yoshiwara/) — open license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Newton's law of cooling — parameter identification (modeling)" numero="5">

**Problem:** A cup of coffee at $90\,°C$ is placed in a room at $20\,°C$. After 5 min it is at $70\,°C$. (a) Model $T(t)$. (b) Calculate $T(15)$. (c) How long until it reaches $30\,°C$?

**Strategy:** $T(t) - T_a = (T_0 - T_a) e^{-kt}$ with $T_a = 20$, $T_0 = 90$.

**Solution:**

(a) $T(t) = 20 + 70\,e^{-kt}$. Use $T(5) = 70$: $50 = 70\,e^{-5k} \Rightarrow e^{-5k} = 5/7 \Rightarrow k = \ln(7/5)/5 \approx 0.0673$/min.

(b) $T(15) = 20 + 70\,e^{-0.0673 \times 15} = 20 + 70 \times 0.364 \approx 45.5\,°C$.

(c) $30 = 20 + 70\,e^{-kt} \Rightarrow e^{-kt} = 1/7 \Rightarrow t = \ln 7/k \approx 1.946/0.0673 \approx 28.9$ min.

**Verification:** The difference $T - T_a$ falls from $70$ to $50$ in 5 min (factor $5/7$). In 15 min ($= 3 \times 5$): $(5/7)^3 = 125/343 \approx 0.364$, so $T - 20 \approx 25.5$, $T \approx 45.5\,°C$ ✓.

**Source.** [OpenStax College Algebra 2e §6.7](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/6-7-exponential-and-logarithmic-models) — CC-BY 4.0 license.
</Exemplo>
