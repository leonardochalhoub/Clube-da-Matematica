<Porta nivel="formal" titulo="Rigorous definition">

<Definicao titulo="Quadratic function">

Let $a, b, c \in \mathbb{R}$ with $a \neq 0$. The function $f: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ defined by

$$f(x) = ax^2 + bx + c$$

is called a **quadratic function** (or degree-2 polynomial). The graph of $f$ is a **parabola**.

</Definicao>

> "A quadratic function is a polynomial function of degree 2. The graph of a quadratic function is a parabola." — [OpenStax College Algebra 2e, §5.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/5-1-quadratic-functions)

### Roots — Quadratic formula

The **discriminant** $\Delta = b^2 - 4ac$ determines the nature of the roots of $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$:

<Equation latex="x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{\Delta}}{2a}, \quad \Delta = b^2 - 4ac" explicacao="Quadratic formula: Δ > 0 → two distinct real roots; Δ = 0 → one double real root; Δ < 0 → no real roots (two complex conjugates)." />

### Vertex and vertex form

Completing the square in $ax^2 + bx + c$, we obtain the **vertex form**:

<Equation latex="f(x) = a(x - x_V)^2 + y_V" explicacao="Vertex form: directly reveals vertex V = (xV, yV), axis of symmetry x = xV, and extreme value yV." />

where the vertex coordinates are:

<Equation latex="x_V = -\frac{b}{2a}, \qquad y_V = f(x_V) = -\frac{\Delta}{4a}" explicacao="xV is also the average of the roots (when they exist). yV is the minimum value if a > 0, maximum if a < 0." />

### Vieta's formulas

If $x_1, x_2$ are the roots of $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$ (when $\Delta \geq 0$):

<Equation latex="x_1 + x_2 = -\frac{b}{a}, \qquad x_1 \cdot x_2 = \frac{c}{a}" explicacao="Girard-Vieta relations: sum and product of roots expressed directly in terms of coefficients, without needing to find the roots individually." />

> "The vertex of the parabola is the maximum point if $a < 0$ or the minimum point if $a > 0$." — [OpenStax College Algebra 2e, §5.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/5-1-quadratic-functions)

### Sign of the quadratic function

With roots $x_1 \leq x_2$ (when $\Delta > 0$) and $a > 0$: $f(x) \leq 0$ for $x \in [x_1, x_2]$; $f(x) \geq 0$ for $x \leq x_1$ or $x \geq x_2$. If $a < 0$, the signs reverse.

<Teorema titulo="Discriminant and position of the parabola">

For $f(x) = ax^2 + bx + c$ with $a > 0$:

- $\Delta > 0$: parabola crosses the $x$-axis at two points; $f$ takes negative values on $(x_1, x_2)$.
- $\Delta = 0$: parabola is tangent to the $x$-axis; $f(x) \geq 0$ for all $x$, with equality at $x_V$.
- $\Delta < 0$: parabola does not cross the $x$-axis; $f(x) > 0$ for all $x \in \mathbb{R}$.

(Reverse the signs of $f$ for $a < 0$.)

</Teorema>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

Take a ball and throw it really high. It goes up, up, up, then comes down, down, down.

If you draw the path of the ball, you get a curve. This shape has a name: **parabola**.

Every time you throw something up — a ball, a rock — its path makes this same drawing. It's always the same curve!

Mathematicians discovered a formula that describes this path: $y = ax^2 + bx + c$. No need to memorize it. Just know that **every parabola has a formula like this**.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

A parabola is the curve a ball traces through the air. But it also appears in everyday things:

- **Satellite dish** (the parabolic TV dish) — captures the signal and focuses it all at a single point.
- **Car headlight** — the inner mirror is parabolic. This makes all the light go in one direction.
- **Bridge arches** — an inverted parabola, supporting weight efficiently.

The formula is $y = ax^2 + bx + c$. The $a$ controls how "open" or "narrow" the curve is. The sign of $a$ controls which way it points:

- $a > 0$: opens upward (looks like a smile)
- $a < 0$: opens downward (looks like a frown)

The lowest (or highest) point of the parabola has a special name: **vertex**.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old">

The quadratic function $f(x) = ax^2 + bx + c$ is the first "curved" function you study formally. The graph is a **parabola**.

**The four things you need to master:**

**1. Discriminant and roots (quadratic formula)**

$$\Delta = b^2 - 4ac, \qquad x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{\Delta}}{2a}$$

- $\Delta > 0$: two real zeros (two points on the $x$-axis)
- $\Delta = 0$: one real zero (parabola tangent to the $x$-axis)
- $\Delta < 0$: no real zeros (parabola entirely above or below)

**2. Vertex**

$$x_V = -\frac{b}{2a}, \qquad y_V = f(x_V)$$

Vertex is a maximum if $a < 0$, minimum if $a > 0$.

**3. Vertex form**

$$f(x) = a(x - x_V)^2 + y_V$$

This form reveals everything geometric: axis of symmetry at $x = x_V$, extreme value $y_V$, opening controlled by $|a|$.

**4. Sign**

With $a > 0$ and roots $x_1 < x_2$: $f < 0$ between the roots, $f > 0$ outside.

<Insight>

**Why is a ballistic trajectory a parabola?** The gravitational force is constant, generating constant acceleration. Constant acceleration implies position that is quadratic in time — that is exactly the formula of a parabola. The parabola is not mere coincidence: it is the direct consequence of Newton's law.

</Insight>

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

### The quadratic as an engineering prerequisite

The quadratic function is not a trivial pre-calculus topic — it reappears throughout the curriculum:

- **2nd-order ODEs** with constant coefficients ($y'' + by' + cy = 0$) have a **characteristic equation** $\lambda^2 + b\lambda + c = 0$. The discriminant $\Delta$ determines the physical behavior:

| $\Delta$ | ODE solution | Application |
|---|---|---|
| $\Delta > 0$ | Distinct real exponentials | Overdamped |
| $\Delta = 0$ | Exponential with factor $t$ | Critically damped |
| $\Delta < 0$ | Sines and cosines (oscillation) | Underdamped — RLC circuit |

- **Linear regression** — fitting a line by least squares minimizes $\sum_i (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2$, which is quadratic in the coefficients. The minimum condition is the gradient equal to zero.
- **Newton-Raphson** — each iteration approximates the function by a truncated Taylor quadratic and minimizes that local quadratic. **Every Newton iteration is solving a quadratic.**

### Derivation of the quadratic formula (completing the square)

Starting from $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$ with $a \neq 0$:

$$ax^2 + bx + c = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; x^2 + \frac{b}{a}x = -\frac{c}{a}$$

Add $\left(\frac{b}{2a}\right)^2$ to both sides:

$$\left(x + \frac{b}{2a}\right)^2 = \frac{b^2 - 4ac}{4a^2} = \frac{\Delta}{4a^2}$$

Take the square root: $x + \dfrac{b}{2a} = \pm\dfrac{\sqrt{\Delta}}{2a}$. Therefore $x = \dfrac{-b \pm \sqrt{\Delta}}{2a}$. $\square$

### Quadratic programming (QP)

Optimization $\min \tfrac{1}{2} \mathbf{x}^T Q \mathbf{x} + \mathbf{c}^T \mathbf{x}$ subject to $A\mathbf{x} \leq \mathbf{b}$. When $Q$ is positive semidefinite, it is convex and solved via KKT. Relevant instances:

- **Markowitz portfolio**: $\min \mathbf{w}^T \Sigma \mathbf{w}$ given expected return (covered in Trimester 12).
- **Linear SVM**: maximum margin is a QP in $\mathbb{R}^n$.

> "The vertex of a parabola is the point at which the parabola changes direction. This point is also the maximum or minimum value of the quadratic function." — [OpenStax College Algebra 2e, §5.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/5-1-quadratic-functions)

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

### Why the quadratic appears everywhere

The deep answer is that **any smooth function has a 2nd-order Taylor approximation that is quadratic**:

$$f(x) \approx f(x_0) + f'(x_0)(x - x_0) + \tfrac{1}{2} f''(x_0)(x - x_0)^2$$

Around a stationary point ($f'(x_0) = 0$), the dominant approximation is quadratic. Every stability analysis, every sufficient optimality condition, every curvature metric starts here. The quadratic is not a school topic — it is the **universal local model** of any smooth phenomenon.

### Galois and the degree-5 barrier

The quadratic formula exists (degree 2). Cardano (1545) and Ferrari discovered formulas for degrees 3 and 4. Degree 5 or above: no formula in radicals exists. This was proved by Niels Abel (1824) and generalized by Évariste Galois (1831), using the theory of permutation groups. Galois's argument is one of the most beautiful proofs in 19th-century mathematics — involving symmetry, commutativity of groups, and the impossibility of a closed-form formula.

### Galton and regression to the mean

Francis Galton (1886) noticed that children of very tall parents tended to be tall, but not as tall as their parents. He fitted a linear regression to the scatter plot and coined the term **regression**. The squared residuals he minimized — the objective function $\sum (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2$ — is quadratic in the parameters. The parabola is at the heart of statistics.

### Discriminant in algebraic geometry

For conic curves $Ax^2 + Bxy + Cy^2 + Dx + Ey + F = 0$, the discriminant $B^2 - 4AC$ distinguishes:

- $B^2 - 4AC < 0$: ellipse (circle when $A = C$, $B = 0$)
- $B^2 - 4AC = 0$: parabola
- $B^2 - 4AC > 0$: hyperbola

The discriminant $\Delta = b^2 - 4ac$ of the scalar quadratic equation is the one-dimensional case of the same geometric invariant.

</Porta>

### Scenario 1: Optimal product price (maximum revenue)

A store sells 100 lunch boxes per day at R\$ 25.00. Internal research: each R\$ 1.00 price reduction brings 8 extra customers. What price maximizes daily revenue?

Let $x$ be the price reduction (in reais). Price: $p(x) = 25 - x$. Quantity: $q(x) = 100 + 8x$.

$$R(x) = p \cdot q = (25 - x)(100 + 8x) = 2500 + 200x - 100x - 8x^2 = -8x^2 + 100x + 2500$$

Vertex: $x_V = -100/(2 \cdot (-8)) = 100/16 = 6{,}25$. Optimal price: $p = 25 - 6.25 = 18.75$ reais (R\$ 18.75).

Maximum revenue: $R(6{,}25) = -8 \cdot 39{,}0625 + 625 + 2500 = -312{,}5 + 3125 = 2812{,}50$ reais/day (R\$ 2,812.50/day).

### Scenario 2: Launch trajectory (ENEM-style)

An object is launched vertically with $v_0 = 40$ m/s. Neglecting air resistance, $h(t) = 40t - 5t^2$ (m, s).

- Maximum height: $t_V = 40/10 = 4$ s; $h(4) = 160 - 80 = 80$ m.
- Return to ground: $h(t) = 0 \Rightarrow t(40 - 5t) = 0 \Rightarrow t = 0$ or $t = 8$ s.

### Scenario 3: Production cost with diminishing returns

A company has production cost $C(q) = q^2 - 80q + 2000$ (R\$, $q$ in units). What quantity minimizes unit cost?

Average cost: $\overline{C}(q) = C(q)/q = q - 80 + 2000/q$. Minimum: zero derivative, or for high-school purposes, note that total cost $C(q)$ is minimized at $q = 40$ (vertex). At that point, $C(40) = 1600 - 3200 + 2000 = 400$ reais.

## Worked examples

> Five examples with increasing difficulty — from direct application of the quadratic formula to profit optimization. Each example cites the source book.

<Exemplo titulo="Quadratic formula — complete solution (application)" numero="1">

**Problem:** Solve $2x^2 - 7x + 3 = 0$.

**Strategy:** Identify $a, b, c$; compute $\Delta$; apply the quadratic formula; verify with Vieta's formulas.

**Solution:**

1. **Coefficients.** $a = 2$, $b = -7$, $c = 3$.
2. **Discriminant.** $\Delta = (-7)^2 - 4 \cdot 2 \cdot 3 = 49 - 24 = 25 > 0$. Two distinct real roots.
3. **Quadratic formula.** $x = \dfrac{7 \pm \sqrt{25}}{4} = \dfrac{7 \pm 5}{4}$.
   - $x_1 = \dfrac{7 + 5}{4} = 3$
   - $x_2 = \dfrac{7 - 5}{4} = \dfrac{1}{2}$
4. **Factoring.** $2(x - 3)(x - \tfrac{1}{2}) = (x - 3)(2x - 1)$.

**Verification (Vieta):** $x_1 + x_2 = 3 + \tfrac{1}{2} = \tfrac{7}{2} = -b/a = 7/2$ ✓ and $x_1 \cdot x_2 = 3/2 = c/a = 3/2$ ✓.

**Source.** [OpenStax — College Algebra 2e §5.3](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/5-3-quadratic-functions) — license CC-BY 4.0.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Vertex, vertex form, and concavity (intermediate)" numero="2">

**Problem:** For $f(x) = -3x^2 + 12x - 7$, determine the vertex, vertex form, concavity, and maximum value.

**Strategy:** Use $x_V = -b/(2a)$, compute $y_V = f(x_V)$, write the vertex form; concavity comes from the sign of $a$.

**Solution:**

1. **$x_V$.** $x_V = -12/(2 \cdot (-3)) = -12/(-6) = 2$.
2. **$y_V$.** $f(2) = -3(4) + 12(2) - 7 = -12 + 24 - 7 = 5$.
3. **Vertex form.** $f(x) = -3(x - 2)^2 + 5$.
4. **Concavity.** $a = -3 < 0$: parabola opens downward. Vertex is a **maximum**.
5. **Maximum value of $f$:** $y_V = 5$, attained at $x = 2$.

**Verification:** Expand $-3(x-2)^2 + 5 = -3(x^2 - 4x + 4) + 5 = -3x^2 + 12x - 12 + 5 = -3x^2 + 12x - 7$ ✓.

**Source.** [Stitz–Zeager — Precalculus §2.3](https://www.stitz-zeager.com/szprecalculus07042013.pdf) — license CC-BY-NC-SA.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Quadratic inequality with sign analysis (intermediate)" numero="3">

**Problem:** Solve $x^2 - 2x - 8 \leq 0$.

**Strategy:** Find the roots of the associated equation; use the concavity to determine where the parabola lies below the $x$-axis.

**Solution:**

1. **Roots.** $x^2 - 2x - 8 = 0$: $\Delta = 4 + 32 = 36$. $x = (2 \pm 6)/2$: $x_1 = -2$, $x_2 = 4$.
2. **Factoring.** $(x + 2)(x - 4) = 0$.
3. **Sign analysis.** $a = 1 > 0$: "smiling" parabola. It lies **below** the axis ($f \leq 0$) **between** the roots, including the endpoints (non-strict inequality).
4. **Solution.** $x \in [-2, 4]$.

**Verification:**
- At $x = 0$ (interior): $0 - 0 - 8 = -8 \leq 0$ ✓.
- At $x = 5$ (exterior): $25 - 10 - 8 = 7 > 0$ ✓ (not in solution).
- At $x = -2$ (boundary): $4 + 4 - 8 = 0 \leq 0$ ✓ (included).

**Source.** [OpenStax — College Algebra 2e §5.3](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/5-3-quadratic-functions) — license CC-BY 4.0.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Area optimization with rectangular fence (advanced)" numero="4">

**Problem:** A farmer has 120 m of fencing and wants to enclose a rectangular pasture against an existing wall (the fence covers only 3 sides). What dimensions maximize the area?

**Strategy:** Model the area as a function of a single variable using the fence-length constraint. The vertex of the resulting quadratic gives the maximum.

**Solution:**

1. **Variables.** Let $x$ be the depth (sides perpendicular to the wall) and $y$ the width parallel to the wall. Fence: $2x + y = 120 \Rightarrow y = 120 - 2x$.
2. **Area.** $A(x) = x \cdot y = x(120 - 2x) = 120x - 2x^2$.
3. **Physical domain.** $x > 0$ and $y > 0 \Rightarrow x \in (0, 60)$.
4. **Vertex.** $a = -2 < 0$ — has a maximum. $x_V = -120/(2 \cdot (-2)) = 30$ m. $y = 120 - 60 = 60$ m.
5. **Maximum area.** $A(30) = 30 \cdot 60 = 1800$ m².

**Verification:** $A(25) = 25 \cdot 70 = 1750 < 1800$ ✓ and $A(35) = 35 \cdot 50 = 1750 < 1800$ ✓.

**Remark.** The optimal ratio is $y = 2x$ (the dimension parallel to the wall is twice the perpendicular one). This generalizes: with $n$ sides fenced, the optimum divides the available length equally among the fenced sides.

**Source.** [Yoshiwara — Modeling, Functions, and Graphs ch. 6](https://textbooks.aimath.org/textbooks/approved-textbooks/yoshiwara/) — free license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Monopolist profit maximization (real modeling)" numero="5">

**Problem:** A firm has revenue $R(q) = 180q$ (R\$/unit, fixed price) and cost $C(q) = 2q^2 + 20q + 500$. Determine the quantity that maximizes profit and compute the maximum profit.

**Strategy:** Define $L(q) = R(q) - C(q)$; identify it as a quadratic; use the vertex.

**Solution:**

1. **Profit.** $L(q) = 180q - (2q^2 + 20q + 500) = -2q^2 + 160q - 500$.
2. **Concavity.** $a = -2 < 0$: has a maximum.
3. **$q^*$.** $q_V = -160/(2 \cdot (-2)) = 160/4 = 40$ units.
4. **Maximum profit.** $L(40) = -2(1600) + 160(40) - 500 = -3200 + 6400 - 500 = 2700$ reais.
5. **Verification:** $L(39) = -2(1521) + 6240 - 500 = -3042 + 5740 = 2698 < 2700$ ✓.

**Interpretation (MR = MC):** Marginal revenue $R'(q) = 180$; marginal cost $C'(q) = 4q + 20$. Setting equal: $4q + 20 = 180 \Rightarrow q = 40$ — the vertex of the quadratic coincides with the microeconomic optimum.

**Source.** [Yoshiwara — Modeling, Functions, and Graphs ch. 6](https://textbooks.aimath.org/textbooks/approved-textbooks/yoshiwara/) — free license.
</Exemplo>
