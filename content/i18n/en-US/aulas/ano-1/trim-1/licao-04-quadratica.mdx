<Porta nivel="formal" titulo="Rigorous definition">

<Definicao titulo="Quadratic function">

Let $a, b, c \in \mathbb{R}$ with $a \neq 0$. The function $f: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ defined by

$$f(x) = ax^2 + bx + c$$

is called a **quadratic function** (or polynomial of degree 2). The graph of $f$ is a **parabola**.

</Definicao>

> "A quadratic function is a polynomial function of degree 2. The graph of a quadratic function is a parabola." — [OpenStax College Algebra 2e, §5.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/5-1-quadratic-functions)

### Roots — Quadratic formula

The **discriminant** $\Delta = b^2 - 4ac$ determines the nature of the roots of $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$:

<Equation latex="x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{\Delta}}{2a}, \quad \Delta = b^2 - 4ac" explicacao="Quadratic formula: Δ > 0 → two distinct real roots; Δ = 0 → double real root; Δ < 0 → no real roots (two complex conjugate roots)." />

### Vertex and canonical form

Completing the square in $ax^2 + bx + c$, we obtain the **canonical form**:

<Equation latex="f(x) = a(x - x_V)^2 + y_V" explicacao="Canonical form: directly reveals the vertex V = (xV, yV), axis of symmetry x = xV, and extreme value yV." />

where the coordinates of the vertex are:

<Equation latex="x_V = -\frac{b}{2a}, \qquad y_V = f(x_V) = -\frac{\Delta}{4a}" explicacao="xV is also the average of the roots (when they exist). yV is the minimum value if a > 0, maximum if a < 0." />

### Vieta's relations

If $x_1, x_2$ are the roots of $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$ (when $\Delta \geq 0$):

<Equation latex="x_1 + x_2 = -\frac{b}{a}, \qquad x_1 \cdot x_2 = \frac{c}{a}" explicacao="Girard–Vieta relations: sum and product of roots expressed directly by coefficients, without needing to find the roots individually." />

> "The vertex of the parabola is the maximum point if $a < 0$ or the minimum point if $a > 0$." — [OpenStax College Algebra 2e, §5.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/5-1-quadratic-functions)

### Sign of the quadratic function

With roots $x_1 \leq x_2$ (when $\Delta > 0$) and $a > 0$: $f(x) \leq 0$ for $x \in [x_1, x_2]$; $f(x) \geq 0$ for $x \leq x_1$ or $x \geq x_2$. If $a < 0$, the signs reverse.

<Teorema titulo="Discriminant and parabola position">

For $f(x) = ax^2 + bx + c$ with $a > 0$:

- $\Delta > 0$: parabola crosses the $x$-axis at two points; $f$ takes negative values on $(x_1, x_2)$.
- $\Delta = 0$: parabola is tangent to the $x$-axis; $f(x) \geq 0$ for all $x$, with equality at $x_V$.
- $\Delta < 0$: parabola does not cross the $x$-axis; $f(x) > 0$ for all $x \in \mathbb{R}$.

(Reverse the signs of $f$ for $a < 0$.)

</Teorema>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

Throw a ball high up in the air. It goes up, up, up, then down, down, down.

If you draw the path of the ball, it makes a curve. This drawing has a name: **parabola**.

Every time you throw something high — a ball, a rock — its path makes this same drawing. It is always the same curve!

Mathematicians discovered a formula that describes this path: $y = ax^2 + bx + c$. You do not need to memorize it. Just know that **every parabola has a formula like this**.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

The parabola is the curve that a ball makes in the air. But it also appears in everyday things:

- **Satellite dish** (that curved dish for satellite TV) — captures the signal and concentrates everything at one point.
- **Car headlight** — the mirror inside is parabolic. This makes the light go all in one direction.
- **Bridge arch** — inverted parabola, supports weight efficiently.

The formula is $y = ax^2 + bx + c$. The $a$ controls how "open" or "closed" the curve is. The sign of $a$ controls which way it points:

- $a > 0$: opens upward (looks like a smile)
- $a < 0$: opens downward (looks like a sad face)

The lowest point (or highest point) of the parabola has a special name: **vertex**.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old teen">

The quadratic function $f(x) = ax^2 + bx + c$ is the first formally "curved" function you study. The graph is a **parabola**.

**Four things you need to master:**

**1. Discriminant and roots (Quadratic formula)**

$$\Delta = b^2 - 4ac, \qquad x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{\Delta}}{2a}$$

- $\Delta > 0$: two real zeros (two points on the $x$-axis)
- $\Delta = 0$: one real zero (parabola tangent to the $x$-axis)
- $\Delta < 0$: no real zeros (parabola entirely above or below)

**2. Vertex**

$$x_V = -\frac{b}{2a}, \qquad y_V = f(x_V)$$

Vertex is maximum if $a < 0$, minimum if $a > 0$.

**3. Canonical form**

$$f(x) = a(x - x_V)^2 + y_V$$

This form reveals everything geometric: axis of symmetry at $x = x_V$, extreme value $y_V$, opening controlled by $|a|$.

**4. Sign**

With $a > 0$ and roots $x_1 < x_2$: $f < 0$ between the roots, $f > 0$ outside.

<Insight>

**Why is the ballistic trajectory a parabola?** Gravitational force is constant, generating constant acceleration. Constant acceleration implies position is quadratic in time — this is exactly the parabola formula. The parabola is not mere coincidence: it is the direct consequence of Newton's law.

</Insight>

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

### The quadratic as engineering prerequisite

The quadratic function is not a trivial precalculus topic — it reappears throughout the curriculum:

- **2nd-order ODEs** with constant coefficients ($y'' + by' + cy = 0$) have a **characteristic equation** $\lambda^2 + b\lambda + c = 0$. The discriminant $\Delta$ determines the physical behavior:

| $\Delta$ | ODE solution | Application |
|---|---|---|
| $\Delta > 0$ | Distinct real exponentials | Overcritical damping |
| $\Delta = 0$ | Exponential with $t$ factor | Critical damping |
| $\Delta < 0$ | Sines and cosines (oscillation) | Underdamped — RLC circuit |

- **Linear regression** — least-squares line fitting minimizes $\sum_i (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2$, which is quadratic in the coefficients. The minimization condition is gradient equal to zero.
- **Newton-Raphson** — each iteration approximates the function by a truncated Taylor quadratic and minimizes that local quadratic. **Every Newton iteration solves a quadratic.**

### Proof of quadratic formula (completing the square)

Starting from $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$ with $a \neq 0$:

$$ax^2 + bx + c = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; x^2 + \frac{b}{a}x = -\frac{c}{a}$$

Add $\left(\frac{b}{2a}\right)^2$ to both sides:

$$\left(x + \frac{b}{2a}\right)^2 = \frac{b^2 - 4ac}{4a^2} = \frac{\Delta}{4a^2}$$

Take the square root: $x + \dfrac{b}{2a} = \pm\dfrac{\sqrt{\Delta}}{2a}$. So $x = \dfrac{-b \pm \sqrt{\Delta}}{2a}$. $\square$

### Quadratic programming (QP)

Optimization $\min \tfrac{1}{2} \mathbf{x}^T Q \mathbf{x} + \mathbf{c}^T \mathbf{x}$ subject to $A\mathbf{x} \leq \mathbf{b}$. When $Q$ is positive semidefinite, it is convex and solved via KKT. Relevant instances:

- **Markowitz portfolio**: $\min \mathbf{w}^T \Sigma \mathbf{w}$ given expected return (covered in Term 12).
- **Linear SVM**: maximum margin is a QP in $\mathbb{R}^n$.

> "The vertex of a parabola is the point at which the parabola changes direction. This point is also the maximum or minimum value of the quadratic function." — [OpenStax College Algebra 2e, §5.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/5-1-quadratic-functions)

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

### Why the quadratic appears everywhere

The deep answer is that **any smooth function has a 2nd-order Taylor approximation that is quadratic**:

$$f(x) \approx f(x_0) + f'(x_0)(x - x_0) + \tfrac{1}{2} f''(x_0)(x - x_0)^2$$

Around a stationary point ($f'(x_0) = 0$), the dominant approximation is quadratic. Every stability analysis, every optimality condition, every curvature metric starts here. The quadratic is not a high-school topic — it is the **universal local model** of any smooth phenomenon.

### Galois and the barrier of degree 5

The quadratic formula exists (degree 2). Cardano (1545) and Ferrari discovered formulas for degrees 3 and 4. Degree 5 or higher: no formula with radicals exists. This was proved by Niels Abel (1824) and generalized by Évariste Galois (1831), using permutation group theory. Galois's argument is one of the most beautiful proofs of 19th-century mathematics — it involves symmetry, group commutativity, and the impossibility of a closed formula.

### Galton and regression to the mean

Francis Galton (1886) noticed that sons of very tall fathers tended to be tall, but not as tall as their fathers. He fit a linear regression to the cloud of points and coined the term **regression**. The squared residuals he minimized — the objective function $\sum (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2$ — is quadratic in the parameters. The parabola is at the heart of statistics.

### Discriminant in algebraic geometry

For conic curves $Ax^2 + Bxy + Cy^2 + Dx + Ey + F = 0$, the discriminant $B^2 - 4AC$ distinguishes:

- $B^2 - 4AC < 0$: ellipse (circle when $A = C$, $B = 0$)
- $B^2 - 4AC = 0$: parabola
- $B^2 - 4AC > 0$: hyperbola

The discriminant $\Delta = b^2 - 4ac$ of the scalar quadratic equation is the one-dimensional case of the same geometric invariant.

</Porta>

### Scenario 1: Optimal product price (maximum revenue)

A shop sells 100 lunch boxes/day at R\$ 25.00. Internal survey: each R\$ 1.00 price reduction brings 8 extra customers. What price maximizes daily revenue?

Let $x$ be the price reduction (in reais). Price: $p(x) = 25 - x$. Quantity: $q(x) = 100 + 8x$.

$$R(x) = p \cdot q = (25 - x)(100 + 8x) = 2500 + 200x - 100x - 8x^2 = -8x^2 + 100x + 2500$$

Vertex: $x_V = -100/(2 \cdot (-8)) = 100/16 = 6.25$. Optimal price: $p = 25 - 6.25 = 18.75$ reais (US$9.38 at ~2 reais per dollar).

Maximum revenue: $R(6.25) = -8 \cdot 39.0625 + 625 + 2500 = -312.5 + 3125 = 2,812.50$ reais/day (US$1,406.25/day).

### Scenario 2: Launch trajectory (ENEM type)

An object is launched vertically with $v_0 = 40$ m/s. Neglecting air resistance, $h(t) = 40t - 5t^2$ (m, s).

- Maximum height: $t_V = 40/10 = 4$ s; $h(4) = 160 - 80 = 80$ m.
- Return to ground: $h(t) = 0 \Rightarrow t(40 - 5t) = 0 \Rightarrow t = 0$ or $t = 8$ s.

### Scenario 3: Production cost with decreasing returns

Company has production cost $C(q) = q^2 - 80q + 2000$ (R\$, $q$ in units). What quantity minimizes unit cost?

Average cost: $\overline{C}(q) = C(q)/q = q - 80 + 2000/q$. Minimum: derivative zero or, for high-school purposes, note that total cost $C(q)$ is minimum at $q = 40$ (vertex). At that point, $C(40) = 1600 - 3200 + 2000 = 400$ reais.

## Worked examples

<Exemplo titulo="Quadratic formula — complete solution (application)" numero="1">

**Problem:** Solve $2x^2 - 7x + 3 = 0$.

**Strategy:** Identify $a, b, c$; compute $\Delta$; apply the quadratic formula; verify with Vieta's relations.

**Solution:**

1. **Coefficients.** $a = 2$, $b = -7$, $c = 3$.
2. **Discriminant.** $\Delta = (-7)^2 - 4 \cdot 2 \cdot 3 = 49 - 24 = 25 > 0$. Two distinct real roots.
3. **Quadratic formula.** $x = \dfrac{7 \pm \sqrt{25}}{4} = \dfrac{7 \pm 5}{4}$.
   - $x_1 = \dfrac{7 + 5}{4} = 3$
   - $x_2 = \dfrac{7 - 5}{4} = \dfrac{1}{2}$
4. **Factorization.** $2(x - 3)(x - \tfrac{1}{2}) = (x - 3)(2x - 1)$.

**Verification (Vieta):** $x_1 + x_2 = 3 + \tfrac{1}{2} = \tfrac{7}{2} = -b/a = 7/2$ ✓ and $x_1 \cdot x_2 = 3/2 = c/a = 3/2$ ✓.

**Source.** [OpenStax — College Algebra 2e §5.3](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/5-3-quadratic-functions) — CC-BY 4.0 license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Vertex, canonical form and concavity (intermediate)" numero="2">

**Problem:** For $f(x) = -3x^2 + 12x - 7$, determine the vertex, canonical form, concavity, and maximum value.

**Strategy:** Use $x_V = -b/(2a)$, compute $y_V = f(x_V)$, assemble the canonical form; concavity comes from the sign of $a$.

**Solution:**

1. **$x_V$.** $x_V = -12/(2 \cdot (-3)) = -12/(-6) = 2$.
2. **$y_V$.** $f(2) = -3(4) + 12(2) - 7 = -12 + 24 - 7 = 5$.
3. **Canonical form.** $f(x) = -3(x - 2)^2 + 5$.
4. **Concavity.** $a = -3 < 0$: parabola opens downward. Vertex is **maximum**.
5. **Maximum value of $f$:** $y_V = 5$, attained at $x = 2$.

**Verification:** Expand $-3(x-2)^2 + 5 = -3(x^2 - 4x + 4) + 5 = -3x^2 + 12x - 12 + 5 = -3x^2 + 12x - 7$ ✓.

**Source.** [Stitz–Zeager — Precalculus §2.3](https://www.stitz-zeager.com/szprecalculus07042013.pdf) — CC-BY-NC-SA license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Quadratic inequality with sign analysis (intermediate)" numero="3">

**Problem:** Solve $x^2 - 2x - 8 \leq 0$.

**Strategy:** Find the roots of the associated equation; use concavity to determine where the parabola is below the $x$-axis.

**Solution:**

1. **Roots.** $x^2 - 2x - 8 = 0$: $\Delta = 4 + 32 = 36$. $x = (2 \pm 6)/2$: $x_1 = -2$, $x_2 = 4$.
2. **Factorization.** $(x + 2)(x - 4) = 0$.
3. **Sign analysis.** $a = 1 > 0$: parabola is "smiling". It is **below** the $x$-axis ($f \leq 0$) **between** the roots, including the endpoints (non-strict inequality).
4. **Solution.** $x \in [-2, 4]$.

**Verification:**
- At $x = 0$ (interior): $0 - 0 - 8 = -8 \leq 0$ ✓.
- At $x = 5$ (exterior): $25 - 10 - 8 = 7 > 0$ ✓ (not in solution).
- At $x = -2$ (boundary): $4 + 4 - 8 = 0 \leq 0$ ✓ (included).

**Source.** [OpenStax — College Algebra 2e §5.3](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/5-3-quadratic-functions) — CC-BY 4.0 license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Optimization of rectangular enclosure area (advanced)" numero="4">

**Problem:** A rancher has 120 m of fencing and wants to fence a rectangular pasture against an existing wall (fence covers only 3 sides). What dimensions maximize the area?

**Strategy:** Model the area as a function of a single variable using the fencing constraint. The vertex of the resulting quadratic gives the maximum.

**Solution:**

1. **Variables.** Let $x$ be the depth (sides perpendicular to the wall) and $y$ the width parallel to the wall. Fencing: $2x + y = 120 \Rightarrow y = 120 - 2x$.
2. **Area.** $A(x) = x \cdot y = x(120 - 2x) = 120x - 2x^2$.
3. **Physical domain.** $x > 0$ and $y > 0 \Rightarrow x \in (0, 60)$.
4. **Vertex.** $a = -2 < 0$ — has maximum. $x_V = -120/(2 \cdot (-2)) = 30$ m. $y = 120 - 60 = 60$ m.
5. **Maximum area.** $A(30) = 30 \cdot 60 = 1800$ m².

**Verification:** $A(25) = 25 \cdot 70 = 1750 < 1800$ ✓ and $A(35) = 35 \cdot 50 = 1750 < 1800$ ✓.

**Observation.** The optimal ratio is $y = 2x$ (the dimension parallel to the wall is twice the perpendicular one). This result generalizes: with $n$ fenced sides, the optimum divides the available length equally among the sides with fence.

**Source.** [Yoshiwara — Modeling, Functions, and Graphs ch. 6](https://textbooks.aimath.org/textbooks/approved-textbooks/yoshiwara/) — free license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Monopolist profit maximization (real modeling)" numero="5">

**Problem:** A company has revenue $R(q) = 180q$ (R\$/unit, fixed price) and cost $C(q) = 2q^2 + 20q + 500$. Determine the quantity that maximizes profit and compute maximum profit.

**Strategy:** Define $L(q) = R(q) - C(q)$; identify as quadratic; use the vertex.

**Solution:**

1. **Profit.** $L(q) = 180q - (2q^2 + 20q + 500) = -2q^2 + 160q - 500$.
2. **Concavity.** $a = -2 < 0$: has maximum.
3. **$q^*$.** $q_V = -160/(2 \cdot (-2)) = 160/4 = 40$ units.
4. **Maximum profit.** $L(40) = -2(1600) + 160(40) - 500 = -3200 + 6400 - 500 = 2700$ reais (US$1,350 at ~2 reais per dollar).
5. **Verification:** $L(39) = -2(1521) + 6240 - 500 = -3042 + 5740 = 2698 < 2700$ \checkmark.

**Interpretation (MR = MC):** Marginal revenue $R'(q) = 180$; marginal cost $C'(q) = 4q + 20$. Setting them equal: $4q + 20 = 180 \Rightarrow q = 40$ — the vertex of the quadratic coincides with the optimal point of microeconomics.

**Source.** [Yoshiwara — Modeling, Functions, and Graphs ch. 6](https://textbooks.aimath.org/textbooks/approved-textbooks/yoshiwara/) — free license.
</Exemplo>
