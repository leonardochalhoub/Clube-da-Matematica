### Fundamental number sets

<Definicao titulo="Hierarchy of real numbers">

- **Naturals** $\mathbb{N} = \{0, 1, 2, 3, \ldots\}$. (Brazilian convention includes zero; French convention excludes it.)
- **Integers** $\mathbb{Z} = \{\ldots, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, \ldots\}$ — closed under addition, subtraction, and multiplication.
- **Rationals** $\mathbb{Q} = \{p/q \mid p \in \mathbb{Z},\ q \in \mathbb{Z}_{\neq 0}\}$ — every number expressible as a ratio of integers.
- **Reals** $\mathbb{R}$ — completes the number line (fills the "gaps" like $\sqrt{2}$, $\pi$, $e$).

</Definicao>

> "Every real number corresponds to a unique position on the number line. The converse is also true: each location on the number line corresponds to exactly one real number." — [OpenStax College Algebra 2e, §1.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/1-1-real-numbers-algebra-essentials#Figure_01_01_001)

<Definicao titulo="Rational and irrational numbers">

> "The set of rational numbers is written as $\lbrace m/n \mid m \text{ and } n \text{ are integers and } n \neq 0 \rbrace$. Notice from the definition that rational numbers are fractions (or quotients) containing integers in both the numerator and the denominator, and the denominator is never 0." — [OpenStax College Algebra 2e, §1.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/1-1-real-numbers-algebra-essentials#fs-id11713028028)

> "Such numbers are called *irrational* because they cannot be written as fractions. These numbers make up the set of irrational numbers." — [OpenStax College Algebra 2e, §1.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/1-1-real-numbers-algebra-essentials#fs-id11713028028)

</Definicao>

### Set operations

<Definicao titulo="Union, intersection, difference, complement">

- **Union**: $A \cup B = \{x : x \in A \text{ or } x \in B\}$
- **Intersection**: $A \cap B = \{x : x \in A \text{ and } x \in B\}$
- **Difference**: $A \setminus B = \{x \in A : x \notin B\}$
- **Complement** (in a universe $U$): $A^c = U \setminus A$

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

Imagine you have a box with **toys**. Another box with **books**.

- **Union** (∪) is putting both boxes together into one. It has toys AND books.
- **Intersection** (∩) is what is in BOTH boxes at the same time. Usually empty — a toy is not a book.
- But if one box has "**red balls**" and the other has "**small balls**", the intersection is the balls that are **red AND small**.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

Mathematicians love organizing numbers into families.

- The family of **naturals** $\mathbb{N}$ is just what you use to count things: 0, 1, 2, 3, ...
- The family of **integers** $\mathbb{Z}$ adds the negatives: ..., −2, −1, 0, 1, 2, ...
- The family of **rationals** $\mathbb{Q}$ adds fractions: 1/2, 3/4, −5/7, ...
- The family of **reals** $\mathbb{R}$ adds strange numbers like **π** (3.14159...) and $\sqrt{2}$ (1.41421...).

And **intervals** are short ways of saying "all the numbers between such and such":
- `[0, 5]` means "every number from 0 to 5, **including** 0 and 5".
- `(0, 5)` means "every number from 0 to 5, **not including** 0 or 5".

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old">

This is the lesson that establishes the **language** we will use for the next 3 years. All advanced mathematics — calculus, linear algebra, statistics — uses this notation. Without it, it is like trying to read a physics book without knowing the alphabet.

**The 4 central number sets:**

| Symbol | Name | Example |
|---|---|---|
| $\mathbb{N}$ | naturals | 0, 1, 2, 3, ... |
| $\mathbb{Z}$ | integers | ..., −3, −2, −1, 0, 1, 2, ... |
| $\mathbb{Q}$ | rationals | 1/2, 3/4, −7/5, 0.333... = 1/3 |
| $\mathbb{R}$ | reals | all of the above + $\pi$, $\sqrt{2}$, $e$ |

And an important observation: **not every real number is rational**. Irrationals (like $\sqrt{2}$ and $\pi$) are real numbers that CANNOT be written as a fraction of integers. This is a theorem known since Pythagoras (5th century BC) — an elegant proof that you will see in exercise 1.27 of this lesson.

**Intervals** are abbreviations for "all real numbers between given limits":

- `[3, 7]` = $\{x \in \mathbb{R} : 3 \leq x \leq 7\}$ (closed, with endpoints included)
- `(3, 7)` = $\{x : 3 < x < 7\}$ (open, without the endpoints)

When the teacher writes $x \in [3, 7]$ on the board, they are saying: "x is any real number between 3 and 7, including 3 and 7". Very compact. Compare with writing that out in full.

<Insight>

**Why is there a difference between $[3, 7]$ and $(3, 7)$?** Because in advanced mathematics, "including the point" and "approaching it without reaching it" are different phenomena. When you study **limits** in a few months, you will see that $\lim_{x \to 3^+}$ asks what happens *when x approaches 3 without being 3*. The difference between open and closed begins here, now, with you at 15.

</Insight>

</Porta>

### Critical points

**1. Does ℕ include 0?** Brazilian convention (BNCC) and French convention: YES. American convention and several classical ones: NO. I adopt **0 ∈ ℕ** throughout this program, aligned with BNCC. When reading a foreign textbook, check the author's convention.

**2. Brazilian vs. Anglo-Saxon interval notation:**
- BR: $]a, b[$ for open, $[a, b]$ for closed (Bourbaki notation inherited from France)
- Anglo: $(a, b)$ for open, $[a, b]$ for closed

I adopt **Anglo notation** (parentheses for open) for compatibility with 90% of the open-licensed books referenced in `livros/CATALOG.md`.

> "In this book, we will use interval notation with parentheses for open intervals and brackets for closed intervals, as is common in the English-language literature." — [Stitz-Zeager, Precalculus, Ch. 1](https://www.stitz-zeager.com/szprecalculus07042013.pdf)

**3. Density of ℚ in ℝ.** Between any two distinct reals $a < b$, there exists a rational $q$ with $a < q < b$. **Proof** (Exercise 1.29): use the Archimedean property — for every $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists $n \in \mathbb{N}$ such that $1/n < \varepsilon$.

**4. Finite vs. countably infinite vs. uncountable sets.** Cantor (1874) proved that ℝ is **uncountable** but ℚ is **countable** — both are infinite, but with different cardinalities. You will use this fact in university Analysis (Calculus IV / Real Analysis).

### Properties of Real Numbers

> "The commutative property of addition states that numbers may be added in any order without affecting the sum." — [OpenStax College Algebra 2e, §1.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/1-1-real-numbers-algebra-essentials#fs-id11713028028)

> "The distributive property states that the product of a factor and a sum is the sum of the factor multiplied by each term in the sum." — [OpenStax College Algebra 2e, §1.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/1-1-real-numbers-algebra-essentials#fs-id11713028028)

### Cross-domain connections

- **Calculus**: the domain of a function is always a subset of ℝ.
- **Probability**: the sample space is a set.
- **Linear Algebra**: vectors are tuples in ℝⁿ or ℂⁿ.
- **Computing**: primitive types `int`, `float`, `bool` correspond to ℤ (bounded), ℝ (finite precision), $\{T, F\}$.

### Numerical example

Solve $|2x - 3| \leq 5$ and express the result in interval notation:

$$|2x - 3| \leq 5 \iff -5 \leq 2x - 3 \leq 5 \iff -2 \leq 2x \leq 8 \iff -1 \leq x \leq 4$$

Answer: $x \in [-1, 4]$.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

Set theory is the ZFC foundation (Zermelo-Fraenkel + Axiom of Choice) that underpins all modern mathematics. The intuitive "definitions" presented in this lesson have well-known pitfalls:

- **Russell's Paradox**: the set of all sets that do not contain themselves. If it contains itself, then it does not. If it does not, then it does. This broke Cantor's naive theory (1901) and led Zermelo to axiomatize the theory with restricted comprehension.

- **Continuum Hypothesis**: Cantor conjectured that there is no cardinality between $|\mathbb{N}|$ and $|\mathbb{R}|$. Gödel (1940) and Cohen (1963) proved that the hypothesis is **independent** of the ZFC axioms — both it and its negation are consistent with ZFC.

- **Axiom of Choice**: apparently innocent, but implies strange results such as Banach-Tarski (decompose a sphere and reassemble two equal to the original). The decision to adopt AC is practical but philosophically delicate.

In professional practice (engineering, applied science, finance), none of this appears. You use $\mathbb{R}^n$ as Euclidean space and proceed. But if you ever do a graduate degree in Analysis, Logic, or Set Theory, you will return to these paradoxes.

### What is "real" anyway?

Computationally, ℝ does not exist — every digital implementation uses **floating point** (IEEE 754), with ~7 digits of precision (float32) or ~15 (float64). Floating point errors propagate in numerical computation (covered in Trimester 10 with REAMAT). When you write `0.1 + 0.2` in Python, you get `0.30000000000000004`. $\mathbb{Q} \subsetneq \mathbb{R}$ is mathematical abstraction; the computer works in a finite subset much smaller than $\mathbb{Q}$.

</Porta>

### Example 1: Age range for ENEM

The ENEM 2025 registration accepts candidates who are 16 years old by the date of the first exam. In notation:

$$\text{candidate's age} \in [16, +\infty)$$

Note the half-open interval on the right (there is no upper limit).

### Example 2: Minimum wage and income tax exemption

In Brazil in 2025, full income tax exemption for monthly earnings:

$$\text{monthly income} \in [0,\ 2\,824]$$

(Range in BRL.) Above that, progressive rates apply.

### Example 3: Fasting blood glucose ranges (medicine)

| Range | Interval (mg/dL) | Diagnosis |
|---|---|---|
| Normal | $[0, 99]$ | healthy |
| Pre-diabetes | $[100, 125]$ | attention |
| Diabetes | $[126, +\infty)$ | treatment |

Every medical guideline is expressed as a **partition** of the set of possible values into intervals. If you are a biomedical engineer or a healthcare software developer, you will work with these ranges every day.

### Example 4: Domain of a cost function

Cost function $C(q) = 50 + 12q + 0.5q^2$ for manufacturing $q$ units.

Mathematical domain: $C: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$.
Physical domain (real): $q \in \{0, 1, 2, 3, \ldots\} = \mathbb{N}$ (you do not manufacture half a car unit).

When the calculus "optimal point" gives $q^* = 17.3$, it is your job as an engineer to decide whether to round to 17 or 18 — continuous mathematics produces a solution in ℝ; the application requires a solution in ℕ. This **discrete vs. continuous** tension is one of the five sources of epistemological difficulty in Calculus according to Rezende (UFF, 2003).

## Worked examples

<Exemplo titulo="Listing elements of a set (application)" numero="1">

**Problem:** List, in brace notation, the set $A = \{x \in \mathbb{Z} : -2 < x \leq 3\}$.

**Strategy:** The problem asks us to list the elements of a set. The set notation gives us two pieces of information:
1.  Which number set the elements belong to: $\mathbb{Z}$ (integers).
2.  Which conditions those elements must satisfy: $-2 < x \leq 3$.
We combine this information to identify all integers that fit.

**Solution:**

1.  **Identify the type of number:** The elements $x$ must be integers ($\mathbb{Z}$).
2.  **Analyze the first condition:** $x > -2$. This means $x$ must be greater than $-2$. The integers satisfying this part are $-1, 0, 1, 2, 3, \ldots$.
3.  **Analyze the second condition:** $x \leq 3$. This means $x$ must be less than or equal to $3$. The integers satisfying this part are $\ldots, 1, 2, 3$.
4.  **Combine the conditions:** We need integers $x$ that are *simultaneously* greater than $-2$ AND less than or equal to $3$.
    *   Integers greater than $-2$: $\{-1, 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, \ldots\}$
    *   Integers less than or equal to $3$: $\{\ldots, -1, 0, 1, 2, 3\}$
    *   The intersection of these two sets is $\{-1, 0, 1, 2, 3\}$.

**Verification:** All listed numbers are integers.
- $-1$ is greater than $-2$ and less than or equal to $3$. (OK)
- $0$ is greater than $-2$ and less than or equal to $3$. (OK)
- $1$ is greater than $-2$ and less than or equal to $3$. (OK)
- $2$ is greater than $-2$ and less than or equal to $3$. (OK)
- $3$ is greater than $-2$ and less than or equal to $3$. (OK)
No other integer between $-2$ and $3$ (excluding $-2$, including $3$) was left out.


**Source.** [OpenStax — College Algebra 2e §1.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/1-1-real-numbers-algebra-essentials) — CC-BY 4.0.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Intersection of intervals (intermediate)" numero="2">

**Problem:** Given the intervals $A = [-3, 7)$ and $B = (1, 10]$, compute $A \cap B$.

**Strategy:** The intersection of two sets includes only the elements that belong to *both* sets. Visualization on the number line is the most effective tool.

**Solution:**

1.  **Visualize interval $A$:** $A = [-3, 7)$ goes from -3 (included) to 7 (excluded).
    ```
    ←—————[----------)————→
         -3          7
    ```
2.  **Visualize interval $B$:** $B = (1, 10]$ goes from 1 (excluded) to 10 (included).
    ```
    ←——————————(----------]————→
               1          10
    ```
3.  **Combine for the intersection:** We need to find the region where both intervals overlap.
    *   The leftmost point where both are present: $1$. But $1$ is excluded in $B$, so it is excluded from the intersection.
    *   The rightmost point where both are present: $7$. But $7$ is excluded in $A$, so it is excluded from the intersection.
    *   The overlap region is between $1$ (excluded) and $7$ (excluded).

$$ A \cap B = (1, 7) $$

**Verification:**
- A point in the intersection: $x=3$. It is in $A$ (since $-3 \leq 3 < 7$) and in $B$ (since $1 < 3 \leq 10$). (OK)
- A point only in $A$: $x=-2$. Not in $B$. (OK)
- A point only in $B$: $x=8$. Not in $A$. (OK)

<Exemplo titulo="Absolute value inequality, type |u| ≤ c (intermediate)" numero="3">

**Problem:** Solve the inequality $|x - 4| \leq 3$ and express the solution in interval notation.

**Strategy:** Absolute value inequalities of the type $|u| \leq c$ (where $c > 0$) can be rewritten as a compound inequality: $-c \leq u \leq c$. We apply this property and then solve for $x$.

**Solution:**

1.  **Rewrite the inequality:** The absolute value property lets us transform $|x - 4| \leq 3$ into:
    $$ -3 \leq x - 4 \leq 3 $$
2.  **Isolate $x$:** To isolate $x$, we add $4$ to all parts of the inequality:
    $$ -3 + 4 \leq x - 4 + 4 \leq 3 + 4 $$
    $$ 1 \leq x \leq 7 $$
3.  **Express in interval notation:** The inequality $1 \leq x \leq 7$ means $x$ can be any real number between 1 and 7, including 1 and 7.

$$ \text{Solution: } [1, 7] $$

**Verification:**
- Test a point inside the interval: $x=5$. $|5 - 4| = |1| = 1$. $1 \leq 3$. (OK)
- Test an endpoint: $x=1$. $|1 - 4| = |-3| = 3$. $3 \leq 3$. (OK)
- Test the other endpoint: $x=7$. $|7 - 4| = |3| = 3$. $3 \leq 3$. (OK)
- Test a point outside the interval: $x=0$. $|0 - 4| = |-4| = 4$. $4 \not\leq 3$. (OK)
- Test a point outside the interval: $x=8$. $|8 - 4| = |4| = 4$. $4 \not\leq 3$. (OK)
The solution is correct.

<Exemplo titulo="Quadratic inequality (advanced)" numero="4">

**Problem:** Determine the solution set of the inequality $x^2 - x - 6 > 0$ in interval notation.

**Strategy:** To solve a quadratic inequality, we first find the roots of the associated quadratic equation ($x^2 - x - 6 = 0$). These roots divide the real line into intervals. We then test a point in each interval to see where the inequality is satisfied.

**Solution:**

1.  **Find the roots of the associated equation:** $x^2 - x - 6 = 0$.
    *   We can factor: $(x - 3)(x + 2) = 0$.
    *   The roots are $x = 3$ and $x = -2$.
2.  **Divide the real line into intervals:** The roots $-2$ and $3$ divide the real line into three intervals:
    *   $(-\infty, -2)$
    *   $(-2, 3)$
    *   $(3, +\infty)$
3.  **Test a point in each interval:**
    *   **Interval $(-\infty, -2)$:** Choose $x = -3$.
        $(-3)^2 - (-3) - 6 = 9 + 3 - 6 = 6$. Since $6 > 0$, this interval *satisfies* the inequality.
    *   **Interval $(-2, 3)$:** Choose $x = 0$.
        $(0)^2 - (0) - 6 = -6$. Since $-6 \not> 0$, this interval *does not satisfy* the inequality.
    *   **Interval $(3, +\infty)$:** Choose $x = 4$.
        $(4)^2 - (4) - 6 = 16 - 4 - 6 = 6$. Since $6 > 0$, this interval *satisfies* the inequality.
4.  **Combine the satisfying intervals:** The solution is the union of the intervals where the inequality holds. Note that the inequality is strictly greater than 0, so the roots are not included.

$$ \text{Solution: } (-\infty, -2) \cup (3, +\infty) $$

**Verification:**
- The parabola $y = x^2 - x - 6$ opens upward (positive leading coefficient). This means the function values are positive "outside" the roots and negative "between" the roots. The solution found is consistent with the graph of a parabola.


**Source.** [OpenStax — College Algebra 2e §3.6](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/3-6-other-types-of-equations) — CC-BY 4.0.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Modeling with point exclusion (real-world modeling)" numero="5">

**Problem:** A temperature sensor operates correctly in an environment where the temperature $T$ (in Celsius) must be between $10\,°C$ (inclusive) and $40\,°C$ (exclusive). In addition, due to a specific requirement, the temperature cannot be exactly $25\,°C$. Express the valid operating range for $T$ as a union of intervals.

**Strategy:** First, define the base operating interval. Then, remove the specific point that is not allowed. Removing a single point from an interval can result in two disjoint intervals.

**Solution:**

1.  **Define the base interval:** The temperature must be between $10\,°C$ (inclusive) and $40\,°C$ (exclusive). This is represented by the interval $[10, 40)$.
2.  **Identify the point to be excluded:** The temperature cannot be $25\,°C$.
3.  **Remove the point from the base interval:** The number $25$ lies within the interval $[10, 40)$. Removing $25$ splits the interval into two parts:
    *   All numbers from $10$ (inclusive) to $25$ (exclusive): $[10, 25)$.
    *   All numbers from $25$ (exclusive) to $40$ (exclusive): $(25, 40)$.
4.  **Express as a union of intervals:** The valid operating range is the union of these two disjoint intervals.

$$ \text{Operating range: } [10, 25) \cup (25, 40) $$

**Verification:**
- $T=10$: Valid. In the first interval.
- $T=20$: Valid. In the first interval.
- $T=25$: Not valid. Not in either interval.
- $T=30$: Valid. In the second interval.
- $T=40$: Not valid. Excluded from the original interval.
The solution is correct and satisfies all conditions.


**Source.** [Yoshiwara — Modeling, Functions, and Graphs ch. 1](https://textbooks.aimath.org/textbooks/approved-textbooks/yoshiwara/) — open license.
</Exemplo>
