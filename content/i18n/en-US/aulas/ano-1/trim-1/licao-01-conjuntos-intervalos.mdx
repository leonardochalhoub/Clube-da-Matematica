### Fundamental number sets

<Definicao titulo="Hierarchy of real numbers">

- **Naturals** $\mathbb{N} = \{0, 1, 2, 3, \ldots\}$. (Brazilian convention includes zero; French convention excludes it.)
- **Integers** $\mathbb{Z} = \{\ldots, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, \ldots\}$ — closed under addition, subtraction, and multiplication.
- **Rationals** $\mathbb{Q} = \{p/q \mid p \in \mathbb{Z},\ q \in \mathbb{Z}_{\neq 0}\}$ — every number expressible as a ratio of integers.
- **Reals** $\mathbb{R}$ — completes the axis (fills the "gaps" like $\sqrt{2}$, $\pi$, $e$).

</Definicao>

> "Every real number corresponds to a unique position on the number line. The converse is also true: each location on the number line corresponds to exactly one real number." — [OpenStax College Algebra 2e, §1.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/1-1-real-numbers-algebra-essentials#Figure_01_01_001)

<Definicao titulo="Rational and irrational numbers">

> "The set of rational numbers is written as $\lbrace m/n \mid m \text{ and } n \text{ are integers and } n \neq 0 \rbrace$. From the definition, we see that rational numbers are fractions (or quotients) containing integers in both the numerator and denominator, and the denominator is never 0." — [OpenStax College Algebra 2e, §1.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/1-1-real-numbers-algebra-essentials#fs-id11713028028)

> "Such numbers are said to be *irrational* because they cannot be written as fractions. These numbers make up the set of irrational numbers." — [OpenStax College Algebra 2e, §1.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/1-1-real-numbers-algebra-essentials#fs-id11713028028)

</Definicao>

### Set operations

<Definicao titulo="Union, intersection, difference, complement">

- **Union**: $A \cup B = \{x : x \in A \text{ or } x \in B\}$
- **Intersection**: $A \cap B = \{x : x \in A \text{ and } x \in B\}$
- **Difference**: $A \setminus B = \{x \in A : x \notin B\}$
- **Complement** (in a universe $U$): $A^c = U \setminus A$

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

Imagine you have a box of **toys**. Another box of **books**.

- **Union** (∪) means putting both boxes together in one. You have toys AND books.
- **Intersection** (∩) means what is in BOTH boxes at the same time. Usually empty — a toy is not a book.
- But if one box has "**red balls**" and the other has "**small balls**", the intersection is the balls that are **red AND small**.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

Mathematicians love organizing numbers into families.

- The family of **naturals** $\mathbb{N}$ is just what you use to count things: 0, 1, 2, 3, ...
- The family of **integers** $\mathbb{Z}$ adds the negatives: ..., −2, −1, 0, 1, 2, ...
- The family of **rationals** $\mathbb{Q}$ adds fractions: 1/2, 3/4, −5/7, ...
- The family of **reals** $\mathbb{R}$ adds weird numbers like **π** (3.14159...) and $\sqrt{2}$ (1.41421...).

And **intervals** are short ways to say "all numbers between this and that":
- `[0, 5]` means "every number from 0 to 5, **including** 0 and 5".
- `(0, 5)` means "every number from 0 to 5, **without** 0 and 5".

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old teen">

This is the lesson that sets up the **language** we'll use for the next 3 years. All advanced mathematics — calculus, linear algebra, statistics — uses this notation. Without it, it's like trying to read a physics book without knowing the letters.

**The 4 central number sets:**

| Symbol | Name | Example |
|---|---|---|
| $\mathbb{N}$ | naturals | 0, 1, 2, 3, ... |
| $\mathbb{Z}$ | integers | ..., −3, −2, −1, 0, 1, 2, ... |
| $\mathbb{Q}$ | rationals | 1/2, 3/4, −7/5, 0.333... = 1/3 |
| $\mathbb{R}$ | reals | all of the above + $\pi$, $\sqrt{2}$, $e$ |

And an important observation: **not every real number is rational**. The irrationals (like $\sqrt{2}$ and $\pi$) are real numbers that CANNOT be written as a fraction of integers. This is a theorem known since Pythagoras (5th century BCE) — an elegant proof that you'll see in exercise 1.27 of this lesson.

**Intervals** are shortcuts for "all real numbers between these limits":

- `[3, 7]` = $\{x \in \mathbb{R} : 3 \leq x \leq 7\}$ (closed, with boundaries included)
- `(3, 7)` = $\{x : 3 < x < 7\}$ (open, without boundaries)

When the teacher writes on the board $x \in [3, 7]$, they're saying: "x is any real number between 3 and 7, including 3 and 7". Super compact. Compare this to writing it out.

<Insight>

**Why is there a difference between $[3, 7]$ and $(3, 7)$?** Because in advanced mathematics, "including the point" and "getting arbitrarily close without reaching it" are different phenomena. When you study **limits** in a few months, you'll see that $\lim_{x \to 3^+}$ asks what happens *when x approaches 3 without being 3*. The difference between open and closed starts here, now, with you at age 15.

</Insight>

</Porta>

### Critical points

**1. Does ℕ include 0?** Brazilian convention (BNCC) and French: YES. American convention and various classics: NO. I adopt **0 ∈ ℕ** throughout this program, aligned with BNCC. When reading foreign books, check the author's convention.

**2. Brazilian vs. Anglo-Saxon interval notation:**
- BR: $]a, b[$ for open, $[a, b]$ for closed (Bourbaki notation inherited from France)
- Anglo: $(a, b)$ for open, $[a, b]$ for closed

I adopt **Anglo notation** (parentheses for open) for compatibility with 90% of open-source books referenced in `livros/CATALOG.md`.

> "In this book, we use interval notation with parentheses for open intervals and brackets for closed intervals, as is common in English-language literature." — [Stitz-Zeager, Precalculus, Chapter 1](https://www.stitz-zeager.com/szprecalculus07042013.pdf)

**3. Density of ℚ in ℝ.** Between any two distinct reals $a < b$, there exists a rational $q$ with $a < q < b$. **Proof** (Exercise 1.29): use the Archimedean property — for every $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists $n \in \mathbb{N}$ such that $1/n < \varepsilon$.

**4. Finite vs. countably infinite vs. uncountable sets.** Cantor (1874) proved that ℝ is **uncountable** but ℚ is **countable** — both are infinite, but with distinct cardinalities. You'll use this fact in university Analysis (Calculus IV / Real Analysis).

### Properties of Real Numbers

> "The commutative property of addition states that numbers can be added in any order without affecting the sum." — [OpenStax College Algebra 2e, §1.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/1-1-real-numbers-algebra-essentials#fs-id11713028028)

> "The distributive property states that the product of a factor by a sum is the sum of the factor by each term of the sum." — [OpenStax College Algebra 2e, §1.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/1-1-real-numbers-algebra-essentials#fs-id11713028028)

### Cross-domain connection

- **Calculus**: function domain is always a subset of ℝ.
- **Probability**: sample space is a set.
- **Linear Algebra**: vectors are tuples in ℝⁿ or ℂⁿ.
- **Computation**: primitive types `int`, `float`, `bool` correspond to ℤ (with bounds), ℝ (with finite precision), $\{T, F\}$.

### Numerical example

Solve $|2x - 3| \leq 5$ and express the result in interval notation:

$$|2x - 3| \leq 5 \iff -5 \leq 2x - 3 \leq 5 \iff -2 \leq 2x \leq 8 \iff -1 \leq x \leq 4$$

Answer: $x \in [-1, 4]$.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

Set theory is the foundation ZFC (Zermelo-Fraenkel + Axiom of Choice) that underpins all modern mathematics. The "intuitive" definitions we present in this lesson have known pitfalls:

- **Russell's Paradox**: the set of all sets that do not contain themselves. If it contains itself, then it doesn't. If it doesn't, then it does. This broke Cantor's naive theory (1901) and led Zermelo to axiomatize the theory with restricted comprehension.

- **Continuum Hypothesis**: Cantor conjectured that no cardinality exists between $|\mathbb{N}|$ and $|\mathbb{R}|$. Gödel (1940) and Cohen (1963) proved the hypothesis is **independent** of ZFC axioms — both it and its negation are consistent with ZFC.

- **Axiom of Choice**: apparently innocent, but implies strange results like Banach-Tarski (decompose a sphere and reassemble two identical to the original). Deciding to adopt AC is practical but philosophically delicate.

In professional practice (engineering, applied science, finance), none of this appears. You use $\mathbb{R}^n$ as Euclidean space and move on. But if you ever pursue graduate study in Analysis, Logic, or Set Theory, you'll come back to these paradoxes.

### What is "real" anyway?

Computationally, ℝ does not exist — every digital implementation uses **floating-point** (IEEE 754), with ~7 digits of precision (float32) or ~15 (float64). Floating-point errors propagate in numerical computation (covered in Term 10 with REAMAT). When you write `0.1 + 0.2` in Python, you get `0.30000000000000004`. $\mathbb{Q} \subsetneq \mathbb{R}$ is mathematical abstraction; the computer works with a much smaller finite subset of $\mathbb{Q}$.

</Porta>

## Worked examples

<Exemplo titulo="Listing set elements (application)" numero="1">

**Problem:** List, using set notation, the set $A = \{x \in \mathbb{Z} : -2 < x \leq 3\}$.

**Strategy:** The problem asks you to list the elements of a set. The set notation gives us two pieces of information:
1. Which number set the elements belong to: $\mathbb{Z}$ (integers).
2. Which conditions those elements must satisfy: $-2 < x \leq 3$.
We'll combine these to identify all integers that fit.

**Solution:**

1. **Identify the number type:** Elements $x$ must be integers ($\mathbb{Z}$).
2. **Analyze the first condition:** $x > -2$. This means $x$ must be greater than $-2$. The integers satisfying this are $-1, 0, 1, 2, 3, \ldots$.
3. **Analyze the second condition:** $x \leq 3$. This means $x$ must be less than or equal to $3$. The integers satisfying this are $\ldots, 1, 2, 3$.
4. **Combine the conditions:** We need integers $x$ that are *simultaneously* greater than $-2$ AND less than or equal to $3$.
    *   Integers greater than $-2$: $\{-1, 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, \ldots\}$
    *   Integers less than or equal to $3$: $\{\ldots, -1, 0, 1, 2, 3\}$
    *   The intersection of these two sets is $\{-1, 0, 1, 2, 3\}$.

**Verification:** All listed numbers are integers.
- $-1$ is greater than $-2$ and less than or equal to $3$. (OK)
- $0$ is greater than $-2$ and less than or equal to $3$. (OK)
- $1$ is greater than $-2$ and less than or equal to $3$. (OK)
- $2$ is greater than $-2$ and less than or equal to $3$. (OK)
- $3$ is greater than $-2$ and less than or equal to $3$. (OK)
No other integer between $-2$ and $3$ (excluding $-2$, including $3$) was left out.

**Source.** [OpenStax — College Algebra 2e §1.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/1-1-real-numbers-algebra-essentials) — CC-BY 4.0 license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Interval intersection (intermediate)" numero="2">

**Problem:** Given intervals $A = [-3, 7)$ and $B = (1, 10]$, calculate $A \cap B$.

**Strategy:** The intersection of two sets includes only elements that belong *to both* sets. Again, visualization on the number line is the most effective tool.

**Solution:**

1. **Visualize interval $A$:** $A = [-3, 7)$ goes from -3 (included) to 7 (excluded).
    ```
    ←—————[----------)————→
         -3          7
    ```
2. **Visualize interval $B$:** $B = (1, 10]$ goes from 1 (excluded) to 10 (included).
    ```
    ←——————————(----------]————→
               1          10
    ```
3. **Combine for the intersection:** We need to find the region where both intervals overlap.
    *   The leftmost point where both are present: $1$. But $1$ is excluded in $B$, so it's excluded in the intersection.
    *   The rightmost point where both are present: $7$. But $7$ is excluded in $A$, so it's excluded in the intersection.
    *   The overlapping region is between $1$ (excluded) and $7$ (excluded).

$$ A \cap B = (1, 7) $$

**Verification:**
- A point in the intersection: $x=3$. It's in $A$ (since $-3 \leq 3 < 7$) and in $B$ (since $1 < 3 \leq 10$). (OK)
- A point only in $A$: $x=-2$. Not in $B$. (OK)
- A point only in $B$: $x=8$. Not in $A$. (OK)
- The endpoints: $1$ is not in $B$, $7$ is not in $A$. Both excluded in the intersection.
The answer is consistent.

**Source.** [Stitz–Zeager — Precalculus §1.2](https://www.stitz-zeager.com/szprecalculus07042013.pdf) — CC-BY-NC-SA license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Absolute value inequality, type |u| ≤ c (intermediate)" numero="3">

**Problem:** Solve the inequality $|x - 4| \leq 3$ and express the solution in interval notation.

**Strategy:** Inequalities with absolute value of the form $|u| \leq c$ (where $c > 0$) can be rewritten as a compound inequality: $-c \leq u \leq c$. We'll apply this property and then solve for $x$.

**Solution:**

1. **Rewrite the inequality:** The absolute value property lets us transform $|x - 4| \leq 3$ into:
    $$ -3 \leq x - 4 \leq 3 $$
2. **Isolate $x$:** To isolate $x$, we add $4$ to all parts of the inequality:
    $$ -3 + 4 \leq x - 4 + 4 \leq 3 + 4 $$
    $$ 1 \leq x \leq 7 $$
3. **Express in interval notation:** The inequality $1 \leq x \leq 7$ means $x$ can be any real number between 1 and 7, including 1 and 7.

$$ \text{Solution: } [1, 7] $$

**Verification:**
- Test a point inside the interval: $x=5$. $|5 - 4| = |1| = 1$. $1 \leq 3$. (OK)
- Test an endpoint: $x=1$. $|1 - 4| = |-3| = 3$. $3 \leq 3$. (OK)
- Test the other endpoint: $x=7$. $|7 - 4| = |3| = 3$. $3 \leq 3$. (OK)
- Test a point outside the interval: $x=0$. $|0 - 4| = |-4| = 4$. $4 \not\leq 3$. (OK)
- Test a point outside the interval: $x=8$. $|8 - 4| = |4| = 4$. $4 \not\leq 3$. (OK)
The solution is correct.

**Source.** [OpenStax — College Algebra 2e §1.7](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/1-7-linear-inequalities-and-absolute-value-inequalities) — CC-BY 4.0 license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Quadratic inequality (advanced)" numero="4">

**Problem:** Determine the solution set of the inequality $x^2 - x - 6 > 0$ in interval notation.

**Strategy:** To solve a quadratic inequality, we first find the roots of the associated quadratic equation ($x^2 - x - 6 = 0$). These roots divide the real line into intervals. Next, we test a point in each interval to see where the inequality is satisfied.

**Solution:**

1. **Find the roots of the associated equation:** $x^2 - x - 6 = 0$.
    *   We can factor the equation: $(x - 3)(x + 2) = 0$.
    *   The roots are $x = 3$ and $x = -2$.
2. **Divide the real line into intervals:** The roots $-2$ and $3$ divide the real line into three intervals:
    *   $(-\infty, -2)$
    *   $(-2, 3)$
    *   $(3, +\infty)$
3. **Test a point in each interval:**
    *   **Interval $(-\infty, -2)$:** Choose $x = -3$.
        $(-3)^2 - (-3) - 6 = 9 + 3 - 6 = 6$. Since $6 > 0$, this interval *satisfies* the inequality.
    *   **Interval $(-2, 3)$:** Choose $x = 0$.
        $(0)^2 - (0) - 6 = -6$. Since $-6 \not> 0$, this interval *does not satisfy* the inequality.
    *   **Interval $(3, +\infty)$:** Choose $x = 4$.
        $(4)^2 - (4) - 6 = 16 - 4 - 6 = 6$. Since $6 > 0$, this interval *satisfies* the inequality.
4. **Combine the intervals that satisfy:** The solution is the union of the intervals where the inequality is true. Note that the inequality is strictly greater than 0, so the roots are not included.

$$ \text{Solution: } (-\infty, -2) \cup (3, +\infty) $$

**Verification:**
- The parabola $y = x^2 - x - 6$ opens upward (coefficient of $x^2$ is positive). This means the function values are positive "outside" the roots and negative "between" the roots. The solution found is consistent with the graph of an upward-opening parabola.

**Source.** [OpenStax — College Algebra 2e §3.6](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/3-6-other-types-of-equations) — CC-BY 4.0 license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Modeling with point exclusion (real modeling)" numero="5">

**Problem:** A temperature sensor operates correctly in an environment where temperature $T$ (in Celsius) must be between $10\,°C$ (inclusive) and $40\,°C$ (exclusive). Additionally, by a specific requirement, the temperature cannot be exactly $25\,°C$. Express the valid operating range for $T$ as a union of intervals.

**Strategy:** First, define the base operating interval. Then, remove the specific point that is not allowed. Removing a single point from an interval can result in two disjoint intervals.

**Solution:**

1. **Define the base interval:** The temperature must be between $10\,°C$ (inclusive) and $40\,°C$ (exclusive). This is represented by the interval $[10, 40)$.
2. **Identify the point to be excluded:** The temperature cannot be $25\,°C$.
3. **Remove the point from the base interval:** The number $25$ is inside the interval $[10, 40)$. Removing $25$ divides the interval into two parts:
    *   All numbers from $10$ (inclusive) to $25$ (exclusive): $[10, 25)$.
    *   All numbers from $25$ (exclusive) to $40$ (exclusive): $(25, 40)$.
4. **Express as union of intervals:** The valid operating range is the union of these two disjoint intervals.

$$ \text{Operating range: } [10, 25) \cup (25, 40) $$

**Verification:**
- $T=10$: Valid. It is in the first interval.

      <li><strong>Write the solution interval.</strong> The inequality is <Eq>{`\\leq`}</Eq> (non-strict), so roots <Eq>{`2`}</Eq> and <Eq>{`3`}</Eq> are included. The solution set in <Eq>{`\\mathbb{R}`}</Eq> is the closed interval <Eq>{`[2, 3]`}</Eq>.</li>
      <li><strong>Count the integers in the interval.</strong> The integers belonging to <Eq>{`[2, 3]`}</Eq> are exactly <Eq>{`\\{2, 3\\}`}</Eq>. So there are <strong>2</strong> integers.</li>
    </ol>
    <p><em>Mental shortcut: for a quadratic with upward concavity, "<Eq>{`\\leq 0`}</Eq>" means "between the roots (included)" and "<Eq>{`\\geq 0`}</Eq>" means "outside the roots (included)". Invert for downward concavity. This shortcut saves the sign-test step when you've already recognized the sign of the leading coefficient.</em></p>
