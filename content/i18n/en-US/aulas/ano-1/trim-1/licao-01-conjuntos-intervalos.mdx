### Fundamental number sets

<Definicao titulo="Hierarchy of the real numbers">

- **Naturals** $\mathbb{N} = \{0, 1, 2, 3, \ldots\}$. (Brazilian convention includes zero; French convention excludes it.)
- **Integers** $\mathbb{Z} = \{\ldots, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, \ldots\}$ — closed under addition, subtraction, and multiplication.
- **Rationals** $\mathbb{Q} = \{p/q \mid p \in \mathbb{Z},\ q \in \mathbb{Z}_{\neq 0}\}$ — every number expressible as a ratio of integers.
- **Reals** $\mathbb{R}$ — completes the line (fills the "gaps" such as $\sqrt{2}$, $\pi$, $e$).

</Definicao>

> "Every real number corresponds to a unique position on the number line. The converse is also true: every location on the number line corresponds to exactly one real number." — [OpenStax College Algebra 2e, §1.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/1-1-real-numbers-algebra-essentials#Figure_01_01_001)

<Definicao titulo="Rational and irrational numbers">

> "The set of rational numbers is written as $\lbrace m/n \mid m \text{ and } n \text{ are integers and } n \neq 0 \rbrace$. Notice from the definition that rational numbers are fractions (or quotients) containing integers in both the numerator and the denominator, and the denominator is never 0." — [OpenStax College Algebra 2e, §1.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/1-1-real-numbers-algebra-essentials#fs-id11713028028)

> "Such numbers are called *irrational* because they cannot be written as fractions. These numbers make up the set of irrational numbers." — [OpenStax College Algebra 2e, §1.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/1-1-real-numbers-algebra-essentials#fs-id11713028028)

</Definicao>

### Set operations

<Definicao titulo="Union, intersection, difference, complement">

- **Union**: $A \cup B = \{x : x \in A \text{ or } x \in B\}$
- **Intersection**: $A \cap B = \{x : x \in A \text{ and } x \in B\}$
- **Difference**: $A \setminus B = \{x \in A : x \notin B\}$
- **Complement** (in a universe $U$): $A^c = U \setminus A$

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

Imagine you have a box of **toys**. Another box with **books**.

- **Union** (∪) is putting both boxes into one. It has toys AND books.
- **Intersection** (∩) is what is in BOTH boxes at the same time. Usually empty — a toy is not a book.
- But if one box has "**red balls**" and the other has "**small balls**", the intersection is the balls that are **red AND small**.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

Mathematicians love organizing numbers into families.

- The family of **naturals** $\mathbb{N}$ is just what you use to count things: 0, 1, 2, 3, ...
- The family of **integers** $\mathbb{Z}$ adds the negatives: ..., −2, −1, 0, 1, 2, ...
- The family of **rationals** $\mathbb{Q}$ adds fractions: 1/2, 3/4, −5/7, ...
- The family of **reals** $\mathbb{R}$ adds strange numbers like **π** (3.14159...) and $\sqrt{2}$ (1.41421...).

And **intervals** are short ways of saying "all numbers between such and such":
- `[0, 5]` means "every number from 0 to 5, **including** 0 and 5".
- `(0, 5)` means "every number from 0 to 5, **excluding** 0 and 5".

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old">

This is the lesson that establishes the **language** we will use over the next 3 years. All advanced mathematics — calculus, linear algebra, statistics — uses this notation. Without it, it is like trying to read a physics book without knowing the alphabet.

**The 4 central number sets:**

| Symbol | Name | Example |
|---|---|---|
| $\mathbb{N}$ | naturals | 0, 1, 2, 3, ... |
| $\mathbb{Z}$ | integers | ..., −3, −2, −1, 0, 1, 2, ... |
| $\mathbb{Q}$ | rationals | 1/2, 3/4, −7/5, 0.333... = 1/3 |
| $\mathbb{R}$ | reals | all of the above + $\pi$, $\sqrt{2}$, $e$ |

And an important observation: **not every real number is rational**. The irrationals (such as $\sqrt{2}$ and $\pi$) are real numbers that CANNOT be written as a fraction of integers. This is a theorem known since Pythagoras (5th century BC) — an elegant proof you will see in exercise 1.27 of this lesson.

**Intervals** are abbreviations for "all real numbers within given bounds":

- `[3, 7]` = $\{x \in \mathbb{R} : 3 \leq x \leq 7\}$ (closed, endpoints included)
- `(3, 7)` = $\{x : 3 < x < 7\}$ (open, no endpoints)

When your teacher writes $x \in [3, 7]$ on the board, they are saying: "x is any real number between 3 and 7, including 3 and 7". Very compact. Compare with writing that out in full.

<Insight>

**Why does the difference between $[3, 7]$ and $(3, 7)$ matter?** Because in advanced mathematics, "including the point" and "approaching it without reaching it" are different phenomena. When you study **limits** in a few months, you will see that $\lim_{x \to 3^+}$ asks what happens *when x approaches 3 without being 3*. The difference between open and closed starts here, now, at age 15.

</Insight>

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

This lesson corresponds roughly to Japanese Math I (ch. 1) plus part of German Klasse 10. The content appears trivial — but notation errors here propagate across all 12 trimesters.

### Critical points

**1. Does ℕ include 0?** Brazilian convention (BNCC) and French: YES. American and several classical conventions: NO. We adopt **0 ∈ ℕ** throughout this program, aligned with the BNCC. When reading foreign textbooks, check the author's convention.

**2. Brazilian vs. Anglo-Saxon interval notation:**
- BR: $]a, b[$ for open, $[a, b]$ for closed (Bourbaki notation inherited from France)
- Anglo: $(a, b)$ for open, $[a, b]$ for closed

We adopt **Anglo notation** (parentheses for open) for compatibility with 90% of the open textbooks referenced in `livros/CATALOG.md`.

> "In this book, we use interval notation with parentheses for open intervals and brackets for closed intervals, as is common in English-language literature." — [Stitz-Zeager, Precalculus, Ch. 1](https://www.stitz-zeager.com/szprecalculus07042013.pdf)

**3. Density of ℚ in ℝ.** Between any two distinct reals $a < b$, there exists a rational $q$ with $a < q < b$. **Proof** (Exercise 1.29): use the Archimedean property — for every $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists $n \in \mathbb{N}$ such that $1/n < \varepsilon$.

**4. Finite vs. countably infinite vs. uncountable sets.** Cantor (1874) proved that ℝ is **uncountable** but ℚ is **countable** — both are infinite, but with distinct cardinalities. You will use this in university Analysis (Calculus IV / Real Analysis).

### Properties of the Real Numbers

> "The commutative property of addition states that numbers may be added in any order without affecting the sum." — [OpenStax College Algebra 2e, §1.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/1-1-real-numbers-algebra-essentials#fs-id11713028028)

> "The distributive property states that the product of a factor times a sum is the sum of the factor times each term in the sum." — [OpenStax College Algebra 2e, §1.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/1-1-real-numbers-algebra-essentials#fs-id11713028028)

### Cross-domain connections

- **Calculus**: the domain of a function is always a subset of ℝ.
- **Probability**: the sample space is a set.
- **Linear Algebra**: vectors are tuples in ℝⁿ or ℂⁿ.
- **Computing**: primitive types `int`, `float`, `bool` correspond to ℤ (bounded), ℝ (finite precision), $\{T, F\}$.

### Numerical example

Solve $|2x - 3| \leq 5$ and express the result in interval notation:

$$|2x - 3| \leq 5 \iff -5 \leq 2x - 3 \leq 5 \iff -2 \leq 2x \leq 8 \iff -1 \leq x \leq 4$$

Answer: $x \in [-1, 4]$.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

Set theory is the ZFC foundation (Zermelo-Fraenkel + Axiom of Choice) that underpins all of modern mathematics. The intuitive "definitions" presented in this lesson carry known pitfalls:

- **Russell's Paradox**: the set of all sets that do not contain themselves. If it contains itself, then it does not. If it does not contain itself, then it does. This broke Cantor's naive theory (1901) and led Zermelo to axiomatize set theory with a restricted comprehension scheme.

- **Continuum Hypothesis**: Cantor conjectured that there is no cardinality between $|\mathbb{N}|$ and $|\mathbb{R}|$. Gödel (1940) and Cohen (1963) proved that the hypothesis is **independent** of the ZFC axioms — both it and its negation are consistent with ZFC.

- **Axiom of Choice**: apparently innocent, but implies strange results such as Banach-Tarski (decomposing a sphere and reassembling two spheres identical to the original). The decision to adopt AC is practical but philosophically delicate.

In professional practice (engineering, applied science, finance), none of this appears. You use $\mathbb{R}^n$ as Euclidean space and proceed. But if you ever pursue graduate study in Analysis, Logic, or Set Theory, you will return to these paradoxes.

### What is "real" anyway?

Computationally, ℝ does not exist — every digital implementation uses **floating point** (IEEE 754), with ~7 digits of precision (float32) or ~15 (float64). Floating-point errors propagate in numerical computation (covered in Trimester 10 with REAMAT). When you write `0.1 + 0.2` in Python, you get `0.30000000000000004`. $\mathbb{Q} \subsetneq \mathbb{R}$ is a mathematical abstraction; the computer works in a finite subset far smaller than $\mathbb{Q}$.

</Porta>

### Example 3: Fasting blood glucose ranges (medicine)

| Range | Interval (mg/dL) | Diagnosis |
|---|---|---|
| Normal | $[0, 99]$ | healthy |
| Pre-diabetes | $[100, 125]$ | watch |
| Diabetes | $[126, +\infty)$ | treatment |

### Example 4: Domain of a cost function

Cost function $C(q) = 50 + 12q + 0.5q^2$ for manufacturing $q$ units.

Mathematical domain: $C: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$.

When the calculus "optimal point" gives $q^* = 17.3$, it is your job as an engineer to decide whether to round to 17 or 18 — continuous mathematics produces a solution in ℝ; the application demands a solution in ℕ. ## Worked examples

<Exemplo titulo="Listing elements of a set (application)" numero="1">

**Problem:** List, in brace notation, the set $A = \{x \in \mathbb{Z} : -2 < x \leq 3\}$.

**Strategy:** The problem asks us to list the elements of a set. The set notation gives us two pieces of information:
1.  Which number set the elements belong to: $\mathbb{Z}$ (integers).
2.  Which conditions those elements must satisfy: $-2 < x \leq 3$.
We combine this information to identify all integers that fit.

**Solution:**

1.  **Identify the type of number:** The elements $x$ must be integers ($\mathbb{Z}$).
2.  **Analyze the first condition:** $x > -2$. This means $x$ must be greater than $-2$. The integers satisfying this part are $-1, 0, 1, 2, 3, \ldots$.
3.  **Analyze the second condition:** $x \leq 3$. This means $x$ must be less than or equal to $3$. The integers satisfying this part are $\ldots, 1, 2, 3$.
4.  **Combine the conditions:** We need integers $x$ that are *simultaneously* greater than $-2$ AND less than or equal to $3$.
    *   Integers greater than $-2$: $\{-1, 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, \ldots\}$
    *   Integers less than or equal to $3$: $\{\ldots, -1, 0, 1, 2, 3\}$
    *   The intersection of these two sets is $\{-1, 0, 1, 2, 3\}$.

**Verification:** All listed numbers are integers.
- $-1$ is greater than $-2$ and less than or equal to $3$. (OK)
- $0$ is greater than $-2$ and less than or equal to $3$. (OK)
- $1$ is greater than $-2$ and less than or equal to $3$. (OK)
- $2$ is greater than $-2$ and less than or equal to $3$. (OK)
- $3$ is greater than $-2$ and less than or equal to $3$. (OK)
No other integer between $-2$ and $3$ (excluding $-2$, including $3$) was left out.


**Source.** [OpenStax — College Algebra 2e §1.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/1-1-real-numbers-algebra-essentials) — license CC-BY 4.0.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Intersection of intervals (intermediate)" numero="2">

**Problem:** Given intervals $A = [-3, 7)$ and $B = (1, 10]$, compute $A \cap B$.

**Strategy:** The intersection of two sets contains only the elements that belong to *both* sets. Visualization on the number line is the most effective tool.

**Solution:**

1.  **Visualize interval $A$:** $A = [-3, 7)$ goes from -3 (included) to 7 (excluded).
    ```
    ←—————[----------)————→
         -3          7
    ```
2.  **Visualize interval $B$:** $B = (1, 10]$ goes from 1 (excluded) to 10 (included).
    ```
    ←——————————(----------]————→
               1          10
    ```
3.  **Combine for the intersection:** We find the region where both intervals overlap.
    *   The leftmost point where both are present: $1$. But $1$ is excluded in $B$, so it is excluded in the intersection.
    *   The rightmost point where both are present: $7$. But $7$ is excluded in $A$, so it is excluded in the intersection.
    *   The overlap region is between $1$ (excluded) and $7$ (excluded).

$$ A \cap B = (1, 7) $$

**Verification:**
- A point in the intersection: $x=3$. It is in $A$ (since $-3 \leq 3 < 7$) and in $B$ (since $1 < 3 \leq 10$). (OK)
- A point only in $A$: $x=-2$. Not in $B$. (OK)
- A point only in $B$: $x=8$. Not in $A$. (OK)
- The endpoints: $1$ is not in $B$, $7$ is not in $A$. Both excluded from the intersection.
The answer is consistent.


**Source.** [Stitz–Zeager — Precalculus §1.2](https://www.stitz-zeager.com/szprecalculus07042013.pdf) — license CC-BY-NC-SA.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Absolute value inequality, type |u| ≤ c (intermediate)" numero="3">

**Problem:** Solve the inequality $|x - 4| \leq 3$ and express the solution in interval notation.

**Strategy:** Absolute value inequalities of the type $|u| \leq c$ (where $c > 0$) can be rewritten as a compound inequality: $-c \leq u \leq c$. We apply this property and then solve for $x$.

**Solution:**

1.  **Rewrite the inequality:** The absolute value property lets us transform $|x - 4| \leq 3$ into:
    $$ -3 \leq x - 4 \leq 3 $$
2.  **Isolate $x$:** Add $4$ to all parts:
    $$ -3 + 4 \leq x - 4 + 4 \leq 3 + 4 $$
    $$ 1 \leq x \leq 7 $$
3.  **Express in interval notation:** The inequality $1 \leq x \leq 7$ means $x$ can be any real number between 1 and 7, including 1 and 7.

$$ \text{Solution: } [1, 7] $$

**Verification:**
- Test a point inside: $x=5$. $|5 - 4| = |1| = 1$. $1 \leq 3$. (OK)
- Test one endpoint: $x=1$. $|1 - 4| = |-3| = 3$. $3 \leq 3$. (OK)
- Test the other endpoint: $x=7$. $|7 - 4| = |3| = 3$. $3 \leq 3$. (OK)
- Test a point outside: $x=0$. $|0 - 4| = |-4| = 4$. $4 \not\leq 3$. (OK)
- Test a point outside: $x=8$. $|8 - 4| = |4| = 4$. $4 \not\leq 3$. (OK)
The solution is correct.


**Source.** [OpenStax — College Algebra 2e §1.7](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/1-7-linear-inequalities-and-absolute-value-inequalities) — license CC-BY 4.0.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Quadratic inequality (advanced)" numero="4">

**Problem:** Find the solution set of the inequality $x^2 - x - 6 > 0$ in interval notation.

**Strategy:** To solve a quadratic inequality, we first find the roots of the associated quadratic equation ($x^2 - x - 6 = 0$). These roots divide the real line into intervals. We then test a point in each interval to see where the inequality holds.

**Solution:**

1.  **Find the roots of the associated equation:** $x^2 - x - 6 = 0$.
    *   Factor: $(x - 3)(x + 2) = 0$.
    *   The roots are $x = 3$ and $x = -2$.
2.  **Divide the real line into intervals:** The roots $-2$ and $3$ split the real line into three intervals:
    *   $(-\infty, -2)$
    *   $(-2, 3)$
    *   $(3, +\infty)$
3.  **Test a point in each interval:**
    *   **Interval $(-\infty, -2)$:** Choose $x = -3$.
        $(-3)^2 - (-3) - 6 = 9 + 3 - 6 = 6$. Since $6 > 0$, this interval *satisfies* the inequality.
    *   **Interval $(-2, 3)$:** Choose $x = 0$.
        $(0)^2 - (0) - 6 = -6$. Since $-6 \not> 0$, this interval *does not satisfy* the inequality.
    *   **Interval $(3, +\infty)$:** Choose $x = 4$.
        $(4)^2 - (4) - 6 = 16 - 4 - 6 = 6$. Since $6 > 0$, this interval *satisfies* the inequality.
4.  **Combine the satisfying intervals:** The solution is the union of the intervals where the inequality holds. Since the inequality is strictly greater than 0, the roots are not included.

$$ \text{Solution: } (-\infty, -2) \cup (3, +\infty) $$

**Verification:**
- The parabola $y = x^2 - x - 6$ opens upward (positive leading coefficient). This means the function values are positive "outside" the roots and negative "between" them. The solution found is consistent with the graph of an upward-opening parabola.


**Source.** [OpenStax — College Algebra 2e §3.6](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/3-6-other-types-of-equations) — license CC-BY 4.0.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Modeling with a point exclusion (real-world modeling)" numero="5">

**Problem:** A temperature sensor operates correctly when the ambient temperature $T$ (in Celsius) is between $10\,°C$ (inclusive) and $40\,°C$ (exclusive). Additionally, due to a specific requirement, the temperature cannot be exactly $25\,°C$. Express the valid operating range for $T$ as a union of intervals.

**Strategy:** First define the base operating interval. Then remove the specific point that is not allowed. Removing a single point from an interval can produce two disjoint intervals.

**Solution:**

1.  **Define the base interval:** The temperature must be between $10\,°C$ (inclusive) and $40\,°C$ (exclusive). This is represented by the interval $[10, 40)$.
2.  **Identify the point to exclude:** The temperature cannot be $25\,°C$.
3.  **Remove the point from the base interval:** The number $25$ lies inside $[10, 40)$. Removing $25$ splits the interval into two parts:
    *   All numbers from $10$ (inclusive) to $25$ (exclusive): $[10, 25)$.
    *   All numbers from $25$ (exclusive) to $40$ (exclusive): $(25, 40)$.
4.  **Express as a union:** The valid operating range is the union of these two disjoint intervals.

$$ \text{Operating range: } [10, 25) \cup (25, 40) $$

**Verification:**
- $T=10$: Valid. In the first interval.
- $T=20$: Valid. In the first interval.
- $T=25$: Not valid. In neither interval.
- $T=30$: Valid. In the second interval.
- $T=40$: Not valid. Excluded from the original interval.
The solution is correct and satisfies all conditions.


**Source.** [Yoshiwara — Modeling, Functions, and Graphs ch. 1](https://textbooks.aimath.org/textbooks/approved-textbooks/yoshiwara/) — free license.
</Exemplo>

<Exercicio numero="1.50" dificuldade="modelagem"
  solucao={<>
    <Eq>{`P(q) = \\begin{cases} 1.80q & \\text{if } 0 \\leq q \\leq 1000 \\\\ 2.00q & \\text{if } 1000 < q \\leq 5000 \\\\ 2.30q & \\text{if } q > 5000 \\end{cases}`}</Eq>

  passos={<>
    <ol>
      <li><strong>Identify the domain and bands.</strong> The liters produced <Eq>{`q`}</Eq> is a non-negative real number: <Eq>{`q \\in [0, +\\infty)`}</Eq>. The problem describes three price bands: up to 1,000 L, between 1,000 and 5,000 L, and above 5,000 L.       <li><strong>Handle the boundary (the part that most often trips students up).</strong> The problem says "up to 1,000 L pays R\$ 1.80/L; between 1,000 and 5,000 L pays R\$ 2.00/L". Who gets the exact point <Eq>{`q = 1000`}</Eq>? A fair reading of "up to" includes the endpoint, so 1,000 L is paid at R\$ 1.80/L. The next point (<Eq>{`q = 5000`}</Eq>) follows the same convention: "between 1,000 and 5,000" includes 5,000 at R\$ 2.00.       <li><strong>Write each branch as a linear function.</strong> Since the unit price is constant within each band and payment starts at zero (no fixed fee), each branch has the form <Eq>{`P(q) = pq`}</Eq>, where <Eq>{`p`}</Eq> is the price per liter in that band.</li>
      <li><strong>Assemble the piecewise function.</strong>
        <br />
        <Eq>{`P(q) = \\begin{cases} 1{,}80\\,q & \\text{if } 0 \\leq q \\leq 1000 \\\\ 2{,}00\\,q & \\text{if } 1000 < q \\leq 5000 \\\\ 2{,}30\\,q & \\text{if } q > 5000 \\end{cases}`}</Eq>

<Exercicio numero="1.57" dificuldade="desafio"
  solucao={<>At step <Eq>{`n`}</Eq>, each of the <Eq>{`2^{n-1}`}</Eq> remaining intervals from the previous step is divided into three, and the middle third is removed. The length of each interval becomes <Eq>{` (1/3)^n `}</Eq>, and the number of intervals is <Eq>{`2^n`}</Eq>. Thus the total length is <Eq>{` (2/3)^n `}</Eq>. As <Eq>{`n \\to \\infty`}</Eq>, this length tends to 0.</>}
  passos={<>
    <ol>
      <li><strong>Step 0 (starting point).</strong> We begin with the interval <Eq>{`[0, 1]`}</Eq>: 1 interval, total length <Eq>{`L_0 = 1`}</Eq>.</li>
      <li><strong>Step 1 (first removal).</strong> We remove the open middle third <Eq>{`(1/3,\\ 2/3)`}</Eq>. What remains is <Eq>{`[0,\\ 1/3] \\cup [2/3,\\ 1]`}</Eq>: 2 intervals each of length <Eq>{`1/3`}</Eq>. Total length <Eq>{`L_1 = 2 \\times \\tfrac{1}{3} = \\tfrac{2}{3}`}</Eq>.</li>
      <li><strong>Step 2 (recursion).</strong> We remove the middle third of each of the 2 intervals. Now there are 4 intervals each of length <Eq>{`(1/3)^2 = 1/9`}</Eq>. Total <Eq>{`L_2 = 4 \\times \\tfrac{1}{9} = \\tfrac{4}{9} = \\left(\\tfrac{2}{3}\\right)^2`}</Eq>.</li>
      <li><strong>Identify the pattern.</strong> At each step the number of intervals doubles (each becomes two) and each length shrinks by a factor of 3. After <Eq>{`n`}</Eq> steps: <Eq>{`2^n`}</Eq> intervals each of length <Eq>{`(1/3)^n`}</Eq>.</li>
      <li><strong>Total length at step n.</strong> Multiplying: <Eq>{`L_n = 2^n \\cdot \\left(\\tfrac{1}{3}\\right)^n = \\left(\\tfrac{2}{3}\\right)^n`}</Eq>. This is a geometric sequence with ratio <Eq>{`2/3`}</Eq>.</li>
      <li><strong>Limit as n → ∞.</strong> Since the ratio satisfies <Eq>{`0 < 2/3 < 1`}</Eq>, the sequence <Eq>{`(2/3)^n`}</Eq> tends monotonically to zero. Hence <Eq>{`\\lim_{n \\to \\infty} L_n = 0`}</Eq>.</li>
    </ol>
    <p><em>Note. Here is the paradox that shook mathematicians at the end of the 19th century: the Cantor set — what remains when we take the infinite intersection of all steps — has <strong>measure zero</strong>, yet contains an <strong>uncountable</strong> number of points (the same cardinality as <Eq>{`\\mathbb{R}`}</Eq>). In other words, it is a set "the size of the entire real line" packed into measure zero. Georg Cantor (1883) constructed this example precisely to show that cardinality and measure are two completely different concepts — one of the most unsettling discoveries in modern mathematics, which founded measure theory and opened the way to topology, fractals, and chaos theory.</em></p>
  </>}
>
The Cantor set is constructed by recursively removing the middle third of $[0, 1]$. After $n$ steps, what is the total length of the remaining intervals? What value does this length approach as $n \to \infty$?
</Exercicio>
