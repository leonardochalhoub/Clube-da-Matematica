### Fundamental number sets

<Definicao titulo="Hierarchy of real numbers">

- **Naturals** $\mathbb{N} = \{0, 1, 2, 3, \ldots\}$. (Brazilian convention includes zero; French convention excludes it.)
- **Integers** $\mathbb{Z} = \{\ldots, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, \ldots\}$ — closed under addition, subtraction and multiplication.
- **Rationals** $\mathbb{Q} = \{p/q \mid p \in \mathbb{Z},\ q \in \mathbb{Z}_{\neq 0}\}$ — every number expressible as a ratio of integers.
- **Reals** $\mathbb{R}$ — completes the line (fills the "gaps" like $\sqrt{2}$, $\pi$, $e$).

</Definicao>

> "Any real number corresponds to a unique position on the number line. Conversely, each location on the number line corresponds to exactly one real number." — [OpenStax College Algebra 2e, §1.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/1-1-real-numbers-algebra-essentials#Figure_01_01_001)

<Definicao titulo="Rational and irrational numbers">

> "The set of rational numbers is written as $\lbrace m/n \mid m \text{ and } n \text{ are integers and } n \neq 0 \rbrace$. From the definition, rational numbers are fractions (or quotients) containing integers in both numerator and denominator, and the denominator is never 0." — [OpenStax College Algebra 2e, §1.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/1-1-real-numbers-algebra-essentials#fs-id11713028028)

> "Such numbers are called *irrational* because they cannot be written as fractions. These numbers make up the set of irrational numbers." — [OpenStax College Algebra 2e, §1.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/1-1-real-numbers-algebra-essentials#fs-id11713028028)

</Definicao>

### Set operations

<Definicao titulo="Union, intersection, difference, complement">

- **Union**: $A \cup B = \{x : x \in A \text{ or } x \in B\}$
- **Intersection**: $A \cap B = \{x : x \in A \text{ and } x \in B\}$
- **Difference**: $A \setminus B = \{x \in A : x \notin B\}$
- **Complement** (in a universe $U$): $A^c = U \setminus A$

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

Imagine you have a box with **toys**. Another box with **books**.

- **Union** (∪) is putting both boxes together in one. Has toys AND books.
- **Intersection** (∩) is what's in BOTH boxes at the same time. Usually empty — a toy isn't a book.
- But if one box has "**red balls**" and the other has "**small balls**", the intersection is the balls that are **red AND small**.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

Mathematicians love organizing numbers into families.

- The family of **naturals** $\mathbb{N}$ is just what you use to count things: 0, 1, 2, 3, ...
- The family of **integers** $\mathbb{Z}$ adds the negatives: ..., −2, −1, 0, 1, 2, ...
- The family of **rationals** $\mathbb{Q}$ adds the fractions: 1/2, 3/4, −5/7, ...
- The family of **reals** $\mathbb{R}$ adds weird numbers like **π** (3.14159...) and $\sqrt{2}$ (1.41421...).

And **intervals** are short ways to say "all numbers between this and that":
- `[0, 5]` means "every number from 0 to 5, **including** 0 and 5".
- `(0, 5)` means "every number from 0 to 5, **without** 0 and without 5".

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old">

This is the lesson that establishes the **language** we'll use for the next 3 years. All advanced mathematics — calculus, linear algebra, statistics — uses this notation. Without it, it's like trying to read a physics book without knowing the letters.

**The 4 central number sets:**

| Symbol | Name | Example |
|---|---|---|
| $\mathbb{N}$ | naturals | 0, 1, 2, 3, ... |
| $\mathbb{Z}$ | integers | ..., −3, −2, −1, 0, 1, 2, ... |
| $\mathbb{Q}$ | rationals | 1/2, 3/4, −7/5, 0.333... = 1/3 |
| $\mathbb{R}$ | reals | all that + $\pi$, $\sqrt{2}$, $e$ |

And an important observation: **not every real number is rational**. The irrationals (like $\sqrt{2}$ and $\pi$) are real numbers that CANNOT be written as a fraction of integers. This is a theorem known since Pythagoras (5th century BCE) — an elegant proof you'll see in exercise 1.41 of this lesson.

**Intervals** are abbreviations for "all real numbers between these bounds":

- `[3, 7]` = $\{x \in \mathbb{R} : 3 \leq x \leq 7\}$ (closed, with boundaries included)
- `(3, 7)` = $\{x : 3 < x < 7\}$ (open, without the boundaries)

When the teacher writes $x \in [3, 7]$ on the board, they're saying: "x is any real number between 3 and 7, including 3 and 7". Ultra-compact. Compare with writing it out in full.

<Insight>

**Why is there a difference between $[3, 7]$ and $(3, 7)$?** Because in advanced mathematics, "include the point" and "approach it without reaching it" are different phenomena. When you study **limit** in a few months, you'll see that $\lim_{x \to 3^+}$ asks what happens *as x approaches 3 without being 3*. The difference between open and closed starts here, now, with you at 15 years old.

</Insight>

</Porta>

### Critical points

**1. Does ℕ include 0?** Brazilian convention (BNCC) and French: YES. American convention and some classics: NO. I adopt **0 ∈ ℕ** throughout this program, aligned with BNCC. When reading a foreign book, check the author's convention.

**2. Interval notation Brazilian vs. Anglo-Saxon:**
- BR: $]a, b[$ for open, $[a, b]$ for closed (Bourbaki notation inherited from France)
- Anglo: $(a, b)$ for open, $[a, b]$ for closed

I adopt **Anglo notation** (parentheses for open) for compatibility with 90% of the open books referenced in `livros/CATALOG.md`.

> "In this book, we will use interval notation with parentheses for open intervals and brackets for closed intervals, as is common in English-language literature." — [Stitz-Zeager, Precalculus, Ch. 1](https://www.stitz-zeager.com/szprecalculus07042013.pdf)

**3. Density of ℚ in ℝ.** Between any two distinct reals $a < b$, there exists a rational $q$ with $a < q < b$. **Proof** (Exercise 1.43): use the Archimedean property — for every $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists $n \in \mathbb{N}$ such that $1/n < \varepsilon$.

**4. Finite vs. countably infinite vs. uncountable sets.** Cantor (1874) proved that ℝ is **uncountable** but ℚ is **countable** — both infinite, but with distinct cardinalities. You'll use this fact in university Analysis (Calculus IV / Real Analysis).

### Properties of Real Numbers

> "The commutative property of addition states that numbers can be added in any order without affecting the sum." — [OpenStax College Algebra 2e, §1.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/1-1-real-numbers-algebra-essentials#fs-id11713028028)

> "The distributive property states that the product of a factor by a sum is the sum of the factor times each term of the sum." — [OpenStax College Algebra 2e, §1.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/1-1-real-numbers-algebra-essentials#fs-id11713028028)

### Cross-domain connection

- **Calculus**: domain of a function is always a subset of ℝ.
- **Probability**: sample space is a set.
- **Linear Algebra**: vectors are tuples in ℝⁿ or ℂⁿ.
- **Computing**: primitive types `int`, `float`, `bool` correspond to ℤ (with bound), ℝ (with finite precision), $\{T, F\}$.

### Numerical example

Solve $|2x - 3| \leq 5$ and express the result in interval notation:

$$|2x - 3| \leq 5 \iff -5 \leq 2x - 3 \leq 5 \iff -2 \leq 2x \leq 8 \iff -1 \leq x \leq 4$$

Answer: $x \in [-1, 4]$.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

Set theory is the ZFC foundation (Zermelo-Fraenkel + Axiom of Choice) that sustains all modern mathematics. The "intuitive" definitions we present in this lesson have known pitfalls:

- **Russell's Paradox**: the set of all sets that don't contain themselves. If it contains itself, then it doesn't. If it doesn't, then it does. This broke Cantor's naive theory (1901) and led Zermelo to axiomatize the theory with restricted comprehension.

- **Continuum Hypothesis**: Cantor conjectured that no cardinality exists between $|\mathbb{N}|$ and $|\mathbb{R}|$. Gödel (1940) and Cohen (1963) proved that the hypothesis is **independent** of ZFC axioms — both it and its negation are consistent with ZFC.

- **Axiom of Choice**: apparently innocent, but implies strange results like Banach-Tarski (decompose a sphere and reassemble two identical to the original). The decision to adopt AC is practical but philosophically delicate.

In professional practice (engineering, applied science, finance), none of this appears. You use $\mathbb{R}^n$ as Euclidean space and move on. But if one day you pursue graduate studies in Analysis, Logic or Set Theory, you'll revisit these paradoxes.

### What is "real" anyway?

Computationally, ℝ doesn't exist — all digital implementation uses **floating-point** (IEEE 754), with ~7 digits of precision (float32) or ~15 (float64). Floating-point errors propagate in numerical computation (covered in Trimester 10 with REAMAT). When you write `0.1 + 0.2` in Python, you get `0.30000000000000004`. $\mathbb{Q} \subsetneq \mathbb{R}$ is mathematical abstraction; the computer works in a much smaller finite subset than $\mathbb{Q}$.

</Porta>

### Example 3: Blood glucose ranges while fasting (medicine)

| Bracket | Interval (mg/dL) | Diagnosis |
|---|---|---|
| Normal | $[0, 99]$ | healthy |
| Pre-diabetes | $[100, 125]$ | caution |
| Diabetes | $[126, +\infty)$ | treatment |

### Example 4: Domain of a cost function

Cost function $C(q) = 50 + 12q + 0.5q^2$ for manufacturing $q$ units.

Mathematical domain: $C: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$.
Physical (real) domain: $q \in \{0, 1, 2, 3, \ldots\} = \mathbb{N}$ (you don't manufacture half a car).

When the computation of the "optimal point" gives $q^* = 17.3$, it's your job as an engineer to decide whether to round to 17 or 18 — continuous mathematics produces a solution in ℝ; application demands a solution in ℕ. This tension **discrete vs. continuous** is one of five sources of epistemological difficulty in Calculus according to Rezende (UFF, 2003).

## Worked examples

<Exemplo titulo="Listing elements of a set (application)" numero="1">

**Problem:** List, in set-builder notation, the set $A = \{x \in \mathbb{Z} : -2 < x \leq 3\}$.

**Strategy:** The problem asks to list the elements of a set. The set notation gives us two pieces of information:
1. Which number set the elements belong to: $\mathbb{Z}$ (integers).
2. Which conditions those elements must satisfy: $-2 < x \leq 3$.
We'll combine these to identify all integers that fit.

**Solution:**

1. **Identify the type of number:** Elements $x$ must be integers ($\mathbb{Z}$).
2. **Analyze the first condition:** $x > -2$. This means $x$ must be greater than $-2$. The integers satisfying this are $-1, 0, 1, 2, 3, \ldots$.
3. **Analyze the second condition:** $x \leq 3$. This means $x$ must be less than or equal to $3$. The integers satisfying this are $\ldots, 1, 2, 3$.
4. **Combine the conditions:** We need integers $x$ that are *simultaneously* greater than $-2$ AND less than or equal to $3$.
    * Integers greater than $-2$: $\{-1, 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, \ldots\}$
    * Integers less than or equal to $3$: $\{\ldots, -1, 0, 1, 2, 3\}$
    * The intersection of these two sets is $\{-1, 0, 1, 2, 3\}$.

**Verification:** All listed numbers are integers.
- $-1$ is greater than $-2$ and less than or equal to $3$. (OK)
- $0$ is greater than $-2$ and less than or equal to $3$. (OK)
- $1$ is greater than $-2$ and less than or equal to $3$. (OK)
- $2$ is greater than $-2$ and less than or equal to $3$. (OK)
- $3$ is greater than $-2$ and less than or equal to $3$. (OK)
No other integer between $-2$ and $3$ (excluding $-2$, including $3$) was left out.


**Source.** [OpenStax — College Algebra 2e §1.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/1-1-real-numbers-algebra-essentials) — CC-BY 4.0.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Intersection of intervals (intermediate)" numero="2">

**Problem:** Given intervals $A = [-3, 7)$ and $B = (1, 10]$, compute $A \cap B$.

**Strategy:** The intersection of two sets includes only elements that belong to *both* sets. Again, visualization on the number line is the most effective tool.

**Solution:**

1. **Visualize interval $A$:** $A = [-3, 7)$ goes from -3 (included) to 7 (excluded).
    ```
    ←—————[----------)————→
         -3          7
    ```
2. **Visualize interval $B$:** $B = (1, 10]$ goes from 1 (excluded) to 10 (included).
    ```
    ←——————————(----------]————→
               1          10
    ```
3. **Combine for intersection:** We need to find the region where both intervals overlap.
    * The leftmost point where both exist: 1. But 1 is excluded in $B$, so it's excluded in the intersection.
    * The rightmost point where both exist: 7. But 7 is excluded in $A$, so it's excluded in the intersection.
    * The overlap region is between 1 (excluded) and 7 (excluded).

$$ A \cap B = (1, 7) $$

**Verification:**
- A point in the intersection: $x=3$. It's in $A$ (since $-3 \leq 3 < 7$) and in $B$ (since $1 < 3 \leq 10$). (OK)
- A point only in $A$: $x=-2$. It's not in $B$. (OK)
- A point only in $B$: $x=8$. It's not in $A$. (OK)
- The endpoints: 1 is not in $B$, 7 is not in $A$. Both excluded in the intersection.
The answer is consistent.


**Source.** [Stitz–Zeager — Precalculus §1.2](https://www.stitz-zeager.com/szprecalculus07042013.pdf) — CC-BY-NC-SA.

<Exemplo titulo="Absolute value inequality, type |u| ≤ c (intermediate)" numero="3">

**Problem:** Solve the inequality $|x - 4| \leq 3$ and express the solution in interval notation.

**Strategy:** Inequalities with absolute value of the form $|u| \leq c$ (where $c > 0$) can be rewritten as a compound inequality: $-c \leq u \leq c$. We'll apply this property and then solve for $x$.

**Solution:**

1. **Rewrite the inequality:** The absolute value property allows us to transform $|x - 4| \leq 3$ into:
    $$ -3 \leq x - 4 \leq 3 $$
2. **Isolate $x$:** To isolate $x$, add $4$ to all parts of the inequality:
    $$ -3 + 4 \leq x - 4 + 4 \leq 3 + 4 $$
    $$ 1 \leq x \leq 7 $$
3. **Express in interval notation:** The inequality $1 \leq x \leq 7$ means that $x$ can be any real number between 1 and 7, including 1 and 7.

$$ \text{Solution: } [1, 7] $$

**Verification:**
- Test a point inside the interval: $x=5$. $|5 - 4| = |1| = 1$. $1 \leq 3$. (OK)
- Test an endpoint: $x=1$. $|1 - 4| = |-3| = 3$. $3 \leq 3$. (OK)
- Test the other endpoint: $x=7$. $|7 - 4| = |3| = 3$. $3 \leq 3$. (OK)
- Test a point outside the interval: $x=0$. $|0 - 4| = |-4| = 4$. $4 \not\leq 3$. (OK)
- Test another point outside: $x=8$. $|8 - 4| = |4| = 4$. $4 \not\leq 3$. (OK)
The solution is correct.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Quadratic inequality (advanced)" numero="4">

**Problem:** Determine the solution set of the inequality $x^2 - x - 6 > 0$ in interval notation.

**Strategy:** To solve a quadratic inequality, first find the roots of the associated quadratic equation ($x^2 - x - 6 = 0$). These roots divide the real line into intervals. Then test a point in each interval to see where the inequality is satisfied.

**Solution:**

1. **Find the roots of the associated equation:** $x^2 - x - 6 = 0$.
    * We can factor the equation: $(x - 3)(x + 2) = 0$.
    * The roots are $x = 3$ and $x = -2$.
2. **Divide the real line into intervals:** The roots $-2$ and $3$ divide the real line into three intervals:
    * $(-\infty, -2)$
    * $(-2, 3)$
    * $(3, +\infty)$
3. **Test a point in each interval:**
    * **Interval $(-\infty, -2)$:** Choose $x = -3$.
        $(-3)^2 - (-3) - 6 = 9 + 3 - 6 = 6$. Since $6 > 0$, this interval *satisfies* the inequality.
    * **Interval $(-2, 3)$:** Choose $x = 0$.
        $(0)^2 - (0) - 6 = -6$. Since $-6 \not> 0$, this interval *does not satisfy* the inequality.
    * **Interval $(3, +\infty)$:** Choose $x = 4$.
        $(4)^2 - (4) - 6 = 16 - 4 - 6 = 6$. Since $6 > 0$, this interval *satisfies* the inequality.
4. **Combine the intervals that satisfy:** The solution is the union of the intervals where the inequality is true. Note that the inequality is strictly greater than 0, so the roots are not included.

$$ \text{Solution: } (-\infty, -2) \cup (3, +\infty) $$

**Verification:**
- The parabola $y = x^2 - x - 6$ has upward concavity (coefficient of $x^2$ is positive). This means the function values are positive "outside" the roots and negative "between" the roots. The solution found is consistent with the parabola's graph.


**Source.** [OpenStax — College Algebra 2e §3.6](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/3-6-other-types-of-equations) — CC-BY 4.0.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Modeling with point exclusion (real modeling)" numero="5">

**Problem:** A temperature sensor operates correctly in an environment where the temperature $T$ (in Celsius) must be between 10°C (inclusive) and 40°C (exclusive). Additionally, by a specific requirement, the temperature cannot be exactly 25°C. Express the valid operating range for $T$ as a union of intervals.

**Strategy:** First, define the base operating interval. Then remove the specific point that is not allowed. Removing a single point from an interval can result in two disjoint intervals.

**Solution:**

1. **Define the base interval:** The temperature must be between 10°C (inclusive) and 40°C (exclusive). This is represented by the interval $[10, 40)$.
2. **Identify the point to be excluded:** The temperature cannot be 25°C.
3. **Remove the point from the base interval:** The number 25 lies within the interval $[10, 40)$. By removing 25, the interval is divided into two parts:
    * All numbers from 10 (inclusive) to 25 (exclusive): $[10, 25)$.
    * All numbers from 25 (exclusive) to 40 (exclusive): $(25, 40)$.
4. **Express as a union of intervals:** The valid operating range is the union of these two disjoint intervals.

$$ \text{Operating range: } [10, 25) \cup (25, 40) $$

**Verification:**
- $T=10$: Valid. It's in the first interval.
- $T=20$: Valid. It's in the first interval.
- $T=25$: Not valid. It's not in either interval.
- $T=30$: Valid. It's in the second interval.
- $T=40$: Not valid. Excluded from the original interval.
The solution is correct and meets all conditions.

</Exemplo>
