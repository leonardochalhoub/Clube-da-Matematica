### Definition and domain

<Definicao titulo="Logarithmic function">

For $a > 0,\; a \neq 1$ and $x > 0$:

$$\log_a x = y \iff a^y = x$$

The **domain** of $f(x) = \log_a x$ is $(0, +\infty)$ and the **range** is $\mathbb{R}$.

Common special cases:

- $\ln x = \log_e x$ — **natural logarithm** (base $e \approx 2.71828$)
- $\log x = \log_{10} x$ — **common logarithm** (US convention; also used in most science texts)

</Definicao>

> "The logarithmic function of base $b$, $f(x) = \log_b(x)$, is the inverse of the exponential function $g(x) = b^x$. The domain is $(0, +\infty)$ and the range is $\mathbb{R}$." — [OpenStax College Algebra 2e §6.3](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/6-3-logarithmic-functions)

### Operational properties

<Definicao titulo="Laws of logarithms">

For $a > 0, a \neq 1$ and $x, y > 0$:

<Equation numero="P1" explicacao="Logarithm of a product is the sum of logarithms." latex="\log_a (xy) = \log_a x + \log_a y" />

<Equation numero="P2" explicacao="Logarithm of a quotient is the difference of logarithms." latex="\log_a \!\left(\frac{x}{y}\right) = \log_a x - \log_a y" />

<Equation numero="P3" explicacao="Logarithm of a power: the exponent comes out front." latex="\log_a (x^n) = n\, \log_a x" />

<Equation numero="P4" explicacao="Change of base: allows computing any log from ln or log₁₀." latex="\log_a x = \frac{\log_b x}{\log_b a}" />

Two fundamental identities (direct consequences of the definition):

<Equation numero="I1" explicacao="The logarithm undoes exponentiation: log_a of a^x is x." latex="\log_a (a^x) = x" />

<Equation numero="I2" explicacao="Exponentiation undoes the logarithm: a raised to log_a of x is x." latex="a^{\log_a x} = x" />

</Definicao>

> "The product rule for logarithms is derived directly from the exponential property $a^m \cdot a^n = a^{m+n}$." — [OpenStax College Algebra 2e §6.5](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/6-5-logarithmic-properties)

### Theorem and proof of the product property

<Teorema titulo="Product property (P1)">

For $a > 0, a \neq 1$ and $x, y > 0$: $\;\log_a(xy) = \log_a x + \log_a y$.

**Proof.** Let $u = \log_a x$ and $v = \log_a y$. By definition, $a^u = x$ and $a^v = y$. Thus $xy = a^u \cdot a^v = a^{u+v}$ (exponential law). Taking $\log_a$ of both sides: $\log_a(xy) = u + v = \log_a x + \log_a y$. $\square$

</Teorema>

<Insight>

**Why is $e$ the "natural" base?** The derivative of $\log_{10}(x)$ is $\dfrac{1}{x \ln 10} \approx \dfrac{1}{2.30\,x}$. The derivative of $\ln(x)$ is simply $\dfrac{1}{x}$, with no extra factor. Calculus "rewards" the base $e$ because it makes limits and derivatives come out clean with no multipliers — hence the name. You will prove this formally in Lesson 42.

</Insight>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

Imagine you have **a thousand coins**. Your mom asks: "how many zeros does that number have?"

You count: 1,000 — three zeros. The answer is **3**.

And a **million** coins? Six zeros. Answer **6**.

And **a billion**? Nine zeros. Answer **9**.

Logarithm base 10 is a little machine that **counts the zeros** in round numbers. You put in the number, it tells you how many zeros it has.

Why is this useful? Because in the real world there are **huge numbers** — the distance to a star, the number of cells in your body, a country's debt — and the logarithm transforms those numbers into something of human scale.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

You already know that $10^3 = 1{,}000$. The logarithm base 10 of 1,000 is 3. That simple.

The question the logarithm answers is: **"to what power do I raise the base to get this number?"**

- $\log_{10}(100) = 2$, because $10^2 = 100$.
- $\log_{10}(1{,}000) = 3$, because $10^3 = 1{,}000$.
- $\log_2(8) = 3$, because $2^3 = 8$.
- $\log_2(1{,}024) = 10$, because $2^{10} = 1{,}024$.

**Why was it invented?** Before calculators, multiplying large numbers was a nightmare. With logarithm tables, multiplication became addition — much faster to do by hand. For 300 years, astronomers and engineers used this every day.

**Where does it appear today?**

- **pH** in Chemistry: measures acidity. Each pH point is 10 times more acidic.
- **Decibel** in Physics: measures sound intensity. Each 10 dB is 10 times more energy.
- **Richter scale** in Geology: magnitude 5 versus magnitude 7 = 100 times more amplitude.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old teen">

Logarithm answers the question: **"to what exponent do I raise the base to get the argument?"**

- $\log_2 8 = 3$ because $2^3 = 8$.
- $\log_{10} 1000 = 3$ because $10^3 = 1000$.
- $\log_{10} 1 = 0$ because $10^0 = 1$.
- $\log_a a = 1$ for any base (because $a^1 = a$).

The two most commonly used logarithms:

- **$\ln$** (natural logarithm, base $e \approx 2.718$): appears in calculus, differential equations, radioactive decay, finance.
- **$\log$** (base 10): appears in scientific scales — pH, decibel, Richter, stellar magnitude.

**Properties that save time:**

$$\log_a(xy) = \log_a x + \log_a y \qquad \log_a\!\left(\frac{x}{y}\right) = \log_a x - \log_a y \qquad \log_a(x^n) = n \log_a x$$

**Logarithmic equations** are solved using the inverse definition: $\log_a x = c \iff x = a^c$. But pay attention: always check the domain — every solution must make the arguments of the logs **positive**.

<Insight>

**Why does $e$ appear in calculus, and not 10?** The derivative of $\log_{10}(x)$ has that annoying factor $1/\ln 10 \approx 0.43$ in the way. The derivative of $\ln(x)$ is simply $1/x$. The base $e$ is the only one that makes this happen cleanly.

</Insight>

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

Log and exponential are inverse functions: $\ln(e^x) = x$ and $e^{\ln x} = x$ for every $x > 0$. In calculus:

$$\frac{d}{dx} \ln x = \frac{1}{x}, \qquad \int \frac{dx}{x} = \ln |x| + C, \qquad \frac{d}{dx} a^x = a^x \ln a$$

### Half-life and exponential decay

If $N(t) = N_0\, e^{-\lambda t}$, the half-life $\tau_{1/2}$ satisfies $N(\tau_{1/2}) = N_0/2$:

$$e^{-\lambda \tau_{1/2}} = \tfrac{1}{2} \;\Longrightarrow\; \tau_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{\lambda}$$

### Logarithmic scales in engineering

| Scale | Formula | Reference |
|---|---|---|
| Richter Magnitude | $M = \log_{10}(A/A_0)$ | reference amplitude $A_0$ |
| Acoustic Decibel | $L = 10\log_{10}(I/I_0)$ | $I_0 = 10^{-12}$ W/m² (hearing threshold) |
| pH | $\text{pH} = -\log_{10}[H^+]$ | molar concentration of $H^+$ |
| Bode Gain | $G = 20\log_{10}\|H(j\omega)\|$ | voltage or current ratio |

The log scale "compresses" multiple orders of magnitude into one navigable axis. An earthquake of magnitude 9 releases $10^9$ times more energy than one of magnitude 1.5.

### Change of base in practice

A scientific calculator has only $\ln$ and $\log_{10}$. For any other base:

$$\log_a x = \frac{\ln x}{\ln a} = \frac{\log x}{\log a}$$

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

The logarithm is the operator that **linearizes multiplicative processes**. Whenever you face growth in chains — compound rates, iterated multiplication, products of probabilities — you move to the logarithmic space.

### Log-likelihood in statistical inference

You never maximize the likelihood $L(\theta) = \prod_i f(x_i;\theta)$ directly — a product of many factors less than 1 underflows numerically. Always use the log-likelihood:

$$\ell(\theta) = \log L(\theta) = \sum_i \log f(x_i;\theta)$$

The sum is stable; the product is not. All frequentist parametric inference (MLE, LRT) and Bayesian (MAP) operates in this space.

### Cross-entropy and neural networks

The classification loss $-\sum y_i \log \hat{p}_i$ is log in action: without it, gradients of $\prod \hat{p}_i$ would vanish for deep networks (vanishing gradient problem).

### Benford's Law

In collections of natural data (prices, populations, physical constants), the probability the first digit is $d$ is $\log_{10}(1 + 1/d)$. The digit 1 appears as first digit ~30% of the time; 9 appears ~4.6%. Used in audit fraud detection — fabricated numbers tend to have uniform distribution of first digit.

### Discrete logarithm and cryptography

In finite field $\mathbb{F}_p$, given $g$ and $h$, finding $x$ such that $g^x \equiv h \pmod{p}$ is the **discrete logarithm problem** (DLP). Computationally hard — basis of the Diffie-Hellman protocol (1976), DSA, ElGamal and, on elliptic curves (ECDLP), of SSL/TLS and blockchain.

### Weber-Fechner Law and human perception

The perceived intensity of a stimulus is logarithmic in physical intensity. That is why pH, decibel, Richter and stellar magnitude are logarithmic scales — they mirror our sensory perception. A whisper at 30 dB and a concert at 110 dB differ by 80 dB, a factor of $10^8$ in energy, yet both are "audible" to a healthy human ear.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practice — scenarios with real data">

### Scenario 1: pH of common solutions

$\text{pH} = -\log_{10}[H^+]$. Comparison of acidity:

| Solution | $[H^+]$ (mol/L) | pH |
|---|---|---|
| Stomach acid | $10^{-2}$ | 2 |
| Vinegar | $\approx 10^{-3}$ | 3 |
| Soft drink (Coca-Cola) | $\approx 10^{-3}$ | 3 |
| Milk | $\approx 10^{-6.5}$ | 6.5 |
| Distilled water | $10^{-7}$ | 7 |
| Human blood | $\approx 10^{-7.4}$ | 7.4 |

Stomach acid is $10^{7.4 - 2} \approx 250{,}000$ times more acidic than blood. That is why the stomach has a mucous lining.

### Scenario 2: Capacitor discharging (electrical engineering)

Voltage: $V(t) = V_0\, e^{-t/\tau}$. When does it reach 10% of initial voltage?

$$0.10 = e^{-t/\tau} \;\Longrightarrow\; t = \tau \ln(10) \approx 2.303\, \tau$$

**Rule of thumb:** a capacitor discharges 90% in **2.3 time constants** ($\ln 10$) and 99% in **4.6 time constants** ($\ln 100$). A circuit engineer knows this 2.3 from memory.

### Scenario 3: Rule of 70 — time to double capital

Continuous compounding: $S(t) = S_0\, e^{rt}$. When does it double?

$$t_\text{dupl} = \frac{\ln 2}{r} \approx \frac{0.693}{r}$$

**Rule of 70:** $t_\text{dupl} \approx 70/r\%$. For a 7% annual rate, it doubles in ~10 years. For 14% annually, it doubles in ~5 years. A 1% difference in fund management fees over 30 years of accumulation can cost US$20,000–40,000.

</Porta>

## Worked examples

<Exemplo titulo="Calculate logarithm directly (application)" numero="1">

**Problem:** Calculate $\log_2 32$.

**Strategy:** Write the argument as a power of the base; the exponent is the answer.

**Solution:**

1. Factor the argument: $32 = 2^5$.
2. Apply the identity $\log_a(a^p) = p$: $\log_2 32 = \log_2(2^5) = 5$.

**Check:** $2^5 = 32$. ✓

**Source.** [OpenStax — College Algebra 2e §6.3](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/6-3-logarithmic-functions) — CC-BY 4.0 license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Use operational properties (intermediate)" numero="2">

**Problem:** Simplify $\log_2 8 + \log_2 4 - \log_2 16$.

**Strategy:** Use P1 and P2 to reduce everything to a single logarithm.

**Solution:**

$$\log_2 8 + \log_2 4 - \log_2 16 = \log_2 \frac{8 \cdot 4}{16} = \log_2 \frac{32}{16} = \log_2 2 = 1$$

**Alternative** (compute separately): $3 + 2 - 4 = 1$. ✓

**Check:** $2^1 = 2$, and the expression reduced to $\log_2 2 = 1$. ✓

**Source.** [OpenStax — Algebra and Trigonometry 2e §6.5](https://openstax.org/books/algebra-and-trigonometry-2e/pages/6-5-logarithmic-properties) — CC-BY 4.0 license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Solve a logarithmic equation (intermediate)" numero="3">

**Problem:** Solve $\log_2 x + \log_2(x - 2) = 3$.

**Strategy:** Combine via P1, apply the definition, and check the domain.

**Solution:**

1. **Domain:** we need $x > 0$ and $x - 2 > 0$, i.e., $x > 2$.
2. **Combine:** $\log_2[x(x-2)] = 3$.
3. **Definition:** $x(x-2) = 2^3 = 8$, i.e., $x^2 - 2x - 8 = 0$.
4. **Factor:** $(x-4)(x+2) = 0 \Rightarrow x = 4$ or $x = -2$.
5. **Filter:** $x = -2 < 2$ → discard. Remains $x = 4$.

**Check:** $\log_2 4 + \log_2 2 = 2 + 1 = 3$. ✓

**Source.** [OpenStax — College Algebra 2e §6.6](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/6-6-exponential-and-logarithmic-equations) — CC-BY 4.0 license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Change of base with calculator (advanced)" numero="4">

**Problem:** Calculate $\log_3 17$ using only $\ln$ and $\log_{10}$.

**Strategy:** Change of base formula $\log_a x = \log_b x / \log_b a$.

**Solution:**

1. **Via ln:** $\log_3 17 = \dfrac{\ln 17}{\ln 3} \approx \dfrac{2.8332}{1.0986} \approx 2.5789$.
2. **Sanity check via $\log_{10}$:** $\dfrac{\log 17}{\log 3} \approx \dfrac{1.2304}{0.4771} \approx 2.5789$. ✓

**Check:** $3^{2.5789} \approx 17$ (calculator confirms). ✓

**Source.** [Stitz–Zeager Precalculus §6.3](https://www.stitz-zeager.com/szprecalculus07042013.pdf) — CC-BY-NC-SA license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="pH as a logarithmic model (real modeling)" numero="5">

**Problem:** In a solution, $[H^+] = 4 \times 10^{-5}$ mol/L. Calculate the pH. (Given: $\log_{10} 4 \approx 0.6$.)

**Strategy:** Apply $\text{pH} = -\log_{10}[H^+]$ and use P1 to separate the factors.

**Solution:**

1. **Definition:** $\text{pH} = -\log_{10}(4 \times 10^{-5})$.
2. **P1:** $\log_{10}(4 \times 10^{-5}) = \log_{10} 4 + \log_{10} 10^{-5} = 0.6 + (-5) = -4.4$.
3. **Sign:** $\text{pH} = -(-4.4) = 4.4$.

**Interpretation:** pH 4.4 — acidic solution, similar to diluted orange juice.

**Check:** $10^{-4.4} = 10^{-5} \cdot 10^{0.6} \approx 10^{-5} \cdot 3.98 \approx 4 \times 10^{-5}$. ✓

**Source.** [OpenStax — Algebra and Trigonometry 2e §6.7](https://openstax.org/books/algebra-and-trigonometry-2e/pages/6-7-exponential-and-logarithmic-models) — CC-BY 4.0 license.
</Ejemplo>
