<Porta nivel="formal" titulo="Definition and properties">

### Definition and domain

<Definicao titulo="Logarithmic function">

For $a > 0,\; a \neq 1$ and $x > 0$:

$$\log_a x = y \iff a^y = x$$

The **domain** of $f(x) = \log_a x$ is $(0, +\infty)$ and the **range** is $\mathbb{R}$.

Special cases of frequent use:

- $\ln x = \log_e x$ — **natural** logarithm (base $e \approx 2{,}71828$)
- $\log x = \log_{10} x$ — **common** logarithm (convention used in Brazil, Portugal, and most science textbooks)

</Definicao>

> "The logarithmic function with base $b$, $f(x) = \log_b(x)$, is the inverse of the exponential function $g(x) = b^x$. The domain is $(0, +\infty)$ and the range is $\mathbb{R}$." — [OpenStax College Algebra 2e §6.3](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/6-3-logarithmic-functions)

### Operational properties

<Definicao titulo="Laws of logarithms">

For $a > 0, a \neq 1$ and $x, y > 0$:

<Equation numero="P1" explicacao="Logarithm of a product equals the sum of the logarithms." latex="\log_a (xy) = \log_a x + \log_a y" />

<Equation numero="P2" explicacao="Logarithm of a quotient equals the difference of the logarithms." latex="\log_a \!\left(\frac{x}{y}\right) = \log_a x - \log_a y" />

<Equation numero="P3" explicacao="Logarithm of a power: the exponent moves in front." latex="\log_a (x^n) = n\, \log_a x" />

<Equation numero="P4" explicacao="Change of base: allows computing any log using ln or log base 10." latex="\log_a x = \frac{\log_b x}{\log_b a}" />

Two fundamental identities (direct consequences of the definition):

<Equation numero="I1" explicacao="The logarithm undoes exponentiation: log_a of a^x equals x." latex="\log_a (a^x) = x" />

<Equation numero="I2" explicacao="Exponentiation undoes the logarithm: a raised to log_a of x equals x." latex="a^{\log_a x} = x" />

</Definicao>

> "The product rule for logarithms is derived directly from the property $a^m \cdot a^n = a^{m+n}$." — [OpenStax College Algebra 2e §6.5](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/6-5-logarithmic-properties)

### Theorem and proof of the product property

<Teorema titulo="Product property (P1)">

For $a > 0, a \neq 1$ and $x, y > 0$: $\;\log_a(xy) = \log_a x + \log_a y$.

**Proof.** Let $u = \log_a x$ and $v = \log_a y$. By definition, $a^u = x$ and $a^v = y$. Therefore $xy = a^u \cdot a^v = a^{u+v}$ (law of exponents). Applying $\log_a$ to both sides: $\log_a(xy) = u + v = \log_a x + \log_a y$. $\square$

</Teorema>

<Insight>

**Why is $e$ the "natural base"?** The derivative of $\log_{10}(x)$ is $\dfrac{1}{x \ln 10} \approx \dfrac{1}{2{,}30\,x}$. The derivative of $\ln(x)$ is simply $\dfrac{1}{x}$, with no extra factor. Calculus "rewards" base $e$ because it makes limits and derivatives come out without multipliers — hence the name. You will prove this formally in Lesson 42.

</Insight>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

Imagine you have **one thousand coins**. Your mom asks: "how many zeros does that number have?"

You count: 1,000 — three zeros. The answer is **3**.

And **one million** coins? Six zeros. Answer: **6**.

And **one billion**? Nine zeros. Answer: **9**.

The base-10 logarithm is a little machine that **counts the zeros** of round numbers. You put a number in, and it tells you how many zeros it has.

Why is this useful? Because in the real world there are **enormous** numbers — the distance to a star, the number of cells in the body, a country's debt — and the logarithm turns those numbers into something human-sized and easy to talk about.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

You already know that $10^3 = 1{,}000$. The base-10 logarithm of 1,000 is 3. Simple as that.

The question the logarithm answers is: **"to what power do I raise the base to get that number?"**

- $\log_{10}(100) = 2$, because $10^2 = 100$.
- $\log_{10}(1{,}000) = 3$, because $10^3 = 1{,}000$.
- $\log_2(8) = 3$, because $2^3 = 8$.
- $\log_2(1{,}024) = 10$, because $2^{10} = 1{,}024$.

**Why was it invented?** Before calculators, multiplying large numbers was a nightmare. With logarithm tables, multiplication became addition — much faster to do on paper. For 300 years, astronomers and engineers used this every single day.

**Where does it appear today?**

- **pH** in Chemistry: measures acidity. Each pH point is 10 times more acidic.
- **Decibel** in Physics: measures sound intensity. Every 10 dB is 10 times more energy.
- **Richter scale** in Geology: magnitude 5 vs magnitude 7 = 100 times greater amplitude.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old">

Logarithm answers the question: **"to what exponent must I raise the base to obtain the argument?"**

- $\log_2 8 = 3$ because $2^3 = 8$.
- $\log_{10} 1000 = 3$ because $10^3 = 1000$.
- $\log_{10} 1 = 0$ because $10^0 = 1$.
- $\log_a a = 1$ for any base (because $a^1 = a$).

The two most commonly used logarithms:

- **$\ln$** (natural logarithm, base $e \approx 2{,}718$): appears in calculus, differential equations, radioactive decay, finance.
- **$\log$** (base 10): appears in scientific scales — pH, decibel, Richter, stellar magnitude.

**Properties that save time:**

$$\log_a(xy) = \log_a x + \log_a y \qquad \log_a\!\left(\frac{x}{y}\right) = \log_a x - \log_a y \qquad \log_a(x^n) = n \log_a x$$

**Logarithmic equations** are solved using the inverse definition: $\log_a x = c \iff x = a^c$. Important: always check the domain — every solution must make the arguments of the logs **positive**.

<Insight>

**Why does $e$ appear in calculus, and not 10?** The derivative of $\log_{10}(x)$ has that factor $1/\ln 10 \approx 0{,}43$ getting in the way. The derivative of $\ln(x)$ is simply $1/x$. The base $e$ is the only one that makes this happen cleanly.

</Insight>

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

Log and exponential are inverse functions: $\ln(e^x) = x$ and $e^{\ln x} = x$ for all $x > 0$. In calculus:

$$\frac{d}{dx} \ln x = \frac{1}{x}, \qquad \int \frac{dx}{x} = \ln |x| + C, \qquad \frac{d}{dx} a^x = a^x \ln a$$

### Half-life and exponential decay

If $N(t) = N_0\, e^{-\lambda t}$, the half-life $\tau_{1/2}$ satisfies $N(\tau_{1/2}) = N_0/2$:

$$e^{-\lambda \tau_{1/2}} = \tfrac{1}{2} \;\Longrightarrow\; \tau_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{\lambda}$$

### Logarithmic scales in engineering

| Scale | Formula | Reference |
|---|---|---|
| Richter magnitude | $M = \log_{10}(A/A_0)$ | reference amplitude $A_0$ |
| Acoustic decibel | $L = 10\log_{10}(I/I_0)$ | $I_0 = 10^{-12}$ W/m² (hearing threshold) |
| pH | $\text{pH} = -\log_{10}[H^+]$ | molar concentration of $H^+$ |
| Bode gain | $G = 20\log_{10}|H(j\omega)|$ | voltage or current ratio |

The log scale "compresses" many orders of magnitude into a navigable axis. An earthquake of magnitude 9 releases $10^9$ times more energy than one of magnitude 1.5.

### Change of base in practice

Scientific calculators only have $\ln$ and $\log_{10}$. For any other base:

$$\log_a x = \frac{\ln x}{\ln a} = \frac{\log x}{\log a}$$

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

The logarithm is the operator that **linearizes multiplicative processes**. Whenever one faces chain growth — compound rates, iterated multiplication processes, products of probabilities — the approach is to move into logarithmic space.

### Log-likelihood in statistical inference

One never maximizes the likelihood $L(\theta) = \prod_i f(x_i;\theta)$ directly — a product of many factors less than 1 underflows numerically. One always uses the log-likelihood:

$$\ell(\theta) = \log L(\theta) = \sum_i \log f(x_i;\theta)$$

The sum is numerically stable; the product is not. All frequentist parametric inference (MLE, LRT) and Bayesian inference (MAP) operates in this space.

### Cross-entropy and neural networks

The classification loss $-\sum y_i \log \hat{p}_i$ is the logarithm in action: without it, the gradients of $\prod \hat{p}_i$ would vanish for deep networks (the vanishing gradient problem).

### Benford's Law

In collections of natural data (prices, populations, physical constants), the probability that the leading digit is $d$ is $\log_{10}(1 + 1/d)$. The digit 1 appears as the leading digit roughly 30% of the time; the digit 9, only about 4.6%. Used in tax auditing to detect fraud — fabricated numbers tend to have a uniform distribution of leading digits.

### Discrete logarithm and cryptography

In a finite field $\mathbb{F}_p$, given $g$ and $h$, finding $x$ such that $g^x \equiv h \pmod{p}$ is the **discrete logarithm problem** (DLP). Computationally hard — the basis of the Diffie-Hellman protocol (1976), DSA, ElGamal, and, on elliptic curves (ECDLP), of SSL/TLS and blockchain.

### Weber-Fechner law and human perception

The perceived intensity of a stimulus is logarithmic in the physical intensity. That is why pH, decibel, Richter scale, and stellar magnitude are logarithmic scales — they mirror our sensory perception. A whisper at 30 dB and a concert at 110 dB differ by 80 dB = a factor of $10^8$ in energy, yet both are "audible" to a healthy human ear.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practice — scenarios with real data">

### Scenario 1: pH of common solutions

$\text{pH} = -\log_{10}[H^+]$. Acidity comparison:

| Solution | $[H^+]$ (mol/L) | pH |
|---|---|---|
| Stomach acid | $10^{-2}$ | 2 |
| Vinegar | $\approx 10^{-3}$ | 3 |
| Soft drink (Coca-Cola) | $\approx 10^{-3}$ | 3 |
| Milk | $\approx 10^{-6{,}5}$ | 6.5 |
| Distilled water | $10^{-7}$ | 7 |
| Human blood | $\approx 10^{-7{,}4}$ | 7.4 |

Stomach acid is $10^{7{,}4 - 2} \approx 250{,}000$ times more acidic than blood. Hence the mucus lining of the stomach.

### Scenario 2: Discharging capacitor (electrical engineering)

Voltage: $V(t) = V_0\, e^{-t/\tau}$. When does it reach 10% of the initial voltage?

$$0{,}10 = e^{-t/\tau} \;\Longrightarrow\; t = \tau \ln(10) \approx 2{,}303\, \tau$$

**Practical rule:** a capacitor discharges 90% in **2.3 time constants** ($\ln 10$) and 99% in **4.6 time constants** ($\ln 100$). Electrical engineers know this 2.3 by heart.

### Scenario 3: Rule of 70 — time to double capital

Continuous compounding: $S(t) = S_0\, e^{rt}$. When does it double?

$$t_\text{double} = \frac{\ln 2}{r} \approx \frac{0{,}693}{r}$$

**Rule of 70:** $t_\text{double} \approx 70/r\%$. At a 7% annual rate, capital doubles in about 10 years. At 14% per year, it doubles in about 5 years. A 1% difference in management fees on a pension fund can cost 20,000–40,000 currency units over 30 years of accumulation.

</Porta>

## Worked examples

<Exemplo titulo="Computing a logarithm directly (application)" numero="1">

**Problem:** Compute $\log_2 32$.

**Strategy:** Write the argument as a power of the base; the exponent is the answer.

**Solution:**

1. Factor the argument: $32 = 2^5$.
2. Apply the identity $\log_a(a^p) = p$: $\log_2 32 = \log_2(2^5) = 5$.

**Verification:** $2^5 = 32$. ✓

**Source.** [OpenStax — College Algebra 2e §6.3](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/6-3-logarithmic-functions) — CC-BY 4.0 license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Using operational properties (intermediate)" numero="2">

**Problem:** Simplify $\log_2 8 + \log_2 4 - \log_2 16$.

**Strategy:** Use P1 and P2 to reduce everything to a single logarithm.

**Solution:**

$$\log_2 8 + \log_2 4 - \log_2 16 = \log_2 \frac{8 \cdot 4}{16} = \log_2 \frac{32}{16} = \log_2 2 = 1$$

**Alternative** (compute separately): $3 + 2 - 4 = 1$. ✓

**Verification:** $2^1 = 2$, and the expression reduced to $\log_2 2 = 1$. ✓

**Source.** [OpenStax — Algebra and Trigonometry 2e §6.5](https://openstax.org/books/algebra-and-trigonometry-2e/pages/6-5-logarithmic-properties) — CC-BY 4.0 license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Solving a logarithmic equation (intermediate)" numero="3">

**Problem:** Solve $\log_2 x + \log_2(x - 2) = 3$.

**Strategy:** Combine using P1, apply the definition, and check the domain.

**Solution:**

1. **Domain:** we need $x > 0$ and $x - 2 > 0$, that is $x > 2$.
2. **Combine:** $\log_2[x(x-2)] = 3$.
3. **Definition:** $x(x-2) = 2^3 = 8$, that is $x^2 - 2x - 8 = 0$.
4. **Factor:** $(x-4)(x+2) = 0 \Rightarrow x = 4$ or $x = -2$.
5. **Filter:** $x = -2 < 2$ → discard. The solution is $x = 4$.

**Verification:** $\log_2 4 + \log_2 2 = 2 + 1 = 3$. ✓

**Source.** [OpenStax — College Algebra 2e §6.6](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/6-6-exponential-and-logarithmic-equations) — CC-BY 4.0 license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Change of base with a calculator (advanced)" numero="4">

**Problem:** Compute $\log_3 17$ using only $\ln$ and $\log_{10}$.

**Strategy:** Change of base formula $\log_a x = \log_b x / \log_b a$.

**Solution:**

1. **Via ln:** $\log_3 17 = \dfrac{\ln 17}{\ln 3} \approx \dfrac{2{,}8332}{1{,}0986} \approx 2{,}5789$.
2. **Sanity check via $\log_{10}$:** $\dfrac{\log 17}{\log 3} \approx \dfrac{1{,}2304}{0{,}4771} \approx 2{,}5789$. ✓

**Verification:** $3^{2{,}5789} \approx 17$ (calculator confirms). ✓

**Source.** [Stitz–Zeager Precalculus §6.3](https://www.stitz-zeager.com/szprecalculus07042013.pdf) — CC-BY-NC-SA license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="pH as a logarithmic model (real-world modeling)" numero="5">

**Problem:** In a solution, $[H^+] = 4 \times 10^{-5}$ mol/L. Compute the pH. (Given: $\log_{10} 4 \approx 0{,}6$.)

**Strategy:** Apply $\text{pH} = -\log_{10}[H^+]$ and use P1 to separate the factors.

**Solution:**

1. **Definition:** $\text{pH} = -\log_{10}(4 \times 10^{-5})$.
2. **P1:** $\log_{10}(4 \times 10^{-5}) = \log_{10} 4 + \log_{10} 10^{-5} = 0{,}6 + (-5) = -4{,}4$.
3. **Sign:** $\text{pH} = -(-4{,}4) = 4{,}4$.

**Interpretation:** pH 4.4 — acidic solution, similar to diluted orange juice.

**Verification:** $10^{-4{,}4} = 10^{-5} \cdot 10^{0{,}6} \approx 10^{-5} \cdot 3{,}98 \approx 4 \times 10^{-5}$. ✓

**Source.** [OpenStax — Algebra and Trigonometry 2e §6.7](https://openstax.org/books/algebra-and-trigonometry-2e/pages/6-7-exponential-and-logarithmic-models) — CC-BY 4.0 license.
</Exemplo>
