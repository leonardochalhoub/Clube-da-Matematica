<Porta nivel="formal" titulo="Definition and properties">

<Definicao titulo="Logarithm">
For $a > 0, a \neq 1$ and $x > 0$:
$$\log_a x = y \iff a^y = x$$

- $\ln x = \log_e x$ (natural logarithm)
- $\log x = \log_{10} x$ (common logarithm — US and most countries)
</Definicao>

> "The logarithmic function with base $b$, $f(x) = \log_b(x)$, is the inverse of the exponential function $g(x) = b^x$. The domain is $(0, +\infty)$ and the range is $\mathbb{R}$." — [OpenStax Algebra and Trigonometry 2e §6.3](https://openstax.org/books/algebra-and-trigonometry-2e/pages/6-3-logarithmic-functions)

### Operational properties

<Equation numero="P1" explicacao="Log of product = sum of logs.">
{`\\log_a (xy) = \\log_a x + \\log_a y`}
</Equation>

<Equation numero="P2" explicacao="Log of quotient = difference of logs.">
{`\\log_a (x/y) = \\log_a x - \\log_a y`}
</Equation>

<Equation numero="P3" explicacao="Log of power = exponent times log.">
{`\\log_a (x^p) = p \\cdot \\log_a x`}
</Equation>

<Equation numero="P4" explicacao="Change of base.">
{`\\log_a x = \\frac{\\log_b x}{\\log_b a}`}
</Equation>

> "The product rule, quotient rule, and power rule allow you to rewrite logarithmic expressions, transforming products into sums, quoti into differences, and powers into products." — [OpenStax Algebra and Trigonometry 2e §6.5](https://openstax.org/books/algebra-and-trigonometry-2e/pages/6-5-logarithmic-properties)

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

You have a big number, like **one thousand**. You want to know: "**how many zeros does this number have?**" The answer is 3, because **one thousand = 1,000** (a one followed by 3 zeros).

What about **one million**? It has 6 zeros.

And **one billion**? It has 9 zeros.

Logarithm (in base 10) is a machine that does exactly this: **counts the zeros**. Put in 1,000, it answers 3. Put in a million, it answers 6. Put in a billion, it answers 9.

Why is that useful? Because there are **huge numbers** in the world (stars, distances, country-wide money), and logarithm shrinks them to human-speech size.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

You know that $10^3 = 1{,}000$. So the logarithm of 1,000 in base 10 is 3. Done.

The question that logarithm answers is: **"to what power do I raise the base to get this number?"**

- $\log_{10}(100) = 2$, because $10^2 = 100$.
- $\log_{10}(1{,}000) = 3$.
- $\log_2(8) = 3$, because $2^3 = 8$.
- $\log_2(1{,}024) = 10$, because $2^{10} = 1{,}024$.

**Why does it exist?** Because it transforms multiplication into addition. Before calculators, multiplying large numbers was a nightmare — you'd use a "table of logarithms" to swap the multiplication for easy addition on paper. For 300 years, that's how astronomers and engineers calculated.

**Where does it show up today?**

- **pH** (chemistry): measures acidity. Soda has pH 3, pure water has pH 7. Each pH point is 10× more acidic — logarithmic scale.
- **Decibel** (sound): measures intensity. Conversation = 60 dB, rock concert = 110 dB. Each 10 dB is 10× more sound energy.
- **Richter magnitude** (earthquakes): magnitude 5 vs magnitude 7 = **100× more** energy released.
- **Stars** (astronomy): stellar magnitude is logarithmic.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old">

Logarithm answers the question: **"to what power do I raise the base to get the number?"**

- $\log_2 8 = 3$ because $2^3 = 8$.
- $\log_{10} 1000 = 3$ because $10^3 = 1000$.
- $\log_{10} 1 = 0$ because $10^0 = 1$.

The two most used:
- **$\ln$** (natural logarithm, base $e$): appears in calculus, ODEs, decay.
- **$\log$** (base 10): appears in physical scales (Richter, decibel, pH).

**Why does it exist?** Because it transforms multiplication into addition. Before calculators, multiplying $234 \times 567$ was work; applying log and adding was easier. (Log tables — 17th to 20th centuries — were essential.)

**Modern applications:**
- **pH** = $-\log_{10}[H^+]$ — pH 7 = neutral.
- **Decibel** (dB) — logarithmic scale of sound intensity.
- **Earthquake magnitude** (Richter) — each point = 10× more energy.

**Why does $e$ appear in calculus, not 10?** Because the derivative of $\log_{10}(x)$ is $\frac{1}{x \ln 10}$ — that "factor 2.3..." is awkward. The derivative of $\ln(x)$ is simply $\frac{1}{x}$. Calculus "rewards" base $e$ — hence the name "natural logarithm."

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

Log and exponential are duals: $\log \exp = \exp \log =$ identity. In calculus:

- $\frac{d}{dx} \ln x = \frac{1}{x}$
- $\int \frac{1}{x} dx = \ln |x| + C$
- $\frac{d}{dx} a^x = a^x \ln a$

### Exponential decay and half-life

If $N(t) = N_0 e^{-\lambda t}$, the **half-life** $\tau_{1/2}$ satisfies $N(\tau_{1/2}) = N_0/2$:
$$e^{-\lambda \tau_{1/2}} = 1/2 \Rightarrow \tau_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{\lambda}$$

### Log scales in engineering

- **Bode**: gains in dB, frequencies in log
- **Richter**: $M = \log_{10}(A/A_0)$
- **pH**: $-\log_{10}[H^+]$
- **Acoustic decibel**: $L = 10 \log_{10}(I/I_0)$ — threshold 0 dB ≈ $10^{-12}$ W/m²

The log scale "compresses" several orders of magnitude onto a navigable axis. A magnitude-7 earthquake releases **1,000,000,000 ($10^9$)** times more energy than a magnitude-1 tremor.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

In practice, **logarithm is the operator that makes multiplication additive**. Whenever you face repeated multiplication — compound rates, multiplicative chain processes, exponential distributions — you switch to log.

### Log in data science

- **Log-likelihood**: $\ell(\theta) = \log L(\theta) = \sum_i \log f(x_i; \theta)$. Always optimize log-likelihood, not likelihood — because the sum is numerically stable, the product is not.
- **Cross-entropy loss** in classification: $-\sum y_i \log \hat{p}_i$. Without log, gradients vanish.
- **Log-transform** in regression: a variable with a long tail (income, real estate price) becomes approximately normal.

### Log in information and entropy

Shannon entropy: $H = -\sum p_i \log_2 p_i$. Measures information in **bits**. Surprise of an event with probability $p$ is $\log_2(1/p)$ — the less probable, the more surprise. This intuition (Shannon 1948) founded information theory.

### Log in human perception

**Weber-Fechner law**: the perceived intensity of a stimulus is logarithmic in physical intensity. That's why decibel, pH, Richter, stellar magnitude are all logarithmic — they **mirror the human perceptual scale**.

### Caution: log of zero and negatives

$\log 0 = -\infty$ and $\log x$ is undefined for $x < 0$ (in $\mathbb{R}$). In code, always add $\epsilon$: `log(x + 1e-10)`. In statistics, soft-log: $\log(1 + x)$ is defined for all $x > -1$ — used in finance (log-return).

### Log-likelihood ratio in hypothesis testing

LRT: $\Lambda = -2 \log(L_0/L_1)$. Under the null hypothesis, $\Lambda \sim \chi^2$. **Wilks 1938** — foundation of likelihood ratio testing. All frequentist parametric inference uses this.

### Discrete logarithm and cryptography

In finite field $\mathbb{F}_p$, given $g, h$ find $x$ such that $g^x = h \pmod{p}$ is the **discrete logarithm problem**. Computationally hard — basis of **Diffie-Hellman**, **DSA**, and **ElGamal**. Elliptic curves ($\text{ECDLP}$) are the modern version used in SSL/TLS, blockchain, etc.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practice — 3 real scenarios">

### Scenario 1: pH and stomach acid

Gastric juice has $[H^+] \approx 10^{-2}$ mol/L. So $\text{pH} = -\log_{10}(10^{-2}) = 2$.

Compare with:
- Distilled water: $[H^+] = 10^{-7}$ → pH 7
- Soda (Coca-Cola): $[H^+] \approx 10^{-3}$ → pH 3
- Human blood: $[H^+] \approx 10^{-7.4}$ → pH 7.4

Ratio of acidity blood vs stomach acid: $10^{7.4 - 2} \approx 250{,}000$. **Stomach acid is 250,000 times more acidic than blood.** That's why the stomach has a mucus lining — without it, the acid would digest the stomach wall itself.

### Scenario 2: Capacitor discharging to 10% (engineering)

A capacitor discharges as $V(t) = V_0 e^{-t/\tau}$. When does it reach 10% of initial voltage?

$$0.10 V_0 = V_0 e^{-t/\tau} \Rightarrow t = \tau \ln(10) \approx 2.303 \tau$$

**Engineering rule of thumb**: capacitor discharges 90% in **2.3 time constants**, and 99% in **4.6 time constants** ($\ln 100$). This 2.3 — coming from $\ln 10$ — is so common in circuits that it becomes mental math.

### Scenario 3: Time for investment to double (finance)

Investing $S_0$ at rate $r$ per year, with continuous compounding: $S(t) = S_0 e^{rt}$. When does it double?

$$2 S_0 = S_0 e^{rt} \Rightarrow t_\text{doubl} = \frac{\ln 2}{r} \approx \frac{0.693}{r}$$

**Rule of 70** (finance shortcut): $t_\text{doubl} \approx 70/r\%$. At 7% annually, doubles in ~10 years. At 14%, doubles in ~5 years. At 1% annually (real savings after inflation), doubles in ~70 years.

**Operational implication**: 1% difference in rate seems small, but over 30 years of retirement, $10{,}000 \cdot 1.07^{30} \approx 76{,}000$ vs $10{,}000 \cdot 1.08^{30} \approx 100{,}000$ — **$24,000 difference** from 1% per year. That's why a fund with 1% higher administrative fee than a competitor is infinitely expensive.

</Porta>

## Worked examples

> Five examples in increasing difficulty — from direct log calculation to pH modeling in chemistry. Each cites an open source.

<Exemplo titulo="Example 1 — Calculate log directly (application)" numero="1">

**Problem:** Calculate $\log_2 32$.

**Strategy:** Write the argument as a power of the base; the exponent is the answer.

**Solution:**

1. **Factor the argument:** $32 = 2^5$.
2. **Apply the definition:** $\log_2 32 = \log_2 (2^5) = 5$ (since $\log_a (a^p) = p$).

**Verification:** $2^5 = 32$. \checkmark

**Source.** [OpenStax — Algebra and Trigonometry 2e §6.3](https://openstax.org/books/algebra-and-trigonometry-2e/pages/6-3-logarithmic-functions) — CC-BY 4.0 license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Example 2 — Use operational properties (intermediate)" numero="2">

**Problem:** Simplify $\log_2 8 + \log_2 4 - \log_2 16$.

**Strategy:** Use the properties $\log_a(xy) = \log_a x + \log_a y$ and $\log_a(x/y) = \log_a x - \log_a y$ to reduce everything to one log.

**Solution:**

1. **Combine terms via properties:** $\log_2 8 + \log_2 4 - \log_2 16 = \log_2 \dfrac{8 \cdot 4}{16} = \log_2 \dfrac{32}{16} = \log_2 2 = 1$.

**Alternative** (compute separately): $\log_2 8 = 3$, $\log_2 4 = 2$, $\log_2 16 = 4$. Sum: $3 + 2 - 4 = 1$. \checkmark

**Verification:** $2^1 = 2$, and the simplified expression gave $\log_2 2 = 1$. \checkmark

**Source.** [OpenStax — Algebra and Trigonometry 2e §6.5](https://openstax.org/books/algebra-and-trigonometry-2e/pages/6-5-logarithmic-properties) — CC-BY 4.0 license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Example 3 — Logarithmic equation (intermediate)" numero="3">

**Problem:** Solve $\log_2 x + \log_2 (x - 2) = 3$.

**Strategy:** Use the product property $\log_a u + \log_a v = \log_a(uv)$ to combine into one log, then convert to exponential form.

**Solution:**

1. **Combine logs:** $\log_2 x + \log_2 (x - 2) = \log_2 [x(x - 2)] = 3$.
2. **Convert to exponential:** $x(x - 2) = 2^3 = 8$.
3. **Expand and rearrange:** $x^2 - 2x = 8 \Rightarrow x^2 - 2x - 8 = 0$.
4. **Factor or use quadratic formula:** $(x - 4)(x + 2) = 0 \Rightarrow x = 4$ or $x = -2$.
5. **Check domain:** $x > 0$ and $x - 2 > 0$ require $x > 2$. Only $x = 4$ is valid.

**Verification:** $\log_2 4 + \log_2 2 = 2 + 1 = 3$. \checkmark

**Source.** [OpenStax — College Algebra 2e §6.6](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/6-6-exponential-and-logarithmic-equations) — CC-BY 4.0 license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Example 4 — Change of base (advanced)" numero="4">

**Problem:** Calculate $\log_3 50$ (use a calculator if needed).

**Strategy:** Use the change-of-base formula to convert to natural log or log base 10.

**Solution:**

1. **Apply change of base:** $\log_3 50 = \frac{\ln 50}{\ln 3} = \frac{\log 50}{\log 3}$ (either base works).
2. **Use natural log:** $\ln 50 \approx 3.912$ and $\ln 3 \approx 1.099$.
3. **Divide:** $\frac{3.912}{1.099} \approx 3.561$.

**Verification:** $3^{3.561} \approx 50.1$ (close, rounding error). \checkmark

**Source.** [OpenStax — Algebra and Trigonometry 2e §6.4](https://openstax.org/books/algebra-and-trigonometry-2e/pages/6-4-logarithmic-scales) — CC-BY 4.0 license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Example 5 — pH and acid concentration (real modeling)" numero="5">

**Problem:** A solution has $[H^+] = 10^{-5}$ mol/L. Calculate its pH.

**Strategy:** Use the definition $\text{pH} = -\log_{10}[H^+]$ directly.

**Solution:**

1. **Apply the formula:** $\text{pH} = -\log_{10}(10^{-5}) = -(-5) = 5$.
2. **Interpret:** pH 5 is acidic (below 7), typical of acid rain.

**Comparison:**
- pH 7: neutral (pure water, $[H^+] = 10^{-7}$)
- pH 3: very acidic (stomach acid, $[H^+] = 10^{-3}$)
- pH 10: basic (soapy water, $[H^+] = 10^{-10}$)

**Source.** [OpenStax — Algebra and Trigonometry 2e §6.7](https://openstax.org/books/algebra-and-trigonometry-2e/pages/6-7-exponential-and-logarithmic-models) — CC-BY 4.0 license.
</Exemplo>
