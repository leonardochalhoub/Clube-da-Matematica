### Function composition

<Definicao titulo="Composition">

Let $f: B \to C$ and $g: A \to B$. The **composition** $f \circ g : A \to C$ is the function defined by

$$
(f \circ g)(x) = f(g(x)), \quad \forall x \in A.
$$

The domain of $f \circ g$ is the set of all $x \in \operatorname{dom}(g)$ such that $g(x) \in \operatorname{dom}(f)$:

$$
\operatorname{dom}(f \circ g) = \{x \in \operatorname{dom}(g) : g(x) \in \operatorname{dom}(f)\}.
$$

</Definicao>

> "When we combine functions in such a way that the output of one function becomes the input of another, we call this the **composition of functions**. The resulting function is called a *composed function*." — [OpenStax College Algebra 2e, §3.4](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/3-4-composition-of-functions)

<Insight>

**Order matters.** In general $f \circ g \neq g \circ f$. For $f(x) = 2x$ and $g(x) = x + 3$: $(f \circ g)(x) = 2x + 6$ and $(g \circ f)(x) = 2x + 3$. Composition is like getting dressed: socks before shoes is different from shoes before socks.

</Insight>

### Inverse function

<Definicao titulo="Inverse">

$f: A \to B$ admits an **inverse function** if and only if $f$ is **bijective**. In that case, the function $f^{-1}: B \to A$ is the unique one satisfying simultaneously:

$$
f^{-1}(f(x)) = x \quad \forall x \in A, \qquad f(f^{-1}(y)) = y \quad \forall y \in B.
$$

Equivalently, $f \circ f^{-1} = \operatorname{id}_B$ and $f^{-1} \circ f = \operatorname{id}_A$.

</Definicao>

> "For a function to have an inverse function, it must be a **one-to-one function**. A function is one-to-one if each output value corresponds to exactly one input value." — [Stitz–Zeager Precalculus, §5.2](https://www.stitz-zeager.com/szprecalculus07042013.pdf)

<Definicao titulo="Algorithm to find the inverse">

Given $y = f(x)$, to find $f^{-1}$:

1. Write $y = f(x)$.
2. Swap $x \leftrightarrow y$: write $x = f(y)$.
3. Isolate $y$ — the result is $f^{-1}(x)$.

</Definicao>

<Teorema titulo="Inverse of a composite">

If $f: B \to C$ and $g: A \to B$ are both bijective, then $f \circ g: A \to C$ is bijective and

$$(f \circ g)^{-1} = g^{-1} \circ f^{-1}.$$

**Proof (sketch).** Let us compute $(g^{-1} \circ f^{-1}) \circ (f \circ g)$. By associativity of composition: $g^{-1} \circ (f^{-1} \circ f) \circ g = g^{-1} \circ \operatorname{id}_B \circ g = g^{-1} \circ g = \operatorname{id}_A$. ∎

</Teorema>

<Insight>

**Mnemonic for socks and shoes.** To undo "putting on socks and then shoes," you must first **take off shoes**, then socks — reversed order. That is $(f \circ g)^{-1} = g^{-1} \circ f^{-1}$.

</Insight>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

You have **two machines**.

The first machine takes a number and **adds 1**. Put in 3, get 4. Put in 7, get 8.

The second machine takes a number and **multiplies by 2**. Put in 4, get 8. Put in 5, get 10.

If you hook the two up in a line, the output of the first machine goes into the second. Put 3 in the first: get 4. Enter into the second: get 8. That is **composition** — two machines in a line.

And there is the machine that **undoes**. If the machine adds 1, the undoing machine subtracts 1. You throw 8 in it: get 7. The undoing machine is the **inverse**.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

**Composition** is doing two formulas in sequence.

Example: $g(x) = x - 2$ and $f(u) = 3u + 5$. Composition $f$ after $g$:

1. Apply $g$ to the $x$: gives $x - 2$.
2. Throw the result into $f$: gives $3(x - 2) + 5 = 3x - 1$.

Therefore $(f \circ g)(x) = 3x - 1$.

**Inverse** means undo. If the formula is $f(x) = 2x + 3$, to undo: subtract 3, then divide by 2. Result: $f^{-1}(x) = (x - 3)/2$.

**Visual trick.** On the graph, the inverse is the reflection of the original across the **diagonal line** $y = x$.

**Not every function has an inverse.** $f(x) = x^2$ does not. Why? Because $f(2) = 4$ and $f(-2) = 4$: if I give you just "4", you don't know if it came from 2 or from −2. There is no way to undo it.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old teenager">

**Composition** is to chain two operations: the output of $g$ becomes the input of $f$.

**Pay attention to order:** $f \circ g$ reads "f after g", and in general $f \circ g \neq g \circ f$.

For $f(x) = 2x$ and $g(x) = x + 3$:
- $(f \circ g)(x) = f(g(x)) = f(x+3) = 2(x+3) = 2x + 6$
- $(g \circ f)(x) = g(f(x)) = g(2x) = 2x + 3$

Different!

**Domain of the composite.** When computing $f(g(x))$, $g(x)$ must "land" in the domain of $f$. Restrictions appear: for $f(x) = \sqrt{x}$ and $g(x) = x^2 - 4$, we need $g(x) \geq 0$, that is, $x^2 \geq 4$, so $x \leq -2$ or $x \geq 2$.

**Algorithm to find $f^{-1}$:** swap $x \leftrightarrow y$, isolate.

Example: $f(x) = 4x - 7$.
$$y = 4x - 7 \quad \to \quad x = 4y - 7 \quad \to \quad y = \frac{x + 7}{4}$$
So $f^{-1}(x) = (x + 7)/4$.

**Verification:** $f(f^{-1}(x)) = 4 \cdot \dfrac{x+7}{4} - 7 = x$. Right!

<Insight>

**Condition for existence of $f^{-1}$: bijection.** A function has an inverse if and only if it is injective (each output has at most one input) and surjective (each output is reached by at least one input). This is tested graphically by the **horizontal line test**: if no horizontal line crosses the graph more than once, the function is injective.

</Insight>

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

Composition is the central mechanism of calculus: the **chain rule** (Trim 6) is exactly $(f \circ g)'(x) = f'(g(x)) \cdot g'(x)$. Correct decomposition of an expression into inner/outer parts is a prerequisite for differentiating correctly.

### Domain of the composite — with care

$\operatorname{dom}(f \circ g) = \{x \in \operatorname{dom}(g) : g(x) \in \operatorname{dom}(f)\}$.

For $f(x) = 1/(x-2)$ and $g(x) = 1/x$: we need $x \neq 0$ (so $g$ is defined) and $g(x) \neq 2$, i.e., $1/x \neq 2$, i.e., $x \neq 1/2$. So $\operatorname{dom}(f \circ g) = \mathbb{R} \setminus \{0, 1/2\}$.

### Inverse: bijective or restrict

$f$ has a global inverse $\iff$ $f$ is bijective. In practice:

- **Not injective?** Restrict the domain. $f(x) = x^2$ becomes injective and invertible on $[0, +\infty)$ with $f^{-1}(y) = \sqrt{y}$.
- **Not surjective?** Restrict the codomain to the image.

### Symmetric group

The bijective functions from a set $A$ to itself, under composition, form a **group** called $\text{Sym}(A)$ (or $S_n$ when $A$ has $n$ finite elements). The neutral element is $\operatorname{id}_A$; the inverse of $f$ is $f^{-1}$. This is the fundamental structure of abstract algebra.

### Simple cryptography

A **substitution cipher** encrypts via $E: \{a,\ldots,z\} \to \{0,\ldots,25\}$ and decrypts via $D = E^{-1}$. If $E$ is not bijective, decryption is impossible. The Caesar cipher is $E(x) = (x + k) \bmod 26$ with inverse $E^{-1}(y) = (y - k) \bmod 26$.

### Notation: $f^{-1} \neq 1/f$

$f^{-1}(x)$ denotes the inverse function of $f$. NOT $1/f(x)$. The confusion persists until 2nd year university.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

**Functional composition is the foundation of every pipeline.** In Haskell, `(f . g) x = f (g x)`. In Python, `functools.reduce(compose, [f, g, h])`. In SQL, subqueries are compositions. In sklearn, `Pipeline` is a composition of transformers. Every abstraction of "data flow" is, at bottom, the algebra of compositions.

### Inverse problems and regularization

In engineering, the classic inverse problem is: **given $y$, find $x$ such that $f(x) = y$.** In computed tomography, $f$ is the Radon operator; in seismology, it is the wave-propagation operator. If $f$ is not injective (underdetermined system), or if there is noise, "$f^{-1}$" is ill-posed in the Hadamard sense. The solution: **Tikhonov regularization** minimizes $\|f(x) - y\|^2 + \lambda \|x\|^2$, producing a stable pseudo-inverse. Ridge regression in ML is exactly that.

### Iterated functions and dynamics

$f^n(x) = \underbrace{f \circ f \circ \cdots \circ f}_{n}(x)$. The study of orbits $\{x, f(x), f^2(x), \ldots\}$ is the nucleus of **dynamical systems theory**: fixed points, cycles, attractors. The Mandelbrot set is $\{c \in \mathbb{C} : f_c^n(0)\text{ does not diverge}\}$ where $f_c(z) = z^2 + c$.

**Neural networks are compositions.** An $L$-layer network is $f_\theta = f^{(L)} \circ \cdots \circ f^{(1)}$, where each $f^{(k)}(x) = \sigma(W^{(k)} x + b^{(k)})$. Training is to optimize the parameters of that composition via backpropagation — which is exactly the chain rule applied recursively.

### Inverses at industrial scale

Computing $f^{-1}$ analytically is rare in production. One uses **Newton-Raphson** to solve $f(x) = y$ numerically. Computing implied volatility of Black-Scholes (lesson 119) is exactly that: given the market price of an option, numerically invert the BS formula to extract $\sigma$.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practice — real-data examples">

### Celsius/Fahrenheit conversion

Directly, from Brazil to the US: $F(C) = \dfrac{9}{5}C + 32$.

Inverse (Fahrenheit to Celsius): swap, isolate:

$$x = \frac{9}{5}y + 32 \quad\Rightarrow\quad y = \frac{5}{9}(x - 32)$$

So $F^{-1}(x) = \dfrac{5}{9}(x - 32)$.

Application: 100 °F (fever in the US) $\Rightarrow$ $\dfrac{5}{9}(68) = 37.78$ °C. Normal temperature: $F^{-1}(98.6) = 37$ °C.

### Normalization pipeline (ML / data science)

In preprocessing, z-score normalization is the composition:

1. $g(x) = x - \bar{x}$ (centers)
2. $f(y) = y/\sigma$ (scales)

Composition: $(f \circ g)(x) = (x - \bar{x})/\sigma$.

Inverse to untransform model predictions: $(f \circ g)^{-1} = g^{-1} \circ f^{-1}$, that is, first $f^{-1}(z) = z \cdot \sigma$, then $g^{-1}(y) = y + \bar{x}$. Result: $(f \circ g)^{-1}(z) = z \cdot \sigma + \bar{x}$.

### Caesar cipher

Encodes each letter $\ell \in \{0, \ldots, 25\}$ with shift $k$:

$$E_k(\ell) = (\ell + k) \bmod 26$$

Decryption = inverse: $D_k(\ell) = (\ell - k) \bmod 26 = E_{-k}(\ell)$.

For $k = 3$: "A" (=0) $\to$ "D" (=3). Decrypts: "D" (=3) $\to$ "A" (=0). The decryption function is literally $E_k^{-1}$.

</Porta>

## Worked examples

<Exemplo titulo="Calculate (f∘g)(x) — note that order changes the result" numero="1">

**Problem:** Given $f(x) = 3x + 1$ and $g(x) = x^2$, calculate $(f \circ g)(x)$ and $(g \circ f)(x)$ and compare.

**Strategy:** Apply the definition $(f \circ g)(x) = f(g(x))$ — first evaluate $g(x)$, then substitute the result into $f$. Repeat in reverse order for $(g \circ f)$.

**Solution:**

1. $(f \circ g)(x) = f(g(x)) = f(x^2) = 3x^2 + 1$.
2. $(g \circ f)(x) = g(f(x)) = g(3x + 1) = (3x + 1)^2 = 9x^2 + 6x + 1$.
3. Compare: $3x^2 + 1 \neq 9x^2 + 6x + 1$. Order changed everything.

**Verification:** At $x = 2$: $(f \circ g)(2) = 3 \cdot 4 + 1 = 13$ and $(g \circ f)(2) = 7^2 = 49$. Distinct, confirming non-commutativity.

**Source.** [OpenStax College Algebra 2e §3.4](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/3-4-composition-of-functions) — CC-BY 4.0.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Find the inverse of an affine function and verify" numero="2">

**Problem:** Determine $f^{-1}(x)$ for $f(x) = 4x - 7$. Verify that $f \circ f^{-1} = f^{-1} \circ f = \operatorname{id}$.

**Strategy:** Standard algorithm: write $y = f(x)$, swap $x \leftrightarrow y$, isolate $y$. Then validate.

**Solution:**

1. $y = 4x - 7$.
2. Swap: $x = 4y - 7$.
3. Isolate: $4y = x + 7 \Rightarrow y = (x + 7)/4$.
4. So $f^{-1}(x) = (x + 7)/4$.

**Verification:**
- $f(f^{-1}(x)) = 4 \cdot \dfrac{x+7}{4} - 7 = (x + 7) - 7 = x$. ✓
- $f^{-1}(f(x)) = \dfrac{(4x - 7) + 7}{4} = \dfrac{4x}{4} = x$. ✓

**Source.** [OpenStax College Algebra 2e §5.7](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/5-7-inverses-and-radical-functions) — CC-BY 4.0.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Domain of composite with square root — watch out for restrictions" numero="3">

**Problem:** Let $f(x) = \sqrt{x}$ and $g(x) = x^2 - 4$. Determine $(f \circ g)(x)$ and the maximum domain.

**Strategy:** The composite exists where $g(x)$ lands in the domain of $f$, that is, where $g(x) \geq 0$.

**Solution:**

1. $(f \circ g)(x) = f(g(x)) = \sqrt{x^2 - 4}$.
2. To exist: $x^2 - 4 \geq 0 \Rightarrow x^2 \geq 4 \Rightarrow |x| \geq 2$.
3. Domain: $(-\infty, -2] \cup [2, +\infty)$.

**Verification:** At $x = 3$: $g(3) = 5$; $f(5) = \sqrt{5}$. And $\sqrt{9-4} = \sqrt{5}$. ✓. At $x = 1$: $g(1) = -3 < 0$, outside the domain of $f$ — correct.

**Source.** [Stitz–Zeager Precalculus §5.1](https://www.stitz-zeager.com/szprecalculus07042013.pdf) — CC-BY-NC-SA.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Inverse of a quadratic function with domain restriction" numero="4">

**Problem:** $f(x) = x^2 - 4x + 7$ is not invertible on $\mathbb{R}$. Restrict the domain so it becomes invertible and determine $f^{-1}$.

**Strategy:** Complete the square to identify the vertex. Restrict to one branch (convention: right branch) and invert.

**Solution:**

1. $f(x) = (x-2)^2 + 3$. Vertex $(2, 3)$, parabola increasing for $x \geq 2$.
2. Restrict to $A = [2, +\infty)$: $f$ is strictly increasing, hence injective.
3. $y = (x-2)^2 + 3 \Rightarrow (x-2)^2 = y - 3$. Since $x \geq 2$, we have $x - 2 = \sqrt{y-3}$, so $x = 2 + \sqrt{y-3}$.
4. $f^{-1}: [3, +\infty) \to [2, +\infty)$, $f^{-1}(y) = 2 + \sqrt{y-3}$.

**Verification:** $f^{-1}(7) = 2 + \sqrt{4} = 4$. $f(4) = (4-2)^2 + 3 = 7$. ✓

**Source.** [OpenStax College Algebra 2e §5.7](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/5-7-inverses-and-radical-functions) — CC-BY 4.0.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Composition in chain: currency conversion modeled as inverse of composite" numero="5">

**Problem:** Dollar to real conversion via $f(d) = 5.00 \cdot d$ (simplified rate). Real to loyalty points via $g(r) = r/10$. (a) Model the composite function dollar to points. (b) Determine the inverse points to dollar. (c) 150 points equals how many dollars?

**Strategy:** Direct composition is $g \circ f$. The inverse is $(g \circ f)^{-1} = f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}$ — watch the reversed order.

**Solution:**

1. $(g \circ f)(d) = g(f(d)) = g(5d) = 5d/10 = d/2$.
2. $f^{-1}(r) = r/5$ and $g^{-1}(p) = 10p$.
3. $(g \circ f)^{-1}(p) = f^{-1}(g^{-1}(p)) = f^{-1}(10p) = 10p/5 = 2p$.
4. For $p = 150$: $2 \cdot 150 = 300$ dollars.

**Verification:** $300$ US\$ → R\$ $1500$ → $150$ points. ✓

**Source.** [Yoshiwara — Modeling, Functions, and Graphs chapter 4](https://textbooks.aimath.org/textbooks/approved-textbooks/yoshiwara/) — free.
</Exemplo>
