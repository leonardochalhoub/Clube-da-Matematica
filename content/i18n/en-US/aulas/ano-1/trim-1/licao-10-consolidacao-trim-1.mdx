### Conceptual arc of the trimester

Trim 1 builds a single idea from the ground up: **how to describe change**.

$$
\underbrace{\text{Sets}}_{\text{L1}} \to \underbrace{\text{Functions}}_{\text{L2}} \to \underbrace{f(x) = ax+b}_{\text{L3: constant ARC}} \to \underbrace{f(x) = ax^2+bx+c}_{\text{L4: linear ARC}} \to \underbrace{f \circ g,\ f^{-1}}_{\text{L5}} \to \underbrace{a^x,\ \ln x}_{\text{L6-7: ARC proportional to value}} \to \underbrace{N_0 e^{kt}}_{\text{L8}} \to \underbrace{\Delta y/\Delta x}_{\text{L9: gateway to calculus}}
$$

Each step answers the question "what happens to $y$ when $x$ changes a little?": linear (always the same), quadratic (grows linearly), exponential (grows proportionally).

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old">

**This lesson is the test of what you learned.** If you understood Trim 1, you will solve 70%+ of these problems in 4 h.

**Mental map of what must be fluent:**

1. **Sets and domain**: every function has restrictions. Log requires a positive argument; square root requires a non-negative radicand; the denominator cannot be zero.
2. **Function** $=$ a box that receives $x$ and returns $f(x)$. Domain = valid inputs. Range = possible outputs.
3. **Linear** $f(x) = ax + b$: rate of change **constant** $= a$.
4. **Quadratic** $f(x) = ax^2 + bx + c$: parabola, vertex at $x = -b/(2a)$.
5. **Exponential** $f(x) = a \cdot b^x$: grows/decreases **proportional to its own value**.
6. **Logarithm** $\log_b a$: the missing exponent — inverse of the exponential.
7. **ARC** $= \Delta y/\Delta x$: the "average speed" of the function on an interval.

**How to approach combined problems:**

1. **Identify** the type(s) of function involved.
2. **Determine** domain and range based on the physical/real context.
3. **Solve** symbolically.
4. **Verify** with a concrete value: substitute an extreme value and check that it makes sense.

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

Trim 1 built the **basic vocabulary** of real-valued functions. You now possess the catalog of elementary functions (polynomial, exponential, logarithmic) and the concept of **average rate of change** — the antechamber of the derivative.

### Connection to calculus (Year 2 preview)

The ARC on $[a, b]$ is $(f(b) - f(a))/(b - a)$. As $b \to a$, this becomes the **derivative** $f'(a)$. **This entire lesson is precalculus**: the functions you manipulate here are the ones that will be differentiated and integrated in Year 2.

### Unifying diagram (via ARC)

| Family | $f(x)$ | ARC on $[a, b]$ | Behavior |
|---|---|---|---|
| Linear | $ax+b$ | $a$ (constant) | Grows/decreases uniformly |
| Quadratic | $ax^2+bx+c$ | $a(a+b) + b$ — linear in $b$ | Vertex = point of zero ARC |
| Exponential | $c \cdot \alpha^x$ | $c\alpha^a(\alpha^{b-a}-1)/(b-a)$ | Proportional to current value |
| Logarithmic | $\log_\alpha x$ | $\log_\alpha(b/a)/(b-a)$ | Decreases with interval |

The derivative is the limit of each row of this table as $b - a \to 0$.

### Cross-domain connections

| Domain | Key function from Trim 1 |
|---|---|
| **Physics**: free fall | quadratic $h = h_0 - g t^2/2$ |
| **Finance**: compound interest | exponential $S = S_0 (1+i)^t$ |
| **Biology**: population growth | exponential / logistic |
| **Chemistry**: radioactive half-life | decreasing exponential |
| **Engineering**: Beer-Lambert law | exponential $I = I_0 e^{-\mu x}$ |
| **Acoustics**: dB | logarithm $L = 10 \log_{10}(P/P_0)$ |

### Rigor of proofs

The proof exercises train:

- **Even + odd decomposition**: $f(x) = [f(x) + f(-x)]/2 + [f(x) - f(-x)]/2$.
- **Optimization without calculus**: completing the square.
- **Substitution $u = 2^x$** to reduce an exponential to a quadratic.

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

From the analyst's perspective, Trim 1 establishes the building blocks of the **space of smooth functions** $C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})$ — closed under sum, product, composition, and local inverse (where the derivative does not vanish).

### Algebraic structure

- **Polynomials** $\mathbb{R}[x]$: ring — sum and product are closed; no multiplicative inverse in general.
- **Rational functions** $\mathbb{R}(x)$: field — quotient of polynomials, defined where the denominator does not vanish.
- **Exponential and log** extend to **transcendental functions**: they satisfy no polynomial equation. Liouville (1844) proved that $e^x$ and $\ln x$ do not admit an elementary antiderivative in every context.

### Applications in ML / quant finance

- **Logistic regression**: <Eq>{`\\sigma(x) = 1/(1 + e^{-x})`}</Eq> — joins exponential and rational, trains binary classifiers.
- **Black-Scholes**: <Eq>{`C = S \\Phi(d_1) - K e^{-rT} \\Phi(d_2)`}</Eq> — option priced via exponential composed with the cumulative normal.
- **Gradient decay**: `exponential_decay` schedulers in optimizers (Adam, RMSprop) use <Eq>{`\\eta_t = \\eta_0 \\cdot \\gamma^t`}</Eq>.

### What comes next

- **Trim 2** (Lessons 11–20): trigonometry + sequences — generalization of the concept of periodic function and a bridge to limits.
- **Trim 3** (geometry) and **Trim 4** (advanced exponential and statistics) — completion of high-school precalculus.
- **Year 2 - Trim 5**: formal $\varepsilon$-$\delta$ limit. **Lesson 41** marks the transition to rigorous analysis.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practical application — electricity bill, interest, and COVID">

**Three real phenomena that use at least three families from Trim 1.**

### 1. Electricity bill (linear + threshold)

ANEEL models the basic invoice as $F(kWh) = 0{,}76 \cdot kWh + 4{,}68$ (residential Green Flag tariff, 2024, in R$). This is a **linear function**: for each additional kWh, you pay R$ 0.76. The constant term R$ 4.68 is the availability cost (minimum even with zero consumption).

If the bill exceeds R$ 200, the Red Flag kicks in with an additional R$ 2.99 per 100 kWh above 100 kWh — the function becomes **piecewise**.

### 2. Compound interest (exponential + logarithm)

An investment in Tesouro IPCA+ earns a real rate $r$ per year. If you invest R$ 1,000 in 2025 at a real rate of 6.5% per year:

$$M(t) = 1000 \cdot (1{,}065)^t$$

To find how long it takes to double: $\log_{1{,}065}(2) = \ln 2 / \ln(1{,}065) \approx 11$ years.

ARC in the first year: $M(1) - M(0) = 65$ R$/year. In the tenth year: $M(11) - M(10) \approx 131$ R$/year. The ARC **doubles** in the same period as the capital — a characteristic feature of the exponential.

### 3. Pandemic growth (exponential + logistic)

In the first months of COVID-19 in Brazil (March–April 2020), cases grew exponentially: $C(t) \approx C_0 \cdot e^{0{,}18 t}$ (daily rate ~18%).

With vaccination and control measures, the model shifts to logistic: $C(t) = K/(1 + A e^{-rt})$ — a natural brake. The inflection point (where new cases were at their maximum) corresponds to $C = K/2$ — exactly the calculation from Example 5.

**Recipe for solving any combined problem of this type:**

1. Identify which family dominates each time phase.
2. Determine domain and boundary conditions from context.
3. Use the logarithm to "extract" time from exponential targets.
4. Calculate ARC to measure the speed of change at each point.

## Worked examples

<Exemplo titulo="Domain of a composition with log (application)" numero="1">

**Problem:** Find the maximal domain of $f(x) = \log_2(x^2 - 4)$.

**Strategy:** The argument of the logarithm must be strictly positive. Solve the inequality $x^2 - 4 > 0$ by factoring and sign analysis.

**Solution:**

1. Condition: $x^2 - 4 > 0$, i.e., $(x - 2)(x + 2) > 0$.
2. Roots: $x = 2$ and $x = -2$. Parabola opening upward.
3. The inequality holds "outside the roots": $x < -2$ or $x > 2$.
4. Domain: $\text{Dom}(f) = (-\infty, -2) \cup (2, +\infty)$.

**Verification:** At $x = 3$: $f(3) = \log_2(5) \approx 2{,}32$, defined. At $x = 0$: $0^2 - 4 = -4 < 0$ — undefined, consistent with $0 \notin \text{Dom}(f)$. At $x = 2$: $\log_2(0) \to -\infty$ — not in the domain.

**Source.** [OpenStax College Algebra 2e §6.3](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/6-3-logarithmic-functions) — CC-BY 4.0 license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Exponential equation via substitution (intermediate)" numero="2">

**Problem:** Solve $4^x - 5 \cdot 2^x + 4 = 0$.

**Strategy:** Note that $4^x = (2^x)^2$. Substitute $u = 2^x$ (with $u > 0$): the equation becomes quadratic in $u$.

**Solution:**

1. Substitute $u = 2^x$: $u^2 - 5u + 4 = 0$.
2. Factor: $(u - 1)(u - 4) = 0 \Rightarrow u = 1$ or $u = 4$.
3. Back to $x$: $2^x = 1 \Rightarrow x = 0$. $2^x = 4 \Rightarrow x = 2$.
4. Verify in the original: $4^0 - 5 \cdot 2^0 + 4 = 1 - 5 + 4 = 0$ ✓. $4^2 - 5 \cdot 2^2 + 4 = 16 - 20 + 4 = 0$ ✓.
5. Answer: $x = 0$ or $x = 2$.

**Verification:** Both solutions satisfy the original equation. The substitution $u = 2^x$ is canonical: whenever $a^{2x}$ appears together with $a^x$, substitute $u = a^x$.

**Source.** [Stitz–Zeager Precalculus §6.3](https://www.stitz-zeager.com/szprecalculus07042013.pdf) — CC-BY-NC-SA license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Modeling with a piecewise function (intermediate)" numero="3">

**Problem:** A pool is filled in two stages: during the first 2 hours, at a constant flow rate of 500 L/h; after that, at a constant flow rate of 800 L/h. Model the volume $V(t)$. How long does it take to fill a 6,000 L pool?

**Strategy:** Piecewise function (linear in each piece). Calculate $V(2)$ at the end of the first phase; then solve $V(t) = 6\,000$ in the second phase.

**Solution:**

1. Model: <Eq>{`V(t) = 500t`}</Eq> for <Eq>{`0 \\le t \\le 2`}</Eq>, and <Eq>{`V(t) = 1{,}000 + 800(t-2)`}</Eq> for <Eq>{`t > 2`}</Eq>.

2. At $t = 2$: $V(2) = 1\,000$ L.
3. Remaining: $6\,000 - 1\,000 = 5\,000$ L.
4. Time in 2nd phase: $5\,000 / 800 = 6{,}25$ h.
5. Total time: $2 + 6{,}25 = 8{,}25$ h.

**Verification:** $V(8{,}25) = 1\,000 + 800 \cdot (8{,}25 - 2) = 1\,000 + 5\,000 = 6\,000$ L. ✓ Continuity at $t = 2$: both expressions give 1,000 L.

**Source.** [Yoshiwara — Modeling, Functions, and Graphs ch. 2](https://textbooks.aimath.org/textbooks/approved-textbooks/yoshiwara/) — open license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Maximum profit with quadratic (advanced)" numero="4">

**Problem:** A company has cost $C(q) = 50 + 20 q + 0{,}5 q^2$ and revenue $R(q) = 60 q$. Profit is $L(q) = R(q) - C(q)$. (a) When is profit zero? (b) What quantity maximizes profit?

**Strategy:** Compute $L(q)$, find roots for (a) and the vertex of the parabola for (b). Use the quadratic formula and $x_v = -b/(2a)$.

**Solution:**

1. $L(q) = 60 q - (50 + 20 q + 0{,}5 q^2) = -0{,}5 q^2 + 40 q - 50$.
2. **(a) Zero profit:** $-0{,}5 q^2 + 40 q - 50 = 0$. Multiplying by $-2$: $q^2 - 80 q + 100 = 0$. Quadratic formula: $q = (80 \pm \sqrt{6\,000})/2 \approx (80 \pm 77{,}46)/2$. Roots: $q \approx 1{,}27$ or $q \approx 78{,}73$.
3. **(b) Maximum profit:** vertex at $q^* = -40/(2 \cdot (-0{,}5)) = 40$. Maximum profit: $L(40) = -800 + 1\,600 - 50 = 750$.
4. Answer: (a) break-even at $q \approx 1{,}27$ and $q \approx 78{,}73$; (b) maximum profit R$ 750 at $q = 40$ units.

**Verification:** Parabola opening downward (coeff. $-0{,}5 < 0$) — vertex is a maximum ✓. Average of roots: $(1{,}27 + 78{,}73)/2 = 40 = q^*$ ✓.

**Source.** [OpenStax College Algebra 2e §3.2](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/3-2-quadratic-functions) — CC-BY 4.0 license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Logistic model — inflection point (real-world modeling)" numero="5">

**Problem:** $P(t) = 200/(1 + 9 e^{-0{,}5 t})$ (logistic model). (a) Compute $P(0)$. (b) What is the carrying capacity? (c) At what time $t$ does the population reach half the carrying capacity?

**Strategy:** For (a) substitute $t = 0$. For (b) examine $t \to \infty$. For (c) solve $P(t) = 100$.

**Solution:**

1. **(a)** $P(0) = 200/(1 + 9) = 20$. Initial population = **20**.
2. **(b)** As $t \to \infty$: $e^{-0{,}5 t} \to 0$, so $P(t) \to 200$. **Carrying capacity = 200.**
3. **(c)** $200/(1 + 9 e^{-0{,}5 t}) = 100 \Rightarrow e^{-0{,}5 t} = 1/9 \Rightarrow t = 2 \ln 9 \approx 4{,}39$.

**Verification:** At $t = 4{,}39$: $P = 200/(1 + 9 \cdot 1/9) = 100$ ✓. This is the **inflection point** of the logistic curve — where the ARC is at its maximum.

**Source.** [Lebl — Notes on Diffy Qs §1.5](https://www.jirka.org/diffyqs/) — CC-BY-SA license.
</Exemplo>
