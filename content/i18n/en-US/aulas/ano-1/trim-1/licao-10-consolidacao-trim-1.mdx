### Conceptual arc of the term

Trim 1 builds a single idea from the bottom up: **how to describe change**.

$$
\underbrace{\text{Sets}}_{\text{L1}} \to \underbrace{\text{Functions}}_{\text{L2}} \to \underbrace{f(x) = ax+b}_{\text{L3: constant rate}} \to \underbrace{f(x) = ax^2+bx+c}_{\text{L4: linear rate}} \to \underbrace{f \circ g,\ f^{-1}}_{\text{L5}} \to \underbrace{a^x,\ \ln x}_{\text{L6-7: proportional rate}} \to \underbrace{N_0 e^{kt}}_{\text{L8}} \to \underbrace{\Delta y/\Delta x}_{\text{L9: gateway to calculus}}
$$

Each stage answers the question "what happens to $y$ when $x$ changes slightly?": linear (always the same), quadratic (grows linearly), exponential (grows proportionally).

**Mental map of what you must be fluent in:**

1. **Sets and domain**: every function has restrictions. Log requires argument positive; root requires radicand non-negative; denominator cannot be zero.
2. **Function** $= $ box that takes $x$ and outputs $f(x)$. Domain = valid inputs. Range = possible outputs.
3. **Linear** $f(x) = ax + b$: rate of change **constant** $= a$.
4. **Quadratic** $f(x) = ax^2 + bx + c$: parabola, vertex at $x = -b/(2a)$.
5. **Exponential** $f(x) = a \cdot b^x$: grows/decays **proportional to its own value**.
6. **Logarithm** $\log_b a$: the exponent that's missing — inverse of exponential.
7. **Rate of change** $= \Delta y/\Delta x$: the "average velocity" of the function on an interval.

**How to approach combined problems:**

1. **Identify** the type(s) of function involved.
2. **Determine** domain and range based on the physical/real context.
3. **Solve** symbolically.
4. **Check** with a concrete value: substitute an extreme value and see if it makes sense.

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

Trim 1 built the **basic vocabulary** of real functions. You now have the catalog of elementary functions (polynomial, exponential, logarithmic) and the concept of **average rate of change** — antechamber of the derivative.

### Connection to calculus (preview Year 2)

The rate of change on $[a, b]$ is $(f(b) - f(a))/(b - a)$. When $b \to a$, this becomes **derivative** $f'(a)$. **This entire lesson is precalculus**: the functions you manipulate are the ones you'll differentiate and integrate in Year 2.

### Unifying diagram (via rate of change)

| Family | $f(x)$ | Rate of change on $[a, b]$ | Behavior |
|---|---|---|---|
| Linear | $ax+b$ | $a$ (constant) | Grows/decays uniformly |
| Quadratic | $ax^2+bx+c$ | $a(a+b) + b$ — linear in $b$ | Vertex = point of zero rate |
| Exponential | $c \cdot \alpha^x$ | $c\alpha^a(\alpha^{b-a}-1)/(b-a)$ | Proportional to current value |
| Logarithmic | $\log_\alpha x$ | $\log_\alpha(b/a)/(b-a)$ | Decreases with interval |

The derivative is the limit of each line in this table when $b - a \to 0$.

### Cross-domain connection

| Domain | Key function of Trim 1 |
|---|---|
| **Physics**: free fall | quadratic $h = h_0 - g t^2/2$ |
| **Finance**: compound interest | exponential $S = S_0 (1+i)^t$ |
| **Biology**: population growth | exponential / logistic |
| **Chemistry**: radioactive half-life | exponential decay |
| **Engineering**: Beer-Lambert law | exponential $I = I_0 e^{-\mu x}$ |
| **Acoustics**: dB | logarithm $L = 10 \log_{10}(P/P_0)$ |

### Rigor of proofs

The proof exercises train:

- **Even + odd decomposition**: $f(x) = [f(x) + f(-x)]/2 + [f(x) - f(-x)]/2$.
- **Optimization without calculus**: completing the square.
- **Substitution $u = 2^x$** to reduce exponential to quadratic.

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

From the analyst's standpoint, Trim 1 establishes the building blocks of **smooth function space** $C^\infty(\mathbb{R})$ — closed under sum, product, composition, local inverse (where derivative is nonzero).

### Algebraic structure

- **Polynomials** $\mathbb{R}[x]$: ring — sum and product closed; no multiplicative inverse in general.
- **Rational functions** $\mathbb{R}(x)$: field — quotient of polynomials, defined where denominator is nonzero.
- **Exponential and log** extend to **transcendental functions**: satisfy no polynomial equation. Liouville (1844) proved that $e^x$ and $\ln x$ lack elementary antiderivatives in all contexts.

### Applications in ML / quant finance

- **Logistic regression**: $\sigma(x) = 1/(1 + e^{-x})$ — joins exponential and rational, trains binary classifiers.
- **Black-Scholes**: $C = S \Phi(d_1) - K e^{-rT} \Phi(d_2)$ — option priced via composite exponential with normal CDF.
- **Gradient decay**: schedulers `exponential_decay` in optimizers (Adam, RMSprop) use $\eta_t = \eta_0 \cdot \gamma^t$.

### What comes next

- **Trim 2** (Lessons 11–20): trigonometry + sequences — generalization of periodic function concept and bridge to limit.
- **Trim 3** (geometry) and **Trim 4** (advanced exponential and statistics) — closure of high-school precalculus.
- **Year 2 - Trim 5**: formal limit $\varepsilon$-$\delta$. **Lesson 41** marks transition to rigorous analysis.

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practical application — electricity bill, interest, and COVID">

**Three real Brazilian phenomena using at least three families from Trim 1.**

### 1. Electricity bill (linear + threshold)

ANEEL models the basic invoice as $F(kWh) = 0.76 \cdot kWh + 4.68$ (residential rate Green Flag, 2024, in R\$). This is a **linear function**: for each additional kWh, you pay R\$ 0.76. The constant R\$ 4.68 is the availability cost (minimum even with no consumption).

If the bill exceeds R\$ 200, it enters Red Flag with an additional R\$ 2.99 per 100 kWh above 100 kWh — the function becomes **piecewise**.

### 2. Compound interest (exponential + logarithm)

Investment in Treasury IPCA+ earns real rate $r$ per year. If you invest R\$ 1,000 in 2025 with real rate of 6.5% per year:

$$M(t) = 1000 \cdot (1.065)^t$$

To find when it doubles: $\log_{1.065}(2) = \ln 2 / \ln(1.065) \approx 11$ years.

Rate of change in first year: $M(1) - M(0) = 65$ R\$/year. In tenth year: $M(11) - M(10) \approx 131$ R\$/year. The rate of change **doubles** in the same timeframe the capital does — characteristic mark of exponential.

### 3. Pandemic growth (exponential + logistic)

In the first months of COVID-19 in Brazil (March–April 2020), cases grew exponentially: $C(t) \approx C_0 \cdot e^{0.18 t}$ (daily rate ~18%).

With vaccination and control measures, the model shifts to logistic: $C(t) = K/(1 + A e^{-rt})$ — natural brake. The inflection point (where new cases were highest) corresponds to $C = K/2$ — exactly the calculation in Example 5.

**Recipe to solve any combined problem of this type:**

1. Identify which family dominates each time period.
2. Determine domain and boundary conditions from context.
3. Use logarithm to "extract" the time for exponential goals.
4. Calculate rate of change to measure velocity of change at each point.

## Worked examples

<Exemplo titulo="Domain of composition with log (application)" numero="1">

**Problem:** Find the maximum domain of $f(x) = \log_2(x^2 - 4)$.

**Strategy:** The argument of the logarithm must be strictly positive. Solve the inequality $x^2 - 4 > 0$ by factoring and sign analysis.

**Solution:**

1. Condition: $x^2 - 4 > 0$, i.e., $(x - 2)(x + 2) > 0$.
2. Roots: $x = 2$ and $x = -2$. Parabola with upward concavity.
3. The inequality is satisfied "outside the roots": $x < -2$ or $x > 2$.
4. Domain: $\text{Dom}(f) = (-\infty, -2) \cup (2, +\infty)$.

**Check:** At $x = 3$: $f(3) = \log_2(5) \approx 2.32$, defined. At $x = 0$: $0^2 - 4 = -4 < 0$ — undefined, consistent with $0 \notin \text{Dom}(f)$. At $x = 2$: $\log_2(0) \to -\infty$ — not in the domain.

**Source.** [OpenStax College Algebra 2e §6.3](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/6-3-logarithmic-functions) — CC-BY 4.0 license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Exponential equation via substitution (intermediate)" numero="2">

**Problem:** Solve $4^x - 5 \cdot 2^x + 4 = 0$.

**Strategy:** Note that $4^x = (2^x)^2$. Substitute $u = 2^x$ (with $u > 0$): the equation becomes quadratic in $u$.

**Solution:**

1. Substitute $u = 2^x$: $u^2 - 5u + 4 = 0$.
2. Factor: $(u - 1)(u - 4) = 0 \Rightarrow u = 1$ or $u = 4$.
3. Back to variable $x$: $2^x = 1 \Rightarrow x = 0$. $2^x = 4 \Rightarrow x = 2$.
4. Verify in original: $4^0 - 5 \cdot 2^0 + 4 = 1 - 5 + 4 = 0$ ✓. $4^2 - 5 \cdot 2^2 + 4 = 16 - 20 + 4 = 0$ ✓.
5. Answer: $x = 0$ or $x = 2$.

**Check:** Both solutions satisfy the original equation. The substitution $u = 2^x$ is canonical: whenever $a^{2x}$ appears together with $a^x$, substitute $u = a^x$.

**Source.** [Stitz–Zeager Precalculus §6.3](https://www.stitz-zeager.com/szprecalculus07042013.pdf) — CC-BY-NC-SA license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Piecewise function modeling (intermediate)" numero="3">

**Problem:** A pool is filled in two stages: in the first 2 hours, constant flow of 500 L/h; then, constant flow of 800 L/h. Model the volume $V(t)$. How long does it take to fill a 6,000 L pool?

**Strategy:** Piecewise function (linear in each piece). Calculate $V(2)$ at the end of first phase; then solve $V(t) = 6{,}000$ in the second phase.

**Solution:**

1. Model:

$$V(t) = \begin{cases} 500 t & \text{if } 0 \leq t \leq 2 \\ 1{,}000 + 800 (t - 2) & \text{if } t > 2 \end{cases}$$

2. At $t = 2$: $V(2) = 1{,}000$ L.
3. Missing: $6{,}000 - 1{,}000 = 5{,}000$ L to fill.
4. Time in 2nd phase: $5{,}000 / 800 = 6.25$ h.
5. Total time: $2 + 6.25 = 8.25$ h.

**Check:** $V(8.25) = 1{,}000 + 800 \cdot (8.25 - 2) = 1{,}000 + 5{,}000 = 6{,}000$ L. ✓ Continuity at $t = 2$: both expressions give 1,000 L.

**Source.** [Yoshiwara — Modeling, Functions, and Graphs ch. 2](https://textbooks.aimath.org/textbooks/approved-textbooks/yoshiwara/) — open license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Maximum profit with quadratic (advanced)" numero="4">

**Problem:** A firm has cost $C(q) = 50 + 20 q + 0.5 q^2$ and revenue $R(q) = 60 q$. Profit is $L(q) = R(q) - C(q)$. (a) When is profit zero? (b) What quantity maximizes profit?

**Strategy:** Calculate $L(q)$, find roots for (a) and the vertex of the parabola for (b). Use Bhaskara and $x_v = -b/(2a)$.

**Solution:**

1. $L(q) = 60 q - (50 + 20 q + 0.5 q^2) = -0.5 q^2 + 40 q - 50$.
2. **(a) Zero profit:** $-0.5 q^2 + 40 q - 50 = 0$. Multiply by $-2$: $q^2 - 80 q + 100 = 0$. Bhaskara: $q = (80 \pm \sqrt{6{,}000})/2 \approx (80 \pm 77.46)/2$. Roots: $q \approx 1.27$ or $q \approx 78.73$.
3. **(b) Maximum profit:** vertex at $q^* = -40/(2 \cdot (-0.5)) = 40$. Maximum profit: $L(40) = -800 + 1{,}600 - 50 = 750$ (US$).
4. Answer: (a) break-even at $q \approx 1.27$ and $q \approx 78.73$; (b) maximum profit US\$ 750 at $q = 40$ units.

**Check:** Parabola with downward concavity (coef. $-0.5 < 0$) — vertex is maximum \checkmark. Average of roots: $(1.27 + 78.73)/2 = 40 = q^*$ \checkmark.

**Source.** [OpenStax College Algebra 2e §3.2](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/3-2-quadratic-functions) — CC-BY 4.0 license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Logistic model — inflection point (real modeling)" numero="5">

**Problem:** $P(t) = 200/(1 + 9 e^{-0.5 t})$ (logistic model). (a) Calculate $P(0)$. (b) What is carrying capacity? (c) At what time $t$ does the population reach half the capacity?

**Strategy:** For (a) substitute $t = 0$. For (b) examine $t \to \infty$. For (c) solve $P(t) = 100$.

**Solution:**

1. **(a)** $P(0) = 200/(1 + 9) = 20$. Initial population = **20**.
2. **(b)** When $t \to \infty$: $e^{-0.5 t} \to 0$, so $P(t) \to 200$. **Capacity = 200.**
3. **(c)** $200/(1 + 9 e^{-0.5 t}) = 100 \Rightarrow e^{-0.5 t} = 1/9 \Rightarrow t = 2 \ln 9 \approx 4.39$.

**Check:** At $t = 4.39$: $P = 200/(1 + 9 \cdot 1/9) = 100$ \checkmark. This is the **inflection point** of the logistic curve — where the rate of change is maximum.

**Source.** [Lebl — Notes on Diffy Qs §1.5](https://www.jirka.org/diffyqs/) — CC-BY-SA license.
</Exemplo>
