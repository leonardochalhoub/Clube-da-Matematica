<Porta nivel="formal" titulo="Definition and interpretation">

<Definicao titulo="Average rate of change of f on [a, b]">

For $f$ defined on $[a, b]$ with $a < b$:

$$\text{ARC}(f,\, [a, b]) = \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}$$

</Definicao>

> "The ratio $[f(b) - f(a)]/(b - a)$ is called the average rate of change of $f$ on the interval $[a, b]$." — [Active Calculus §1.3](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-1-3-derivative-pt.html)

### Geometric interpretation

The ARC is the **slope of the secant line** to the graph of $f$ through points $(a, f(a))$ and $(b, f(b))$.

### Special cases

- $f$ linear ($f(x) = mx + n$): ARC is constant and equal to $m$, regardless of the interval chosen.
- $f$ quadratic: ARC varies with the interval; it equals $a(p + q) + b$ for $f(x) = ax^2 + bx + c$ on $[p, q]$.
- $f$ constant: ARC $= 0$ for any interval.

### The question that opens calculus

**What if $\Delta x \to 0$?** The secant line "becomes" the tangent line, and the ARC converges to the **instantaneous rate of change** — which is exactly the **derivative** $f'(a)$:

$$f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h}$$

This is the topic of Trimesters 5 (limits) and 6 (derivatives). This lesson is the antechamber.

<Insight titulo="ARC and the Mean Value Theorem">

For $f$ continuous on $[a, b]$ and differentiable on $(a, b)$, there exists $c \in (a, b)$ such that $f'(c) = (f(b) - f(a))/(b - a)$. In other words: the derivative at some interior point equals exactly the ARC. A nontrivial result, formally proved in Trimester 6.

</Insight>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

You leave home for school. The school is **600 meters** from your house. You walk for **10 minutes**.

On **average**, you walked $600 / 10 = 60$ meters per minute. That is your **average speed**.

But along the way you had to wait 2 minutes at a traffic light. You stayed still, walking 0 meters in those 2 minutes. At other times you walked fast to make up time, walking 80 or 90 m/min.

Your speed **on average** was 60 m/min. But your speed **at each moment** was always changing.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

You go up an elevator from floor 0 to floor 20. It takes 30 seconds.

**Average speed** = (20 floors) / (30 s) = 0.667 floors/second.

But at the start the elevator accelerates (small speed), in the middle it goes at high speed, and at the end it decelerates. The speed **is always changing**.

The average rate of change is the speed that holds **on average**. It answers the question "how far did I go divided by how much time it took." It ignores all the details in between.

The **instantaneous speed** — the one on your speedometer at one moment — is what you get when the time interval becomes **infinitesimally short**. This calculation is called the **derivative**, and it is what all this high-school math is building toward. For now, **average** rate is the path forward.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old">

This lesson is different. **This is where calculus begins.**

The average rate of change answers the question: "**by how much did $y$ change relative to $x$, on average, in this interval?**".

Velocity is the perfect example. If you drive from São Paulo to Campinas (98 km) in 1 hour 20 minutes ($= 1.33$ h), your **average velocity** is:

$$v_{\text{avg}} = \frac{\Delta s}{\Delta t} = \frac{98 \text{ km}}{1.33 \text{ h}} \approx 73.5 \text{ km/h}$$

But during the drive you passed stretches at 110 km/h and stretches at 30 km/h. The velocity was not 73.5 the whole time — it was 73.5 **on average**. Your **instantaneous velocity** at any given moment could be very different.

Here is the bridge that opens calculus:

> **What if I take intervals $[t, t + \Delta t]$ that get smaller and smaller? Does the average velocity approach the instantaneous velocity?**

Answer: YES. And this "taking intervals smaller and smaller" is what is called a **limit**. And the result is the **derivative** — the central concept of calculus.

You do not need to understand derivatives now. But remember: **the derivative is the instantaneous rate of change**. Everything you do in this lesson with $\Delta y / \Delta x$ will become $dy/dx$ in a few months.

</Porta>

### Cauchy connection

Augustin-Louis Cauchy (1821) defined the derivative as the limit of the difference quotient:

$$f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h}$$

This quotient $\frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h}$ is exactly the ARC on the interval $[a, a+h]$. Cauchy formalized the "$h \to 0$" via epsilon-delta — in Trimester 5 you will see this.

### ARC and the Mean Value Theorem

For $f$ continuous on $[a,b]$ and differentiable on $(a,b)$, there exists $c \in (a,b)$ with:

$$f'(c) = \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}$$

In other words: the derivative at some interior point equals exactly the ARC. Note: ARC (theorem) and ARC (average rate of change) share the same acronym; the theorem guarantees the existence of a point where the tangent is parallel to the secant.

### ARC in finite differences

To solve ODEs numerically, one approximates $\dot y(t) \approx (y(t+h) - y(t))/h$ — **forward difference**, first order. These schemes form the foundation of Euler, Runge-Kutta, Crank-Nicolson.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

The ARC is the **universal discretization** of the derivative. In any application where you compute a derivative **from data**, you are computing an ARC.

### Finite differences in numerical methods

To solve ODEs numerically, one approximates $\dot y(t) \approx (y(t+h) - y(t))/h$ — **forward difference**, first order. Or $\dot y(t) \approx (y(t+h) - y(t-h))/(2h)$ — **central difference**, more accurate second order.

These schemes form the foundation of Euler, Runge-Kutta, Crank-Nicolson — all numerical integration of differential equations (REAMAT ch. 5).

### Estimating derivatives from noisy data

In the lab, you have $(t_i, y_i)$ with noise. Computing $(y_{i+1} - y_i)/(t_{i+1} - t_i)$ amplifies the noise (derivative is a "high-pass filter"). Solutions:

- **Smoothing** (Savitzky-Golay filter): fit a local polynomial before differentiating.
- **Local regression** (LOESS): moving regression line.
- **Kalman filter**: estimates derivative and state simultaneously.

### ARC in finance

**Simple return** $r_t = (P_t - P_{t-1})/P_{t-1}$ is exactly the ARC of the price. **Log return** $\log(P_t/P_{t-1})$ is an ARC in log space. In practice, log-returns are preferred: they sum, they are treatable under normality, and $\log(1+r) \approx r$ for small $r$.

### ARC in ML/DL

**Numerical gradient**: $\nabla f \approx (f(x + h) - f(x))/h$. Used in back-propagation verification. Always compare analytical gradient (autograd) vs numerical before trusting.

### Caveat: machine precision

For very small $h$, $f(x+h) - f(x)$ suffers **catastrophic cancellation** in float64. Truncation error ($O(h)$) decreases with $h$, but rounding error ($\sim \varepsilon_\text{machine}/h$) grows. Optimal: $h \approx \sqrt{\varepsilon} \approx 10^{-8}$. Below that, numerical noise dominates.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practice — 3 real scenarios">

### Scenario 1: Average velocity of a car by radar

A toll booth in São Paulo records a car passing kilometer 50 at 14:30 and kilometer 200 at 15:50. Distance 150 km, time 1 hour 20 minutes = 4/3 h. Average velocity:

$$v_\text{avg} = \frac{150}{4/3} = 112.5 \text{ km/h}$$

Speed limit: 110 km/h. **Fined**, even without radar catching him in motion — it is the **ARC of position** that proves the offense.

### Scenario 2: Annualized variation in Brazil's GDP

Brazil's GDP (in real 2025 constant values): USD 5.5 trillion in 2010, USD 8.3 trillion in 2020. Compound growth:

$$r = \left(\frac{8.3}{5.5}\right)^{1/10} - 1 = 1.0413 - 1 = 0.0413 = 4.13\% \text{ p.a.}$$

**Real average annual growth: 4.13%.** Use this to compare with inflation, real interest rate, real minimum wage. **Do not use linear ARC** $(8.3 - 5.5)/10 = 0.28$ trillion/year — that number is not a rate, it is absolute change.

### Scenario 3: Approximate marginal cost

A company has cost $C(q) = 0.5 q^2 + 30 q + 200$ (USD). Calculate the cost of producing one more unit when already producing 100.

**Method 1: ARC on $[100, 101]$.** $C(100) = 5,000 + 3,000 + 200 = 8,200$. $C(101) = 5,100.5 + 3,030 + 200 = 8,330.5$. ARC = USD 130.5 per unit.

**Method 2: derivative (preview).** $C'(q) = q + 30$. $C'(100) = 130$ USD per unit.

**Comparison:** the ARC (130.5) is a **good estimate** of marginal cost (130). The difference comes from the quadratic term. For smaller $\Delta q$, the ARC approaches even closer. This is the essence of the derivative concept.

</Porta>

## Worked examples

<Exemplo titulo="ARC of f(x) = x² on [1, 4] (direct application)" numero="1">

**Problem:** Calculate the average rate of change of $f(x) = x^2$ on the interval $[1, 4]$.

**Strategy:** Apply the definition $\text{ARC} = (f(b) - f(a))/(b - a)$ directly with $a = 1$, $b = 4$.

**Solution:**

1. Compute $f(1) = 1^2 = 1$ and $f(4) = 4^2 = 16$.
2. Apply the formula: $\text{ARC} = (16 - 1)/(4 - 1) = 15/3 = 5$.
3. Answer: **ARC = 5**.

**Verification:** This is the slope of the secant line through $(1, 1)$ and $(4, 16)$. Equation of the secant: $y - 1 = 5(x - 1)$, or $y = 5x - 4$. Substituting $x = 4$: $y = 16$, checks out. Substituting $x = 1$: $y = 1$, checks out.

**Source.** [Active Calculus §1.3](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-1-3-derivative-pt.html) — CC-BY-NC-SA license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Average velocity of free fall (kinematics)" numero="2">

**Problem:** The height of a falling stone is $h(t) = 80 - 5t^2$ (m). Calculate the average velocity on the interval $[1, 3]$ s.

**Strategy:** Average velocity is the ARC of position. Since $h$ is in meters and $t$ in seconds, the ARC has units of m/s.

**Solution:**

1. Compute $h(1) = 80 - 5 = 75$ m and $h(3) = 80 - 45 = 35$ m.
2. Change in height: $\Delta h = 35 - 75 = -40$ m (negative: the stone fell).
3. Change in time: $\Delta t = 3 - 1 = 2$ s.
4. Average velocity: $v_m = \Delta h/\Delta t = -40/2 = -20$ m/s.

**Verification:** The derivative $h'(t) = -10t$ (calculus preview). At the midpoint $t = 2$: $h'(2) = -20$ m/s. By the Mean Value Theorem, for quadratics the derivative at the midpoint coincides with the ARC — confirmed.

**Source.** [OpenStax Calculus Volume 1 §2.1](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/2-1-a-preview-of-calculus) — CC-BY-NC-SA license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="ARC on [a, a+h] and the limit that becomes derivative (intermediate)" numero="3">

**Problem:** For $f(x) = x^2$, find the ARC on the interval $[a, a+h]$ as a function of $a$ and $h$. What happens when $h \to 0$?

**Strategy:** Apply the definition with endpoints $a$ and $a + h$, then simplify algebraically.

**Solution:**

1. $f(a + h) = (a + h)^2 = a^2 + 2ah + h^2$.
2. Numerator: $f(a + h) - f(a) = 2ah + h^2 = h(2a + h)$.
3. Denominator: $(a + h) - a = h$.
4. ARC $= h(2a + h)/h = 2a + h$ (valid for $h \neq 0$).
5. As $h \to 0$: ARC $\to 2a$.

**Verification:** For $a = 1$ and $h = 0.1$: ARC $= 2.1$. For $h = 0.01$: ARC $= 2.01$. The limit tends to $2$. This is how Cauchy proved that $f'(x) = 2x$ for $f(x) = x^2$.

**Source.** [Active Calculus §1.3](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-1-3-derivative-pt.html) — CC-BY-NC-SA license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Marginal cost via ARC (economics, advanced)" numero="4">

**Problem:** A company has cost $C(q) = 0.5 q^2 + 30 q + 200$ (USD). Calculate the ARC of cost between $q = 100$ and $q = 101$ units. Interpret economically.

**Strategy:** ARC $= C(101) - C(100)$. Since $\Delta q = 1$, the ARC coincides with the cost of the marginal unit.

**Solution:**

1. $C(100) = 5,000 + 3,000 + 200 = 8,200$ USD.
2. $C(101) = 5,100.5 + 3,030 + 200 = 8,330.5$ USD.
3. ARC $= 8,330.5 - 8,200 = 130.5$ USD per unit.
4. **Interpretation:** producing the 101st unit costs about USD 130.50 more.

**Verification:** The derivative $C'(q) = q + 30$, so $C'(100) = 130$ USD per unit. The ARC (130.5) is a good estimate of the true marginal cost (130). The small difference (0.5) vanishes as $\Delta q \to 0$.

**Source.** [Yoshiwara — Modeling, Functions, and Graphs ch. 5](https://textbooks.aimath.org/textbooks/approved-textbooks/yoshiwara/) — open license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Estimating the derivative via successive ARCs (modeling)" numero="5">

**Problem:** For $f(x) = \sqrt{x}$, calculate the ARC on $[4, 4.5]$, $[4, 4.1]$, and $[4, 4.01]$. To what value do these ARCs converge?

**Strategy:** Compute each ARC numerically and observe the convergence. As $\Delta x$ shrinks, the ARC approaches the derivative.

**Solution:**

1. $[4, 4.5]$: $f(4) = 2$, $f(4.5) \approx 2.1213$. ARC $\approx 0.1213/0.5 \approx 0.2426$.
2. $[4, 4.1]$: $f(4.1) \approx 2.0248$. ARC $\approx 0.0248/0.1 \approx 0.2485$.
3. $[4, 4.01]$: $f(4.01) \approx 2.002498$. ARC $\approx 0.002498/0.01 \approx 0.2498$.
4. The ARCs converge to $0.25 = 1/4$. Therefore $f'(4) = 1/4$.

**Verification:** The exact derivative is $f'(x) = 1/(2\sqrt{x})$. At $x = 4$: $f'(4) = 1/4$. Confirmed.

**Source.** [Active Calculus §1.5](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-1-5-Iim-instant-rate.html) — CC-BY-NC-SA license.
</Exemplo>
