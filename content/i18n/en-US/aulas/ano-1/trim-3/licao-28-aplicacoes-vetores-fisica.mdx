<Porta nivel="formal" titulo="Foundations of vector mechanics">

### Newton's laws in vector form

<Definicao titulo="Newton's second law and equilibrium">

Let an object of mass $m$ be subject to forces $\vec{F}_1, \vec{F}_2, \ldots, \vec{F}_n$. **Newton's second law** in vector form:

$$\sum_{i=1}^{n} \vec{F}_i = m\vec{a}$$

**Static equilibrium** is the special case $\vec{a} = \vec{0}$:

$$\sum_{i=1}^{n} \vec{F}_i = \vec{0}$$

In coordinates, this produces **two independent scalar equations** in 2D:

$$\sum F_x = 0, \qquad \sum F_y = 0$$

</Definicao>

> "Newton's first law tells us that a particle that is not accelerated must have zero net force acting on it. [...] We can use this fact to determine unknown equilibrium forces." — [OpenStax University Physics Volume 1, §5.2](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/5-2-newtons-first-law)

### Decomposition into components

<Definicao titulo="Force in Cartesian components">

For a force of magnitude $F$ at angle $\theta$ from the $x$-axis:

$$F_x = F\cos\theta, \qquad F_y = F\sin\theta$$

$$\vec{F} = F_x\,\hat{\imath} + F_y\,\hat{\jmath} = F\cos\theta\,\hat{\imath} + F\sin\theta\,\hat{\jmath}$$

**Attention to the reference frame:** when the angle is given relative to the vertical, cosine and sine swap roles: $F_x = F\sin\theta$, $F_y = F\cos\theta$.

</Definicao>

### Work

<Definicao titulo="Work as scalar product">

The **work** done by a constant force $\vec{F}$ over a displacement $\vec{d}$ is:

$$W = \vec{F} \cdot \vec{d} = F_x d_x + F_y d_y = Fd\cos\theta$$

Unit: **joule** (J = N·m). Direct consequences:
- $\theta = 0°$: force and displacement parallel — $W = Fd$ (maximum).
- $\theta = 90°$: perpendicular — $W = 0$ (normal force, pendulum tension, centripetal force).
- $\theta = 180°$: antiparallel — $W = -Fd$ (friction force, gravity when ascending).

</Definicao>

> "The work done by a force on the displacement of an object is equal to the component of the force in the direction of displacement times the magnitude of the displacement." — [OpenStax University Physics Volume 1, §7.1](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/7-1-work)

<Teorema titulo="Work-energy theorem">

For an object of mass $m$ under net force $\vec{F}_{\text{res}}$ over displacement $d$:

$$W_{\text{total}} = \Delta K = \frac{1}{2}mv_f^2 - \frac{1}{2}mv_i^2$$

**Corollary:** in motion at constant velocity, $W_{\text{total}} = 0$.
</Teorema>

### Velocity addition

<Definicao titulo="Galilean composition of velocities">

For an object with velocity $\vec{v}_{A/B}$ relative to reference frame $B$, and $B$ with velocity $\vec{v}_{B/C}$ relative to $C$:

$$\vec{v}_{A/C} = \vec{v}_{A/B} + \vec{v}_{B/C}$$

</Definicao>

> "In classical mechanics, velocities add vectorially. [...] The velocity vector of the passenger relative to the ground is the sum of the velocity vector of the passenger relative to the train and the velocity vector of the train relative to the ground." — [OpenStax University Physics Volume 1, §4.5](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/4-5-relative-motion-in-one-and-two-dimensions)

### Tension in three concurrent forces: Lami's theorem

Three forces $\vec{F}_1, \vec{F}_2, \vec{F}_3$ in equilibrium at a point with $\alpha$ = angle between $\vec{F}_2$ and $\vec{F}_3$, $\beta$ = angle between $\vec{F}_1$ and $\vec{F}_3$, $\gamma$ = angle between $\vec{F}_1$ and $\vec{F}_2$:

$$\frac{F_1}{\sin\alpha} = \frac{F_2}{\sin\beta} = \frac{F_3}{\sin\gamma}$$

Follows directly from the law of sines applied to the closed force triangle.

<Insight>

**Why does normal force never do work?** The support surface exerts normal force perpendicular to the object's displacement. Since $\cos 90° = 0$, the dot product is zero. This is a geometric consequence of the dot product — not a coincidence. Every force that, by definition, remains perpendicular to velocity (centripetal, normal, ideal pendulum tension) instantaneously does zero work.

</Insight>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

Imagine you tie a box with a rope and try to drag it across the floor.

If you pull the rope **straight forward**, the box moves easily. You spent energy in the right direction.

If you pull the rope **upward** (lifting), the box barely moves forward. You spent energy, but little of it went forward.

Vectors are like **arrows**: they show where the force goes AND how strong it is. When the arrow points in the direction the box is going, it's efficient. When it points sideways, it's a waste.

In equilibrium — when nothing falls and nothing breaks — all the arrows cancel: they sum to zero.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

Every force has a **direction** — that's why it's a vector.

When you push a shopping cart with the handle tilted downward, only the **horizontal** part of the push moves the cart forward. The **vertical** part just presses the ground.

Mathematically: a force of magnitude $F$ at angle $\theta$ from the ground has horizontal component $F\cos\theta$ and vertical component $F\sin\theta$.

**Work** is how much effort was useful: $W = F \times \text{distance} \times \cos\theta$. If $\theta = 0°$, $\cos 0° = 1$ — all effort is useful. If $\theta = 90°$, $\cos 90° = 0$ — zero work.

**Equilibrium**: an object at rest means the sum of all forces is zero. Two cables holding a chandelier: the tensions in the two cables, added vectorially, balance the weight.

**Boat crossing a river**: the boat's velocity points toward the opposite bank, the current's velocity runs parallel to the river. The resultant is the diagonal — the boat "drifts" downward while crossing.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old">

This lesson applies the dot product (Lesson 27) in real physical situations. Every physicist and engineer uses this toolset every day.

**Force decomposition.** Given force $\vec{F} = (F_x, F_y)$, calculate magnitude and angle: $F = \sqrt{F_x^2 + F_y^2}$ and $\theta = \arctan(F_y / F_x)$. The inverse: given $F$ and $\theta$, the components are $F_x = F\cos\theta$ and $F_y = F\sin\theta$.

**Work.** $W = \vec{F} \cdot \vec{d} = Fd\cos\theta$. Unit: joule (J = N·m). Parallel: $W = Fd$. Perpendicular: $W = 0$. Antiparallel: $W = -Fd$ (negative work — the force slows the object).

**Newton's second law separated by components:**
- $\sum F_x = m a_x$
- $\sum F_y = m a_y$

In equilibrium: $a_x = a_y = 0$, so $\sum F_x = 0$ and $\sum F_y = 0$.

**Inclined plane $\theta$.** Gravity $mg$ decomposed relative to the ramp:
- Parallel to ramp (causes sliding): $mg\sin\theta$
- Perpendicular to ramp (generates normal force): $mg\cos\theta$

Normal force: $N = mg\cos\theta$. Maximum static friction: $f_s = \mu_s N = \mu_s mg\cos\theta$. The block slides if $mg\sin\theta > \mu_s mg\cos\theta$, i.e., if $\tan\theta > \mu_s$.

**Connection to Lesson 27:** $W = \vec{F} \cdot \vec{d}$ is literally the dot product you studied yesterday.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

Vector mechanics is the core of Statics and Dynamics (Applied Mechanics I and II). This lesson covers the foundation of the first day of any engineering program.

### Particle equilibrium in 2D

System of $n$ coplanar concurrent forces at a point: 2 scalar equations.

$$\sum F_x = 0 \quad \Rightarrow \quad \sum_i F_i \cos\theta_i = 0$$
$$\sum F_y = 0 \quad \Rightarrow \quad \sum_i F_i \sin\theta_i = 0$$

For 2 unknowns (tensions in 2 cables): a $2 \times 2$ system — generic unique solution.

### Inclined plane with friction

Block of mass $m$ on ramp $\theta$, static friction coefficient $\mu_s$, kinetic $\mu_k$:

| Condition | Result |
|---|---|
| $\tan\theta < \mu_s$ | block at rest |
| $\tan\theta = \mu_s$ | threshold of slipping |
| $\tan\theta > \mu_s$ | block slides |

Acceleration when sliding: $a = g(\sin\theta - \mu_k\cos\theta)$.

### Work and energy

Work-energy theorem: $W_{\text{total}} = \Delta K = \frac{1}{2}m v_f^2 - \frac{1}{2}m v_i^2$. Applies without calculating intermediate forces — useful when only initial and final states matter.

Power: $P = \vec{F} \cdot \vec{v} = Fv\cos\theta$ (watt, W = J/s).

### Velocity addition — navigation

Boat that must reach the directly opposite bank in a river with current $v_c$: must point the bow $\phi$ upstream, with $\sin\phi = v_c / v_b$. Effective crossing velocity: $v_{\perp} = \sqrt{v_b^2 - v_c^2}$.

### Lami's theorem — three forces in equilibrium

For a node with three cables (bridge, truss, mast):

$$\frac{T_1}{\sin\alpha_1} = \frac{T_2}{\sin\alpha_2} = \frac{W}{\sin\alpha_3}$$

where $\alpha_i$ is the angle between the **other two** forces (angle opposite to $T_i$). Follows from the law of sines.

### Connection to structures

Truss analysis: equilibrium at each node. Determinate planar truss: $m = 2n - 3$ (Maxwell's rule), where $m$ is the number of bars and $n$ is the number of nodes.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

What is presented here as "physics with vectors" is the Euclidean version of a far more general structure.

### Covariant formulation

In Minkowski spacetime (special relativity), force is replaced by four-force $f^\mu = dp^\mu / d\tau$. Work generalizes to the Lorentz scalar $f^\mu u_\mu$. Classical mechanics is the limit $v \ll c$.

### Lagrangian and Hamiltonian mechanics

The Lagrangian $\mathcal{L} = T - V$ produces equations of motion invariant under diffeomorphisms. Newton on a ramp requires decomposing forces; Lagrange treats the ramp as a change of generalized variable — without decomposing any force.

**Example:** block on inclined ramp $\theta$. Generalized coordinate $q = $ position along the ramp. $T = \frac{1}{2}m\dot{q}^2$, $V = -mgq\sin\theta$. Euler-Lagrange immediately gives $m\ddot{q} = mg\sin\theta$ — without a free-body diagram.

### Control and robotics

In robotics, "work" generalizes to generalized power: $P = \boldsymbol{\tau}^T \dot{\boldsymbol{q}}$ where $\boldsymbol{\tau}$ is the vector of torques and $\dot{\boldsymbol{q}}$ are generalized joint velocities. Force decomposition into components is replaced by the Jacobian $\boldsymbol{J}(\boldsymbol{q})$ that maps joint velocities to Cartesian velocities: $\dot{\boldsymbol{x}} = \boldsymbol{J}\dot{\boldsymbol{q}}$.

### Gravitational wave detection

The LIGO interferometer uses vector decomposition of stress tensors to detect perturbations $h_{\mu\nu}$ of spacetime. The sensitivity of $10^{-21}$ m/m is obtained by subtracting the measurements in the two perpendicular arms — exactly the idea of orthogonal components. [2017 Nobel Prize in Physics](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/2017/) — Weiss, Barish and Thorne.

### Finance

Dot product $\vec{F} \cdot \vec{d}$ corresponds in finance to **portfolio value**: $V = \boldsymbol{p}^T \boldsymbol{q}$ where $\boldsymbol{p}$ is the price vector and $\boldsymbol{q}$ is the quantity vector. The "work" of the market is incremental PnL: $dV = \boldsymbol{p}^T d\boldsymbol{q} + \boldsymbol{q}^T d\boldsymbol{p}$. Delta hedging uses projections analogous to force decomposition.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practice — algorithm for any mechanics problem">

**Universal algorithm for vector mechanics:**

1. **Free-body diagram (FBD).** Isolate the object. Draw each force with an arrow indicating direction and point of application.
2. **Choose convenient axes.** Align one axis with the main direction of motion or with the surface (ramp). This minimizes the number of non-zero components.
3. **Decompose each force** into the two axes. Pay attention to sign: positive or negative according to the chosen direction.
4. **Set up the equations.** For each axis: $\sum F_k = m a_k$. In equilibrium: $\sum F_k = 0$.
5. **Solve and verify.** Check dimensions (N·m = J), signs, order of magnitude.

### Quick numerical example: pulled cart

Cart of 20 kg pulled by a rope at $25°$ above the horizontal, force $F = 80$ N, smooth surface.

Axis $x$: $F\cos 25° = m a_x$

$$a_x = \frac{80\cos 25°}{20} = \frac{80 \times 0.906}{20} \approx 3.62\ \text{m/s}^2$$

Axis $y$: $N + F\sin 25° - mg = 0 \implies N = mg - F\sin 25° = 196 - 33.8 \approx 162\ \text{N}$.

### Common pitfalls

- **Wrong angle**: in $W = Fd\cos\theta$, the $\theta$ is the angle between force and displacement — not between force and $x$-axis.
- **Normal is not $mg$**: on inclined plane, $N = mg\cos\theta < mg$.
- **Sign of friction**: friction opposes **motion** (or tendency of motion), not the positive axis.
- **Work of normal force**: always zero on flat surface (perpendicular to displacement).

</Porta>

## Worked examples

<Exemplo titulo="Force decomposition and work — sled" numero="1">

**Problem:** A child pulls a sled with a rope that makes angle $\theta = 35°$ with the ground. The force in the rope is $F = 60$ N. The sled moves $d = 15$ m horizontally. (a) Calculate the horizontal and vertical components of the force. (b) Calculate the work done by the rope.

**Strategy:** Decompose the force into components and apply $W = F_x \cdot d$ (horizontal component times horizontal displacement) or equivalently $W = Fd\cos\theta$.

**Solution:**

(a) Components:
$$F_x = F\cos 35° = 60 \times 0.8192 \approx 49.2\ \text{N}$$
$$F_y = F\sin 35° = 60 \times 0.5736 \approx 34.4\ \text{N}$$

(b) Work:
$$W = F_x \cdot d = 49.2 \times 15 \approx 738\ \text{J}$$

Or directly: $W = Fd\cos\theta = 60 \times 15 \times \cos 35° = 900 \times 0.8192 \approx 737\ \text{J}$.

**Verification:** Only the horizontal component ($49.2$ N) does work on the horizontal displacement. The vertical component ($34.4$ N) is perpendicular to the displacement — zero contribution. The two calculations agree within rounding.

**Source.** [OpenStax University Physics Volume 1, §7.2, p. 306](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/7-2-kinetic-energy) — CC-BY 4.0.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Particle equilibrium — two cables supporting a chandelier" numero="2">

**Problem:** A chandelier of 120 N is suspended by two cables. The left cable makes angle $\alpha = 40°$ with the ceiling (horizontal) and the right makes $\beta = 55°$. Determine the tensions $T_1$ and $T_2$.

**Strategy:** Static equilibrium: $\sum F_x = 0$ and $\sum F_y = 0$. Positive $y$-axis upward.

**Solution:**

Components:
- $T_1$: horizontal $-T_1\cos 40°$ (to the left), vertical $+T_1\sin 40°$ (upward).
- $T_2$: horizontal $+T_2\cos 55°$ (to the right), vertical $+T_2\sin 55°$ (upward).
- Weight: vertical $-120$ N.

$$\text{Axis } x: \quad -T_1\cos 40° + T_2\cos 55° = 0$$
$$\text{Axis } y: \quad T_1\sin 40° + T_2\sin 55° = 120$$

From the first equation: $T_1 = T_2 \dfrac{\cos 55°}{\cos 40°} = T_2 \times \dfrac{0.5736}{0.7660} \approx 0.749\,T_2$.

Substituting into the second:
$$0.749\,T_2 \times 0.6428 + T_2 \times 0.8192 = 120$$
$$0.481\,T_2 + 0.819\,T_2 = 120 \implies 1.300\,T_2 = 120 \implies T_2 \approx 92.3\ \text{N}$$

Therefore $T_1 \approx 0.749 \times 92.3 \approx 69.1\ \text{N}$.

**Verification:** $T_1\sin 40° + T_2\sin 55° = 69.1 \times 0.643 + 92.3 \times 0.819 \approx 44.4 + 75.6 = 120\ \text{N}$. Checks out.

**Source.** [OpenStax University Physics Volume 1, §5.4, p. 218](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/5-4-mass-and-weight) — CC-BY 4.0.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Work and work-energy theorem on an inclined plane" numero="3">

**Problem:** A block of 5 kg starts from rest and slides 8 m down a smooth ramp inclined at $30°$. Determine: (a) the work done by gravity, (b) the work done by the normal force, (c) the velocity at the end.

**Strategy:** Identify each force, calculate its work, apply the theorem $W_{\text{total}} = \Delta K$.

**Solution:**

(a) The weight $P = mg = 5 \times 9.8 = 49$ N points vertically downward. The vertical component of displacement is $d\sin 30° = 8 \times 0.5 = 4$ m (downward). Work done by gravity:

$$W_g = P \times d\sin 30° = 49 \times 4 = 196\ \text{J}$$

(b) Normal force perpendicular to displacement: $W_N = 0$.

(c) By the work-energy theorem ($v_i = 0$):
$$196 = \frac{1}{2} \times 5 \times v_f^2 \implies v_f^2 = 78.4\ \text{m}^2/\text{s}^2 \implies v_f \approx 8.85\ \text{m/s}$$

**Verification:** By kinematics: $a = g\sin 30° = 4.9$ m/s². Then $v_f^2 = 2ad = 2 \times 4.9 \times 8 = 78.4\ \text{m}^2/\text{s}^2$. Checks out.

**Source.** [OpenStax University Physics Volume 1, §7.3, p. 313](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/7-3-work-energy-theorem) — CC-BY 4.0.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Velocity addition — boat crossing a river" numero="4">

**Problem:** A boat can reach $6.0$ km/h in still water. The river has a current of $4.0$ km/h to the east. The boat points north. (a) What is the resultant velocity (magnitude and direction)? (b) If the river is 300 m wide, how much time does it take and where does it land?

**Strategy:** Add velocity vectors. The crossing time depends only on the component perpendicular to the bank.

**Solution:**

(a) $\vec{v}_b = (0,\ 6.0)$ km/h (north), $\vec{v}_c = (4.0,\ 0)$ km/h (east).

$$\vec{v}_{\text{res}} = (4.0,\ 6.0)\ \text{km/h}$$

Magnitude: $v_{\text{res}} = \sqrt{4.0^2 + 6.0^2} = \sqrt{52} \approx 7.21$ km/h.

Direction: $\theta = \arctan(6.0 / 4.0) = \arctan(1.5) \approx 56.3°$ north of east.

(b) Time to cross 300 m = 0.300 km at 6.0 km/h (north component):
$$t = \frac{0.300}{6.0} = 0.050\ \text{h} = 3.0\ \text{min}$$

Drift to the east: $\Delta x = 4.0 \times 0.050 = 0.200\ \text{km} = 200\ \text{m}$.

**Verification:** $7.21 \times \cos(56.3°) \approx 7.21 \times 0.555 \approx 4.0$ km/h (east component). Checks out.

**Source.** [OpenStax University Physics Volume 1, §4.5, p. 183](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/4-5-relative-motion-in-one-and-two-dimensions) — CC-BY 4.0.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Cable tension with Lami's theorem" numero="5">

**Problem:** Weight $W = 500$ N suspended by two cables. Cable 1 makes $30°$ with the horizontal (to the left), cable 2 makes $60°$ (to the right). Determine $T_1$ and $T_2$ by the equilibrium method and verify with Lami's theorem.

**Strategy:** Method 1: $\sum F_x = 0$ and $\sum F_y = 0$. Method 2 (verification): Lami's theorem with the angles between vectors.

**Solution — Method 1:**

$$\sum F_x = 0: \quad -T_1\cos 30° + T_2\cos 60° = 0 \implies T_1\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} = T_2\frac{1}{2} \implies T_1 = \frac{T_2}{\sqrt{3}}$$

$$\sum F_y = 0: \quad T_1\sin 30° + T_2\sin 60° = 500$$

Substituting: $\dfrac{T_2}{\sqrt{3}} \times \dfrac{1}{2} + T_2 \times \dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2} = 500 \implies T_2 \times \dfrac{1 + 3}{2\sqrt{3}} = 500 \implies T_2 = \dfrac{500\sqrt{3}}{2} \approx 433\ \text{N}$

$$T_1 = \frac{433}{\sqrt{3}} \approx 250\ \text{N}$$

**Verification — Lami's theorem:** Angle between $\vec{T}_1$ and $\vec{W}$: $90° + 30° = 120°$. Angle between $\vec{T}_2$ and $\vec{W}$: $90° + 60° = 150°$. Angle between $\vec{T}_1$ and $\vec{T}_2$: $180° - 30° - 60° = 90°$.

$$\frac{T_1}{\sin 150°} = \frac{T_2}{\sin 120°} = \frac{500}{\sin 90°} = 500$$

$T_1 = 500 \times 0.5 = 250$ N, $T_2 = 500 \times 0.866 \approx 433$ N. Both methods agree.

</Exemplo>
