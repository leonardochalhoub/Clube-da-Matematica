### Vectors in $\mathbb{R}^2$

<Definicao titulo="Vector in the plane">

A **vector** $\vec{v} \in \mathbb{R}^2$ is an ordered pair $(v_x, v_y)$ with $v_x, v_y \in \mathbb{R}$. Geometrically it represents a directed displacement: it has magnitude (norm), direction, and sense. Two vectors are equal if and only if they have the same components — regardless of the point of application.

</Definicao>

> "A vector is a tuple of ordered real numbers. Two vectors are equal when their components are equal, component by component." — [Hefferon, Linear Algebra, ch. 1 §I.1](https://hefferon.net/linearalgebra/)

### Magnitude (Euclidean norm)

<Equation explicacao="Magnitude of a vector: Pythagorean theorem applied to components. Always yields a non-negative number." latex="|\vec{v}| = \sqrt{v_x^2 + v_y^2}" />

### Unit vectors and canonical basis

<Definicao titulo="Unit vector and canonical basis">

The **unit vector** (normalized vector) in the direction of $\vec{v} \neq \vec{0}$ is $\hat{v} = \dfrac{\vec{v}}{|\vec{v}|}$, with $|\hat{v}| = 1$.

The **canonical vectors** $\hat{\imath} = (1, 0)$ and $\hat{\jmath} = (0, 1)$ form the **canonical basis** of $\mathbb{R}^2$. Every vector can be written as:

$$\vec{v} = v_x\,\hat{\imath} + v_y\,\hat{\jmath}$$

</Definicao>

### Polar form

For $r = |\vec{v}|$ and $\theta$ the angle with the positive $x$ axis:

<Equation explicacao="Polar to Cartesian conversion. For the reverse path, use r = |v| and θ = atan2(vy, vx) — never simple arctan, which loses the quadrant." latex="\vec{v} = (r\cos\theta,\; r\sin\theta)" />

### Dot product

<Definicao titulo="Dot product (dot product)">

The **dot product** of $\vec{u} = (u_x, u_y)$ and $\vec{v} = (v_x, v_y)$ is the real number:

<Equation explicacao="Algebraic definition: sum of the products of corresponding components." latex="\vec{u} \cdot \vec{v} = u_x v_x + u_y v_y" />

Equivalently, if $\theta$ is the angle between the two vectors:

<Equation explicacao="Geometric definition: relates the dot product to the angle between the vectors." latex="\vec{u} \cdot \vec{v} = |\vec{u}|\,|\vec{v}|\cos\theta" />

</Definicao>

> "The inner product of two vectors $\vec{u}$ and $\vec{v}$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$ is the scalar $\vec{u} \cdot \vec{v} = \sum_{i=1}^n u_i v_i$." — [Beezer, A First Course in Linear Algebra, §VO](http://linear.pugetsound.edu/)

<Teorema titulo="Properties of the dot product">

For $\vec{u}, \vec{v}, \vec{w} \in \mathbb{R}^2$ and $\alpha \in \mathbb{R}$:
- **Commutativity**: $\vec{u} \cdot \vec{v} = \vec{v} \cdot \vec{u}$
- **Distributivity**: $\vec{u} \cdot (\vec{v} + \vec{w}) = \vec{u} \cdot \vec{v} + \vec{u} \cdot \vec{w}$
- **Homogeneity**: $(\alpha\vec{u}) \cdot \vec{v} = \alpha(\vec{u} \cdot \vec{v})$
- **Norm**: $\vec{v} \cdot \vec{v} = |\vec{v}|^2 \geq 0$, with equality $\iff \vec{v} = \vec{0}$

</Teorema>

### Angle between vectors and orthogonality

<Equation explicacao="Angle between two non-zero vectors: isolate cos θ from the geometric definition of the dot product." latex="\cos\theta = \frac{\vec{u} \cdot \vec{v}}{|\vec{u}|\,|\vec{v}|}, \quad \theta \in [0°, 180°]" />

<Definicao titulo="Orthogonality">

$\vec{u}$ and $\vec{v}$ are **orthogonal** (perpendicular) if and only if $\vec{u} \cdot \vec{v} = 0$.

</Definicao>

### Orthogonal projection

<Definicao titulo="Projection of u onto v">

The **scalar projection** of $\vec{u}$ onto $\vec{v}$ is the number:

$$\text{comp}_{\vec{v}}\vec{u} = \frac{\vec{u} \cdot \vec{v}}{|\vec{v}|}$$

The **vector projection** of $\vec{u}$ onto $\vec{v}$ is the vector:

<Equation explicacao="Vector projection: the vector parallel to v which, subtracted from u, leaves a residue perpendicular to v." latex="\text{proj}_{\vec{v}}\vec{u} = \frac{\vec{u} \cdot \vec{v}}{|\vec{v}|^2}\,\vec{v}" />

</Definicao>

<Insight>

**Cauchy–Schwarz inequality.** For all $\vec{u}, \vec{v} \in \mathbb{R}^2$:

$$|\vec{u} \cdot \vec{v}| \leq |\vec{u}|\,|\vec{v}|$$

Equality holds if and only if $\vec{u}$ and $\vec{v}$ are parallel. The cosine formula ($\cos\theta \in [-1,1]$) is a direct consequence of this inequality. Proof in Exercise 26.40.

</Insight>

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

Imagine an **arrow** on paper. It has a size and points to one side. That is a **vector**.

If you want to add two arrows, put the second arrow at the tip of the first. The arrow that goes from the beginning to the very end is the result.

The **dot product** is a calculation that says how much two arrows are "pointing in the same direction". If they both point the same way, the result is positive. If they point opposite ways, it is negative. If they are at right angles, the result is zero — like the corner of a piece of paper.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

A vector is an **arrow** on the plane. The arrow $(3, 4)$ goes 3 to the right and 4 up.

**Magnitude (size)**: use Pythagoras. $\sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = \sqrt{9 + 16} = 5$.

**Sum of vectors**: add component by component. $(2, 1) + (3, 4) = (5, 5)$.

**Multiplication by a number**: $3 \cdot (2, 1) = (6, 3)$ — stretches the arrow 3 times.

**Dot product**: two vectors, one calculation, one number. $(2, 3) \cdot (4, 1) = 2 \times 4 + 3 \times 1 = 11$.

Where it appears:
- **Wind**: velocity has direction, it is a vector.
- **Pushing a cart**: force has direction.
- **GPS**: "go 3 km north and 4 km east" is a vector.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old teenager">

A vector in the plane has three properties:
- **Magnitude** (length of the arrow): $|\vec{v}| = \sqrt{v_x^2 + v_y^2}$
- **Direction** (angle with the $x$ axis): $\theta = \text{atan2}(v_y, v_x)$
- **Sense** (which tip of the arrow)

**Basic operations:**

| Operation | Formula | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Sum | $(u_x + v_x,\; u_y + v_y)$ | vector |
| Difference | $(u_x - v_x,\; u_y - v_y)$ | vector |
| Scalar | $(\alpha v_x,\; \alpha v_y)$ | vector |
| Dot product | $u_x v_x + u_y v_y$ | number |

**Dot product** is the most important operation in this lesson. It measures the "alignment" between two vectors:
- If $\vec{u} \cdot \vec{v} > 0$: acute angle (less than 90°)
- If $\vec{u} \cdot \vec{v} = 0$: perpendicular
- If $\vec{u} \cdot \vec{v} < 0$: obtuse angle (more than 90°)

**Practical example:** You push a crate with force $\vec{F} = (6, 0)$ N and it displaces $\vec{d} = (3, 2)$ m. The work done is $W = \vec{F} \cdot \vec{d} = 6 \times 3 + 0 \times 2 = 18$ joules. The vertical component of force does no work — the dot product captures exactly that.

<Insight>

**Why is the dot product a number, not a vector?** Because it measures a *quantity* — how much two vectors "overlap" in direction. The result is scalar (no direction). This distinguishes it from the cross product (Trimester 12), which produces a vector perpendicular to the plane. In physics, work and power are dot products; angular momentum is a cross product.

</Insight>

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

Vectors and the dot product are the foundation of all applied vector analysis. Master these topics before Trimester 12.

### Cross-domain applications

- **Classical mechanics**: work $W = \vec{F} \cdot \vec{d}$; power $P = \vec{F} \cdot \vec{v}$. The dot product extracts the useful component of the force.
- **Statics**: equilibrium $\sum \vec{F} = \vec{0}$; decomposition on inclined plane via orthogonal projection.
- **Electromagnetism**: electric flux $\Phi_E = \vec{E} \cdot \vec{A}$; Gauss's law.
- **Computer graphics**: illumination by Lambertian model uses $\cos\theta = \hat{n} \cdot \hat{l}$ (surface normal · light direction).
- **Machine Learning**: cosine similarity $\dfrac{\vec{u} \cdot \vec{v}}{|\vec{u}||\vec{v}|}$ measures proximity between embeddings.
- **Quantitative finance**: portfolio return $r_p = \vec{w} \cdot \vec{r}$ (weights · asset returns).

### Orthogonal decomposition

Every vector $\vec{u}$ decomposes **uniquely** into components parallel and perpendicular to $\vec{v}$:

$$\vec{u} = \underbrace{\frac{\vec{u} \cdot \vec{v}}{|\vec{v}|^2}\vec{v}}_{\text{parallel to }\vec{v}} + \underbrace{\vec{u} - \frac{\vec{u} \cdot \vec{v}}{|\vec{v}|^2}\vec{v}}_{\text{perpendicular to }\vec{v}}$$

This decomposition is the basis of the Gram–Schmidt process (Linear Algebra, Trimester 12).

### Matrix interpretation

The dot product is $\vec{u}^T \vec{v}$ in column-vector notation — a preview of the inner product in Hilbert spaces (square-integrable functions $L^2$, quantum mechanics).

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

$\mathbb{R}^2$ with the Euclidean inner product is the real Hilbert space of dimension 2 — the simplest, but structurally identical to $L^2(\Omega)$ (square-integrable functions), which underpins all functional analysis.

### Abstract inner product

An **inner product** on a real vector space $V$ is a map $\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle: V \times V \to \mathbb{R}$ satisfying:
1. **Symmetry**: $\langle u, v \rangle = \langle v, u \rangle$
2. **Linearity in the first argument**: $\langle \alpha u + \beta w, v \rangle = \alpha\langle u,v\rangle + \beta\langle w,v\rangle$
3. **Positive definite**: $\langle v, v \rangle \geq 0$, equal to zero $\iff v = 0$

The Euclidean dot product is the case $V = \mathbb{R}^n$.

### Applications in data science and quant

- **Recommendations**: cosine similarity between user and item vectors is the backbone of collaborative filtering systems (Netflix Prize, 2009).
- **NLP**: word2vec, GloVe, and BERT map tokens into $\mathbb{R}^{300}$ to $\mathbb{R}^{4096}$. Semantic analogies are operations in the vector space.
- **Portfolio**: covariance $\text{Cov}(\vec{r}_i, \vec{r}_j) = \mathbb{E}[\vec{r}_i \cdot \vec{r}_j] - \mathbb{E}[\vec{r}_i]\cdot\mathbb{E}[\vec{r}_j]$ is expected inner product. Minimum variance via projection onto a subspace.
- **Optimal control**: quadratic cost function $J = \vec{x}^T Q \vec{x} + \vec{u}^T R \vec{u}$ uses inner products with weight matrices $Q, R$ positive definite.

### Orthogonality in high dimensions

In high dimensions (concentration of measure phenomenon), two random vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$ are nearly orthogonal with high probability when $n \to \infty$. This fact explains why random bases are nearly orthogonal and is exploited in compressive sensing and transformer learning.

### Cross product (preview — Trimester 12)

In $\mathbb{R}^3$, the cross product $\vec{u} \times \vec{v}$ produces a vector perpendicular to the plane of $\vec{u}$ and $\vec{v}$ with magnitude $|\vec{u}||\vec{v}|\sin\theta$. In $\mathbb{R}^2$, the "signed area" of the parallelogram $\vec{u} \wedge \vec{v} = u_x v_y - u_y v_x$ is the 2D analogue.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practical application">

### Recipe for working with vectors

1. **Write each vector as a pair** $(v_x, v_y)$ — clarity before calculation.
2. **Sum/difference**: component by component.
3. **Magnitude**: $\sqrt{v_x^2 + v_y^2}$ — Pythagoras.
4. **Unit vector**: divide each component by the magnitude.
5. **Dot product**: $u_x v_x + u_y v_y$ — a number.
6. **Angle**: $\theta = \arccos\!\left(\dfrac{\vec{u}\cdot\vec{v}}{|\vec{u}||\vec{v}|}\right)$.
7. **Projection of $\vec{u}$ onto $\vec{v}$**: $\dfrac{\vec{u}\cdot\vec{v}}{|\vec{v}|^2}\,\vec{v}$.

### Example: Work in physics

Force $\vec{F} = (4, 3)$ N, displacement $\vec{d} = (6, 2)$ m.

Work: $W = \vec{F} \cdot \vec{d} = 4 \times 6 + 3 \times 2 = 24 + 6 = 30$ J.

Angle between $\vec{F}$ and $\vec{d}$:
- $|\vec{F}| = \sqrt{16 + 9} = 5$ N
- $|\vec{d}| = \sqrt{36 + 4} = \sqrt{40} \approx 6.32$ m
- $\cos\theta = 30 / (5 \times 6.32) \approx 0.949$, so $\theta \approx 18°$

### Example: Portfolio return

An investor allocates weights $\vec{w} = (0.6;\; 0.4)$ between two assets with monthly returns $\vec{r} = (0.02;\; 0.05)$.

Portfolio return: $r_p = \vec{w} \cdot \vec{r} = 0.6 \times 0.02 + 0.4 \times 0.05 = 0.012 + 0.020 = 0.032$, that is, 3.2% per month.

### Common pitfalls

- **atan vs atan2**: for the angle of a vector with the $x$ axis, always use `atan2(vy, vx)` — the `atan(vy/vx)` function loses the quadrant.
- **Zero vector**: magnitude 0, direction undefined. Normalizing the zero vector produces division by zero.
- **Dot product ≠ cross product**: the scalar produces a number; the cross product (3D) produces a vector. Do not confuse.
- **Heterogeneous units**: $\vec{u} = (3\,\text{m}, 4\,\text{s})$ has no meaningful magnitude. Vectors with distinct units in components do not form a Euclidean space.

</Porta>

## Worked examples

<Exemplo titulo="Magnitude and unit vector of a vector" numero="1">

**Problem:** Given $\vec{v} = (5, -12)$, compute: (a) the magnitude $|\vec{v}|$; (b) the unit vector $\hat{v}$; (c) the angle that $\vec{v}$ makes with the positive $x$ axis (in degrees).

**Strategy:** Apply the magnitude formula, divide by magnitude to get the unit vector, and use arctangent for the angle — remembering to check the quadrant.

**Solution:**

**(a) Magnitude:**

$$|\vec{v}| = \sqrt{5^2 + (-12)^2} = \sqrt{25 + 144} = \sqrt{169} = 13$$

**(b) Unit vector:**

$$\hat{v} = \frac{\vec{v}}{|\vec{v}|} = \frac{(5, -12)}{13} = \left(\frac{5}{13},\; -\frac{12}{13}\right)$$

**(c) Angle:**

$$\theta = \text{atan2}(-12, 5) \approx -67.4°$$

The vector is in the 4th quadrant (positive $x$ component, negative $y$ component). The equivalent positive angle is $360° - 67.4° = 292.6°$.

**Verification:** $|\hat{v}| = \sqrt{(5/13)^2 + (12/13)^2} = \sqrt{25 + 144}/13 = 13/13 = 1$. Correct.

**Source.** [OpenStax Algebra and Trigonometry 2e — §8.8](https://openstax.org/books/algebra-and-trigonometry-2e/pages/8-8-vectors) — CC-BY 4.0 license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Addition of vectors and parallelogram law" numero="2">

**Problem:** A drone receives two simultaneous impulses: $\vec{F}_1 = (6, 0)$ N (horizontal, to the right) and $\vec{F}_2 = (0, 8)$ N (vertical, upward). Compute the resultant force, its magnitude, and the angle it makes with the horizontal.

**Strategy:** Add component by component; compute magnitude; compute angle.

**Solution:**

**Resultant force:**

$$\vec{R} = \vec{F}_1 + \vec{F}_2 = (6 + 0,\; 0 + 8) = (6, 8)\;\text{N}$$

**Magnitude:**

$$|\vec{R}| = \sqrt{6^2 + 8^2} = \sqrt{36 + 64} = \sqrt{100} = 10\;\text{N}$$

**Angle with the horizontal:**

$$\theta = \arctan\!\left(\frac{8}{6}\right) = \arctan(1.3\overline{3}) \approx 53.1°$$

Geometrically, the resultant force forms the diagonal of the rectangle (parallelogram with a right angle) defined by $\vec{F}_1$ and $\vec{F}_2$.

**Verification:** $|\vec{F}_1| = 6$, $|\vec{F}_2| = 8$, $|\vec{R}| = 10$ — Pythagorean triple $(6, 8, 10)$. $\sin(53.1°) \approx 8/10 = 0.8$. Consistent.

**Source.** [OpenStax Calculus Volume 3 — §2.1](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-3/pages/2-1-vectors-in-the-plane) — CC-BY 4.0 license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Dot product and angle between vectors" numero="3">

**Problem:** Given $\vec{u} = (3, 4)$ and $\vec{v} = (5, -12)$, compute: (a) $\vec{u} \cdot \vec{v}$; (b) the angle $\theta$ between the two vectors (in degrees).

**Strategy:** Apply the algebraic definition of the dot product; obtain the angle using the geometric definition.

**Solution:**

**(a) Dot product:**

$$\vec{u} \cdot \vec{v} = 3 \times 5 + 4 \times (-12) = 15 - 48 = -33$$

**(b) Angle:**

We compute the magnitudes:
$$|\vec{u}| = \sqrt{9 + 16} = 5, \qquad |\vec{v}| = \sqrt{25 + 144} = 13$$

Then:
$$\cos\theta = \frac{\vec{u} \cdot \vec{v}}{|\vec{u}|\,|\vec{v}|} = \frac{-33}{5 \times 13} = \frac{-33}{65} \approx -0.508$$

$$\theta = \arccos(-0.508) \approx 120.5°$$

The negative dot product confirms that the angle between the vectors is obtuse (greater than 90°).

**Source.** [Stitz–Zeager Precalculus — §11.9](https://stitz-zeager.com/szprecalc07042013.pdf) — CC-BY-NC-SA license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Orthogonality — parameter determination" numero="4">

**Problem:** Determine the value of $k$ such that the vectors $\vec{u} = (k, 3)$ and $\vec{v} = (2, -6)$ are orthogonal. Then verify that $\vec{u} \cdot \vec{v} = 0$ with the $k$ found.

**Strategy:** The condition for orthogonality is $\vec{u} \cdot \vec{v} = 0$. Set up the equation and solve for $k$.

**Solution:**

**Orthogonality condition:**

$$\vec{u} \cdot \vec{v} = 0 \implies k \times 2 + 3 \times (-6) = 0$$

$$2k - 18 = 0 \implies k = 9$$

**Verification** with $k = 9$: $\vec{u} = (9, 3)$, $\vec{v} = (2, -6)$.

$$\vec{u} \cdot \vec{v} = 9 \times 2 + 3 \times (-6) = 18 - 18 = 0 \checkmark$$

The vectors are orthogonal. Note that $\vec{u} = (9, 3) = 3(3, 1)$ and $\vec{v} = 2(1, -3)$ — the direction $(3, 1)$ is perpendicular to $(1, -3)$, which can be verified geometrically.

**Source.** [OpenStax Algebra and Trigonometry 2e — §8.9](https://openstax.org/books/algebra-and-trigonometry-2e/pages/8-9-the-dot-product) — CC-BY 4.0 license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Orthogonal projection — force on inclined plane" numero="5">

**Problem:** An object sits on an inclined plane at 30° to the horizontal. The weight of the object is $\vec{W} = (0, -mg)$ with $mg = 50$ N. Compute the component of weight parallel to the plane (causing sliding) and the component perpendicular to the plane (compressing the surface).

**Strategy:** The unit vector parallel to the inclined plane (pointing down the slope) is $\hat{t} = (\cos 30°, -\sin 30°)$. Project $\vec{W}$ onto $\hat{t}$ to get the tangential component; the remainder is the normal component.

**Solution:**

**Unit vector parallel to the plane (down the slope):**

$$\hat{t} = (\cos 30°, -\sin 30°) = \left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2},\; -\frac{1}{2}\right)$$

**Tangential component** (parallel to plane):

$$\text{comp}_{\hat{t}}\vec{W} = \vec{W} \cdot \hat{t} = (0)\!\cdot\!\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} + (-50)\!\cdot\!\!\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right) = 25\;\text{N}$$

The weight has a component of **25 N along the plane**, pulling the object down the slope.

**Normal component** (perpendicular to plane):

$$W_\perp = \sqrt{|\vec{W}|^2 - W_t^2} = \sqrt{50^2 - 25^2} = \sqrt{2500 - 625} = \sqrt{1875} = 25\sqrt{3} \approx 43.3\;\text{N}$$

**Verification:** $W_t = mg\sin 30° = 50 \times 0.5 = 25$ N. $W_\perp = mg\cos 30° = 50 \times (\sqrt{3}/2) = 25\sqrt{3}$ N. Consistent with physics formulas.

**Source.** [OpenStax Calculus Volume 3 — §2.3](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-3/pages/2-3-the-dot-product) — CC-BY 4.0 license.
</Exemplo>
