### Dot product in $\mathbb{R}^2$

<Definicao titulo="Dot product">
For $\vec u = (u_1, u_2)$ and $\vec v = (v_1, v_2)$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$:

$$\vec u \cdot \vec v = u_1 v_1 + u_2 v_2$$

Equivalent geometric form (valid in any dimension):

$$\vec u \cdot \vec v = |\vec u|\,|\vec v|\,\cos\theta$$

where $\theta \in [0, \pi]$ is the angle between the vectors.
</Definicao>

> "If $\vec u$ and $\vec v$ are vectors with components $\langle u_1, u_2\rangle$ and $\langle v_1, v_2\rangle$, respectively, then the dot product $\vec u \cdot \vec v$ is given by $\vec u \cdot \vec v = u_1 v_1 + u_2 v_2$." — [Stitz–Zeager Precalculus §11.8](https://www.stitz-zeager.com/szprecalculus07042013.pdf)

| $\vec u \cdot \vec v$ | $\cos\theta$ | Angle |
|---|---|---|
| $> 0$ | $> 0$ | acute, $\theta < 90°$ |
| $= 0$ | $0$ | right, $\theta = 90°$ — orthogonal |
| $< 0$ | $< 0$ | obtuse, $\theta > 90°$ |

### Algebraic properties

- **Commutative**: $\vec u \cdot \vec v = \vec v \cdot \vec u$
- **Distributive**: $\vec u \cdot (\vec v + \vec w) = \vec u \cdot \vec v + \vec u \cdot \vec w$
- **Homogeneity**: $(\alpha\,\vec u) \cdot \vec v = \alpha\,(\vec u \cdot \vec v)$
- **Positive**: $\vec u \cdot \vec u = |\vec u|^2 \geq 0$, with equality $\iff \vec u = \vec 0$

<Teorema titulo="Orthogonality criterion">
For $\vec u, \vec v \neq \vec 0$:
$$\vec u \perp \vec v \iff \vec u \cdot \vec v = 0$$
</Teorema>

### Angle between vectors

From the geometric definition, isolating $\theta$:

<Equation numero="1"
  explicacao="Angle formula: divide the dot product by the product of the magnitudes, apply arccosine. Valid only when both vectors are nonzero."
  latex="\cos\theta = \frac{\vec u \cdot \vec v}{|\vec u|\,|\vec v|}, \quad \theta = \arccos\!\left(\frac{\vec u \cdot \vec v}{|\vec u|\,|\vec v|}\right)" />

### Orthogonal projection

<Definicao titulo="Vector projection of u onto v">

$$\text{proj}_{\vec v}\,\vec u = \frac{\vec u \cdot \vec v}{|\vec v|^2}\,\vec v$$

The scalar magnitude of the projection (signed length) is $\dfrac{\vec u \cdot \vec v}{|\vec v|}$.
</Definicao>

> "If $\vec v \neq \vec 0$, the projection of $\vec u$ onto $\vec v$... is the vector $\text{proj}_{\vec v}(\vec u) = \dfrac{\vec u \cdot \vec v}{\vec v \cdot \vec v}\,\vec v$." — [Stitz–Zeager Precalculus §11.8](https://www.stitz-zeager.com/szprecalculus07042013.pdf)

<Insight>
Orthogonality is the heart of modern mathematics. Orthogonal decomposition (every vector = component parallel + component perpendicular to $\vec v$) is the principle behind Gram–Schmidt, least squares, the Fourier transform, and the attention mechanism of language Transformers — all of them extract "how much" a vector goes in each direction of a basis.
</Insight>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

Imagine two **little straws** lying on the table, starting from the same point but pointing in different directions.

When the two straws point **in the same direction**, they "help each other" a lot. The dot product is **large**.

When they form a **cross** (one forward, the other up), they don't help each other at all. The dot product is **zero**.

When they point in **opposite directions**, one "gets in the way" of the other. The dot product is **negative**.

The dot product is a way to measure "how much two straws agree."

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

The **dot product** is an operation between two vectors that gives **a number** — that's why it's "scalar."

The rule for computing it is straightforward: multiply the first coordinates, multiply the second coordinates, and add the two results.

$$(3, 4) \cdot (1, 2) = 3 \cdot 1 + 4 \cdot 2 = 3 + 8 = 11$$

To know **what the result means**:
- **Positive** number: the two vectors point more or less in the same direction.
- **Zero**: the vectors are perpendicular (90° apart).
- **Negative** number: the vectors point in opposite directions.

**Real application.** You push a cart. If you push straight ahead (same direction as the motion), you do **a lot of work**. If you push up (perpendicular to the motion), you do **zero** useful work — it's like pushing against a horizontal wall.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old teen">

The dot product combines two vectors and returns **a number**. The formula:

$$(u_1, u_2) \cdot (v_1, v_2) = u_1 v_1 + u_2 v_2$$

This is simple to compute. What makes the dot product powerful is what it **measures geometrically**: $\vec u \cdot \vec v = |\vec u|\,|\vec v|\,\cos\theta$. When $\theta = 90°$, $\cos 90° = 0$ and the product is zero — the **perpendicularity** criterion. This is used in analytic geometry all the time.

**Angle between two vectors.** Isolating $\theta$:
$$\theta = \arccos\!\!\left(\frac{\vec u \cdot \vec v}{|\vec u|\,|\vec v|}\right)$$

**Numerical example.** Angle between $(1, 0)$ and $(1, 1)$:
$$\cos\theta = \frac{1 \cdot 1 + 0 \cdot 1}{1 \cdot \sqrt 2} = \frac{1}{\sqrt 2} \implies \theta = 45°$$

**Connection to Lesson 23.** The criterion $m_1 \cdot m_2 = -1$ for perpendicular lines comes exactly from the dot product of the direction vectors: if $\vec u = (1, m_1)$ and $\vec v = (1, m_2)$, then $\vec u \cdot \vec v = 1 + m_1 m_2 = 0 \iff m_1 m_2 = -1$.

**In physics.** The work done by a force $\vec F$ along the displacement $\vec d$ is $W = \vec F \cdot \vec d$ (joules). Force perpendicular to the motion: zero useful work.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

The dot product is the fundamental operation of Euclidean geometry. In $\mathbb{R}^n$:

$$\vec u \cdot \vec v = \sum_{i=1}^n u_i v_i$$

### Equivalence of the two definitions

**Proof via the law of cosines.** In the triangle with sides $|\vec u|, |\vec v|, |\vec u - \vec v|$ and angle $\theta$ between $\vec u$ and $\vec v$:

$$|\vec u - \vec v|^2 = |\vec u|^2 + |\vec v|^2 - 2|\vec u|\,|\vec v|\cos\theta$$

On the other hand, expanding in coordinates:

$$|\vec u - \vec v|^2 = (u_1 - v_1)^2 + (u_2 - v_2)^2 = |\vec u|^2 + |\vec v|^2 - 2(u_1 v_1 + u_2 v_2)$$

Equating: $u_1 v_1 + u_2 v_2 = |\vec u|\,|\vec v|\cos\theta$. QED.

### Cauchy–Schwarz inequality

$$|\vec u \cdot \vec v| \leq |\vec u|\,|\vec v|$$

Equality if and only if the vectors are parallel. Proof: $f(t) = |\vec u + t\vec v|^2 \geq 0$ for all $t$, so the discriminant is $\leq 0$.

### Orthogonal decomposition

Every $\vec u$ decomposes uniquely into a part parallel and a part perpendicular to $\vec v \neq \vec 0$:

$$\vec u = \underbrace{\frac{\vec u \cdot \vec v}{|\vec v|^2}\,\vec v}_{\text{parallel}} + \underbrace{\vec u - \frac{\vec u \cdot \vec v}{|\vec v|^2}\,\vec v}_{\text{perpendicular}}$$

Basis of the **Gram–Schmidt** process, which builds orthonormal bases — and hence of QR factorization and least squares.

### Cross-domain applications

- **Physics**: work $W = \vec F \cdot \vec d$, power $P = \vec F \cdot \vec v$, flux $\Phi = \vec E \cdot \vec A$ (Gauss's law).
- **ML**: cosine similarity $= \vec u \cdot \vec v / (|\vec u|\,|\vec v|)$ — the standard metric for embeddings and semantic search.
- **DSP**: cross-correlation $r_{xy}(\tau) = \sum x_n\,y_{n+\tau}$ is a dot product — the basis of matched filters, GPS, radar.
- **Statistics**: Pearson correlation = cosine between centered vectors.
- **Quantum**: amplitude $\langle \phi | \psi \rangle$ is a complex inner product; probability $= |\langle \phi | \psi \rangle|^2$ (Born rule).

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

The dot product is the canonical example of a **symmetric positive-definite bilinear form** on $\mathbb{R}^n$, or an **inner product** on a real vector space.

### Inner-product axioms

Given a real vector space $V$, $\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle: V \times V \to \mathbb{R}$ is an inner product if:
1. **Symmetry**: $\langle x, y \rangle = \langle y, x \rangle$
2. **Linearity in the first argument**: $\langle \alpha x + \beta y, z \rangle = \alpha\langle x,z\rangle + \beta\langle y,z\rangle$
3. **Positive-definite**: $\langle x, x\rangle \geq 0$, with equality $\iff x = 0$

Induced norm: $\|x\| = \sqrt{\langle x, x\rangle}$. Cauchy–Schwarz: $|\langle x, y\rangle| \leq \|x\|\,\|y\|$.

### Functional generalization

In $L^2(\Omega)$: $\langle f, g\rangle = \int_\Omega f\,\bar g\,d\mu$. The Fourier transform $\hat f(\omega) = \langle f, e^{i\omega t}\rangle$ is "decomposition in an orthonormal basis of exponentials." Each Fourier coefficient is a dot product.

### In ML and quant

- **Attention (Transformer)**: $\text{softmax}(QK^T/\sqrt d)\,V$. The product $QK^T$ is a matrix of dot products — how much each query "resonates" with each key.
- **Kernel methods (SVM)**: $K(x,y) = \langle \phi(x), \phi(y)\rangle$ implicitly. The kernel trick avoids computing $\phi$ explicitly.
- **Markowitz**: portfolio variance $\sigma_p^2 = w^T\Sigma w$ is a quadratic form, an extension of the dot product with a matrix.
- **Word2Vec**: the gradient of the log-likelihood is a function of dot products between word embeddings.

### Metric tensor

On a differentiable manifold $(M, g)$, at each point $p$ the metric tensor $g_p: T_pM \times T_pM \to \mathbb{R}$ is an inner product on the tangent space. General Relativity uses the Lorentzian signature $(-,+,+,+)$ — not positive-definite, but non-degenerate.

### Historical note

J. Willard Gibbs and Oliver Heaviside (1880s) formalized modern vector calculus, extracting the dot product and the cross product from Hamilton's quaternions and Grassmann's calculus of extension. The notation $\vec u \cdot \vec v$ is their legacy.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practical application — three real scenarios">

### Scenario 1: A bodyguard's work (physics)

A guard pushes a broken-down car with force $\vec F = (800, 0)$ N (purely horizontal) over a displacement $\vec d = (15, 0)$ m (horizontal). Work:

$$W = \vec F \cdot \vec d = 800 \cdot 15 + 0 \cdot 0 = 12\,000 \text{ J}$$

Now suppose the ground slopes up: $\vec d = (15, 3)$ m. The work drops to $800 \cdot 15 + 0 \cdot 3 = 12\,000$ J — the vertical component of the displacement does not contribute because the force is horizontal. If instead the force were $\vec F = (800, 200)$ N, the work would be $800 \cdot 15 + 200 \cdot 3 = 12\,600$ J.

### Scenario 2: Cosine similarity in NLP (recruiting)

An HR startup represents candidates by skill vectors. Candidate A: $\vec a = (5, 3, 0, 4)$ (Python, SQL, Java, Data). Candidate B: $\vec b = (4, 4, 1, 5)$. The cosine similarity is:

$$\text{sim} = \frac{\vec a \cdot \vec b}{|\vec a|\,|\vec b|} = \frac{20 + 12 + 0 + 20}{\sqrt{50}\cdot\sqrt{58}} \approx \frac{52}{53{,}8} \approx 0{,}967$$

A result close to 1: candidates with very similar profiles. The dot product (numerator = 52) normalized by the magnitudes gives a scale-independent similarity.

### Scenario 3: Portfolio return (finance)

The return of a portfolio is the dot product of the weight vector $\vec w$ with the return vector $\vec r$:

$$r_p = \vec w \cdot \vec r = \sum_{i=1}^n w_i r_i$$

For a portfolio with weights $(50\%, 30\%, 20\%)$ in assets with returns $(12\%, 8\%, -2\%)$:

$$r_p = 0{,}5 \cdot 0{,}12 + 0{,}3 \cdot 0{,}08 + 0{,}2 \cdot (-0{,}02) = 0{,}06 + 0{,}024 - 0{,}004 = 0{,}08 = 8\%$$

This is pure dot product. Markowitz optimization (minimizing variance) adds the quadratic form $w^T\Sigma w$ — which is also a dot product, but with the covariance matrix.

</Porta>

## Worked examples

> Five examples of increasing difficulty — direct computation, angle, orthogonality by a parameter, projection, and mechanical work. Each example cites its source: the problem comes from an open book.

<Exemplo titulo="Component-by-component computation" numero="1">

**Problem:** Compute $\vec u \cdot \vec v$ for $\vec u = \langle 3, 4 \rangle$ and $\vec v = \langle 1, 2 \rangle$.

**Strategy:** Apply the definition directly: multiply corresponding coordinates and add.

**Solution:**
1. Identify the components: $u_1 = 3,\ u_2 = 4,\ v_1 = 1,\ v_2 = 2$.
2. Apply the formula: $\vec u \cdot \vec v = u_1 v_1 + u_2 v_2 = 3 \cdot 1 + 4 \cdot 2$.
3. Add: $3 + 8 = 11$.

$$\vec u \cdot \vec v = 11$$

**Check:** The result is a positive scalar. Since both vectors have positive components, the angle between them is acute — a positive product, consistent.

**Source.** [Stitz–Zeager Precalculus §11.8, Example 11.8.1](https://www.stitz-zeager.com/szprecalculus07042013.pdf) — CC-BY-NC-SA license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Angle between vectors" numero="2">

**Problem:** Find the angle $\theta$ between $\vec u = \langle 3, -1 \rangle$ and $\vec v = \langle 4, 2 \rangle$.

**Strategy:** Use $\cos\theta = \vec u \cdot \vec v\;/\;(|\vec u|\,|\vec v|)$. Compute the dot product and the magnitudes separately, then apply arccosine.

**Solution:**
1. Dot product: $\vec u \cdot \vec v = 3 \cdot 4 + (-1) \cdot 2 = 12 - 2 = 10$.
2. Magnitude of $\vec u$: $|\vec u| = \sqrt{3^2 + (-1)^2} = \sqrt{10}$.
3. Magnitude of $\vec v$: $|\vec v| = \sqrt{4^2 + 2^2} = \sqrt{20} = 2\sqrt{5}$.
4. $\cos\theta = \dfrac{10}{\sqrt{10} \cdot 2\sqrt{5}} = \dfrac{10}{2\sqrt{50}} = \dfrac{10}{10\sqrt{2}} = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt 2}$.
5. $\theta = \arccos\!\left(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt 2}\right) = 45°$.

**Check:** Positive dot product $\Rightarrow$ acute angle. $45°$ is acute. Consistent.

**Source.** [Stitz–Zeager Precalculus §11.8, Example 11.8.2](https://www.stitz-zeager.com/szprecalculus07042013.pdf) — CC-BY-NC-SA license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Orthogonality by a parameter" numero="3">

**Problem:** For what value of $k$ are the vectors $\vec u = \langle 2, k \rangle$ and $\vec v = \langle 3, 1 \rangle$ perpendicular?

**Strategy:** By the orthogonality criterion, $\vec u \perp \vec v \iff \vec u \cdot \vec v = 0$. Compute the product as a function of $k$ and solve the equation.

**Solution:**
1. Dot product: $\vec u \cdot \vec v = 2 \cdot 3 + k \cdot 1 = 6 + k$.
2. Impose $6 + k = 0$.
3. $k = -6$.

$$\vec u = \langle 2, -6 \rangle \quad \text{is orthogonal to} \quad \vec v = \langle 3, 1 \rangle$$

**Check:** $\langle 2, -6 \rangle \cdot \langle 3, 1 \rangle = 6 - 6 = 0$. Confirmed.

**Source.** [OpenStax Algebra and Trigonometry 2e §8.8, Example 6](https://openstax.org/books/algebra-and-trigonometry-2e/pages/8-8-vectors) — CC-BY 4.0 license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Vector projection" numero="4">

**Problem:** Compute the vector projection of $\vec u = \langle 5, 12 \rangle$ onto $\vec v = \langle 3, 4 \rangle$.

**Strategy:** Use $\text{proj}_{\vec v}\,\vec u = \dfrac{\vec u \cdot \vec v}{|\vec v|^2}\,\vec v$. Compute the numerator and the denominator, then multiply by $\vec v$.

**Solution:**
1. Dot product: $\vec u \cdot \vec v = 5 \cdot 3 + 12 \cdot 4 = 15 + 48 = 63$.
2. Squared magnitude: $|\vec v|^2 = 9 + 16 = 25$.
3. Coefficient: $63/25$.
4. Projection: $\dfrac{63}{25}\langle 3, 4 \rangle = \left\langle \dfrac{189}{25},\, \dfrac{252}{25} \right\rangle$.

**Check:** The resulting vector is a multiple of $\vec v$ — as every projection must be. Residual $\vec u - \text{proj} = \langle 5 - 189/25,\; 12 - 252/25\rangle = \langle -64/25,\; 48/25\rangle$. Verification: $\langle -64/25, 48/25\rangle \cdot \langle 3, 4\rangle = -192/25 + 192/25 = 0$. Orthogonal to $\vec v$. Correct.

**Source.** [Stitz–Zeager Precalculus §11.8, Example 11.8.5](https://www.stitz-zeager.com/szprecalculus07042013.pdf) — CC-BY-NC-SA license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Work of a force" numero="5">

**Problem:** A child pulls a cart with force $\vec F = \langle 10, 0 \rangle$ N (horizontal) along the displacement $\vec d = \langle 12, 5 \rangle$ m. What is the work done?

**Strategy:** In mechanics, the work of a constant force is $W = \vec F \cdot \vec d$. The component of the force perpendicular to the displacement does not contribute.

**Solution:**
1. Identify: $\vec F = \langle 10, 0 \rangle$ N, $\vec d = \langle 12, 5 \rangle$ m.
2. Dot product: $\vec F \cdot \vec d = 10 \cdot 12 + 0 \cdot 5 = 120$.
3. Unit: N·m = J.

$$W = 120 \text{ J}$$

**Check:** The force is horizontal; the vertical component of the displacement (5 m) does not contribute. If $\vec F = \langle 10, 5\rangle$ N (a force with a vertical component), the work would be $10 \cdot 12 + 5 \cdot 5 = 145$ J — larger, because the force points more "with" the displacement.

**Source.** [OpenStax University Physics Volume 1 §7.1 — Work](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/7-1-work) — CC-BY 4.0 license.
</Exemplo>
