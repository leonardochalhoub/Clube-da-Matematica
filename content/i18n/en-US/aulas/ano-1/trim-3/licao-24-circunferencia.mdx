<Porta nivel="formal" titulo="Rigorous definition and properties">

<Definicao titulo="Circle">
The set of all points $(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2$ whose distance to the fixed point $C = (a, b)$ equals $r > 0$:
$$\mathcal{C} = \bigl\{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2 : \sqrt{(x-a)^2 + (y-b)^2} = r\bigr\}$$
</Definicao>

> "A circle is the set of all points in a plane that are at a fixed distance, called the radius, from a fixed point called the center of the circle." — [Stitz–Zeager Precalculus §7.2](https://www.stitz-zeager.com/szprecalculus07042013.pdf)

Squaring both sides (both non-negative, hence equivalent):

<Equation explicacao="Reduced equation of a circle. Each point (x, y) on the curve satisfies this equality." latex="(x - a)^2 + (y - b)^2 = r^2" />

### General form

Expanding the reduced form:

<Equation explicacao="General form of the circle equation. D = -2a, E = -2b, F = a² + b² - r²." latex="x^2 + y^2 + Dx + Ey + F = 0" />

**Recovery of parameters** (without completing the square):
- Center: $(a, b) = (-D/2,\ -E/2)$
- Radius: $r = \sqrt{D^2/4 + E^2/4 - F}$ — exists real only if $D^2 + E^2 - 4F > 0$.

### Relative position — line and circle

Let $d$ be the distance from center $C$ to line $\ell$.

<Definicao titulo="Relative position line–circle">

| Position | Criterion | Common points |
|---|---|---|
| Exterior | $d > r$ | 0 |
| Tangent | $d = r$ | 1 |
| Secant | $d < r$ | 2 |

For line <Eq>{`Ax + By + C_0 = 0`}</Eq>: <Eq>{`\\displaystyle d = \\frac{\\lvert Aa + Bb + C_0 \\rvert}{\\sqrt{A^2 + B^2}}`}</Eq>.

</Definicao>

### Relative position — two circles

Let $C_1, C_2$ be circles with radii $r_1, r_2$ and distance $d = |C_1 C_2|$.

| Position | Criterion |
|---|---|
| Disjoint externally | $d > r_1 + r_2$ |
| Externally tangent | $d = r_1 + r_2$ |
| Secant | $\lvert r_1 - r_2 \rvert < d < r_1 + r_2$ |
| Internally tangent | $d = \lvert r_1 - r_2 \rvert$ |
| One inside the other | $d < \lvert r_1 - r_2 \rvert$ |

### Tangent at a point on the curve

<Teorema titulo="Equation of the tangent">
The tangent line to the circle $(x - a)^2 + (y - b)^2 = r^2$ at point $P_0 = (x_0, y_0) \in \mathcal{C}$ is:
$$(x_0 - a)(x - a) + (y_0 - b)(y - b) = r^2$$

**Proof.** The vector $\vec{CP_0} = (x_0 - a,\ y_0 - b)$ is perpendicular to the tangent at $P_0$ (radius perpendicular to tangent). The equation of the line through $P_0$ with normal vector $\vec{CP_0}$ is exactly the formula above. Since $P_0 \in \mathcal{C}$, we have $(x_0-a)^2 + (y_0-b)^2 = r^2$, which appears upon expanding. ∎
</Teorema>

### Power of a point

<Definicao titulo="Power of a point">
For point $P_0$ and the circle with center $C$ and radius $r$:
<Eq display>{`\\text{pow}(P_0) = \\lvert P_0 C \\rvert^2 - r^2`}</Eq>

- <Eq>{`\\text{pow} < 0`}</Eq>: <Eq>{`P_0`}</Eq> inside the circle.
- <Eq>{`\\text{pow} = 0`}</Eq>: <Eq>{`P_0`}</Eq> on the circle.
- <Eq>{`\\text{pow} > 0`}</Eq>: <Eq>{`P_0`}</Eq> exterior; <Eq>{`\\sqrt{\\text{pow}}`}</Eq> is the length of the external tangent from <Eq>{`P_0`}</Eq>.
</Definicao>

<Insight>
The power of a point is **invariant** for every secant through $P_0$: if the secant meets the circle at $A$ and $B$, then $|P_0 A| \cdot |P_0 B| = |\text{pow}(P_0)|$. This does not depend on which secant you draw — it is the same value. It is a classical result of Greek geometry.
</Insight>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

Grab a **string** and pin one end to the ground with your finger. Hold the other end with chalk and walk in a circle, always keeping the string tight. You draw a **perfect wheel**.

Every point of that wheel stays at the **same distance** from the center. That distance is the **radius**.

That's it. A circle is just this: all the points that are exactly the same distance from the middle.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

A circle is the set of points at the **same distance** from a center — like what a compass draws. The distance from the pointed tip to the pencil is the **radius**.

The equation $(x - a)^2 + (y - b)^2 = r^2$ comes from the distance formula (Lesson 21):
- $(a, b)$ is the center.
- $r$ is the radius.
- For any point $(x, y)$ on the curve, the distance to the center is $r$. Squaring removes the square root.

**Real-world examples:**
- Wi-Fi has circular range.
- Running track: 400 m is the circumference (the length of one lap).
- Bicycle tire: every point on the rubber is the same radius from the axle.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old teen">

A circle is the set of points **equally distant** from a fixed center, at distance $r$ (radius).

Applying the distance formula from Lesson 21:
$$\sqrt{(x - a)^2 + (y - b)^2} = r$$

Squaring: $(x - a)^2 + (y - b)^2 = r^2$. **That's all there is.**

When $a = b = 0$: $x^2 + y^2 = r^2$. For $r = 1$: $x^2 + y^2 = 1$ — the trigonometric circle from Lesson 12!

**Converting general form to reduced — complete the square.** Example: $x^2 + y^2 - 4x + 6y - 12 = 0$.
- Group: $(x^2 - 4x) + (y^2 + 6y) = 12$.
- In $x$: half of $-4$ is $-2$; squared is $4$. Add 4 to both sides.
- In $y$: half of $6$ is $3$; squared is $9$. Add 9.
- Result: $(x - 2)^2 + (y + 3)^2 = 25$. Center $(2, -3)$, radius $5$.

**Warning:** not every equation $x^2 + y^2 + Dx + Ey + F = 0$ is a real circle. If $D^2/4 + E^2/4 - F < 0$, there is no real point — "imaginary circle". If $= 0$, it is a single point.

<Insight>
**Why is the tangent perpendicular to the radius?** Because the radius is the shortest path from the center to the edge. Any other direction leaving the tangent point would go further, so the tangent (the direction that "grazes" without crossing) must be exactly perpendicular to the radius. This will become the special case of gradient perpendicular to the level curve in Calculus III.
</Insight>

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

The circle is the simplest geometric locus in $\mathbb{R}^2$ — and the prototype of the $n$-sphere: $\|x - c\|^2 = r^2$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$.

### 2D trilateration

Three circles determine (generically) a unique position. Subtracting pairs of equations:

$$\bigl[(x-a_1)^2 + (y-b_1)^2 = r_1^2\bigr] - \bigl[(x-a_2)^2 + (y-b_2)^2 = r_2^2\bigr]$$

The terms $x^2$ and $y^2$ cancel — a **linear** equation in $x$ and $y$ remains. Three circles generate two pairs, hence a $2 \times 2$ linear system. Basis of GPS, LORAN, sonar.

### $\ell_2$ regularization (Ridge Regression)

The constraint $\beta_1^2 + \beta_2^2 \leq c$ is a disk in the coefficient space. The Ridge solution is the point where the smallest elliptic level curve of MSE touches the boundary of the disk — direct geometry of circle and ellipse.

### Engineering applications

- **Collision and culling (computer graphics):** bounding sphere — testing collision with a sphere is $O(1)$ compared to complex geometry.
- **Pressure distribution (fluid mechanics):** equipotential surfaces are circular in irrotational 2D flows.
- **von Mises stress:** Mohr's circle represents biaxial stress states — horizontal axis is normal stress, vertical is shear.

### Complete proof of the tangent equation

Let $P_0 = (x_0, y_0)$ be on the circle $(x-a)^2 + (y-b)^2 = r^2$. Radial vector: $\vec{v} = (x_0 - a, y_0 - b)$. The tangent at $P_0$ has $\vec{v}$ as its normal vector:

$$(x_0 - a)(x - x_0) + (y_0 - b)(y - y_0) = 0$$

Expanding and using $(x_0-a)^2 + (y_0-b)^2 = r^2$:

$$(x_0 - a)(x - a) + (y_0 - b)(y - b) = r^2 \quad \square$$

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

The circle is the **curve of constant curvature** $\kappa = 1/r$ — a special case of a Riemannian manifold of dimension 1 immersed in $\mathbb{R}^2$.

### Differential geometry

Parametrization: $\gamma(t) = (a + r\cos t,\ b + r\sin t)$, $t \in [0, 2\pi)$. Unit tangent vector: $T(t) = (-\sin t, \cos t)$. Normal vector (points inward): $N(t) = (-\cos t, -\sin t)$. Curvature: $\kappa = |T'(t)|/|\gamma'(t)| = 1/r$ — constant.

### Osculating circle

On any plane curve $\gamma(t)$, the osculating circle at point $P$ is the circle of curvature equal to the curve's at that point, with radius $\rho = 1/\kappa(P)$. Road engineering: the transition between a straight line ($\kappa = 0$) and a circular curve uses a **clothoid** — a curve in which $\kappa$ grows linearly with arc length — to avoid discontinuity in steering.

### Descartes' theorem (1643)

For four mutually tangent circles with curvatures $k_i = 1/r_i$:

$$(k_1 + k_2 + k_3 + k_4)^2 = 2(k_1^2 + k_2^2 + k_3^2 + k_4^2)$$

Basis of **Apollonian gaskets** — fractals generated by recursive insertions of mutually tangent circles. They appear in number theory (Descartes circle packing with integer curvatures) and in bubble physics (Plateau foam).

### Concentration of measure in high dimension

The $n$-sphere $\mathbb{S}^{n-1} \subset \mathbb{R}^n$ has the property that for large $n$, almost all of the surface area concentrates in an equatorial strip of width $O(1/\sqrt{n})$. This explains counterintuitive phenomena in ML: in $\mathbb{R}^{1000}$, random vectors are almost always orthogonal to each other.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practical application — recipe for analysis">

### Recipe: extracting information from a circle equation

1. **Reduced form** $(x-a)^2 + (y-b)^2 = r^2$: read center $(a, b)$ and radius $r$ directly.
2. **General form** $x^2 + y^2 + Dx + Ey + F = 0$: use $a = -D/2$, $b = -E/2$, $r^2 = a^2 + b^2 - F$. If the result is negative, it is not a real circle.
3. **Quick check**: substitute a known point and test.

### Typical problems and their methods

| Problem | Method |
|---|---|
| Relative position line–circle | Calculate $d(\text{center, line})$, compare with $r$ |
| Tangent at a point on the curve | Formula $(x_0-a)(x-a) + (y_0-b)(y-b) = r^2$ |
| Length of external tangent from $P$ | $\sqrt{\lvert PC \rvert^2 - r^2}$ |
| Circle through 3 points | System $3 \times 3$ or shortcut (right triangle) |
| Position of two circles | Compare $d(C_1, C_2)$ with $r_1 + r_2$ and $|r_1 - r_2|$ |

### Brazilian application: cell tower coverage

A radio base station in São Paulo at point $(0, 0)$ (km) covers a radius of 2 km. Another at $(3, 0)$ covers a radius of 2 km. Distance between centers = 3. Sum of radii = 4. Since $|r_1 - r_2| = 0 < 3 < 4$, the coverages are **secant** — there is an overlap zone. The intersection points are found by solving the linear system (subtraction of equations).

</Porta>

## Worked examples

<Exemplo titulo="Reduced equation from center and radius" numero="1">

**Problem:** Write the equation of the circle with center $C = (-1, 4)$ and radius $r = 3$.

**Strategy:** Substitute directly into $(x - a)^2 + (y - b)^2 = r^2$ with $(a, b) = (-1, 4)$ and $r = 3$.

**Solution:**

1. Identify the parameters: $a = -1$, $b = 4$, $r = 3$.
2. Substitute: $(x - (-1))^2 + (y - 4)^2 = 9$.
3. Simplify: $(x + 1)^2 + (y - 4)^2 = 9$.

**Verification:** A candidate point on the edge: $(-1 + 3, 4) = (2, 4)$. Substituting: $(2+1)^2 + (4-4)^2 = 9 + 0 = 9$. ✓ The center $(-1, 4)$ gives $(0)^2 + (0)^2 = 0 < 9$ — is inside. ✓

**Source.** [Stitz–Zeager Precalculus §7.2](https://www.stitz-zeager.com/szprecalculus07042013.pdf) — CC-BY-NC-SA.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="General form to reduced form via completing the square" numero="2">

**Problem:** Determine center and radius of $x^2 + y^2 - 6x + 8y - 11 = 0$.

**Strategy:** Regroup terms in $x$ and in $y$ and complete the square in each variable separately.

**Solution:**

1. Group: $(x^2 - 6x) + (y^2 + 8y) = 11$.
2. Complete in $x$: half of $-6$ is $-3$, squared is $9$. Add 9 to both sides.
3. Complete in $y$: half of $8$ is $4$, squared is $16$. Add 16.
4. Result: $(x - 3)^2 - 9 + (y + 4)^2 - 16 = 11$, so $(x - 3)^2 + (y + 4)^2 = 36$.
5. Center $C = (3, -4)$, radius $r = 6$.

**Verification:** By shortcut of general form: $D = -6$, $E = 8$, $F = -11$. $a = 3$, $b = -4$, $r^2 = 9 + 16 + 11 = 36$. ✓

**Source.** [Stitz–Zeager Precalculus §7.2](https://www.stitz-zeager.com/szprecalculus07042013.pdf) — CC-BY-NC-SA.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Relative position between line and circle" numero="3">

**Problem:** Determine whether the line $y = x + 1$ is exterior, tangent, or secant to the circle $x^2 + y^2 = 9$.

**Strategy:** Calculate the distance $d$ from the center $(0, 0)$ to the line and compare with $r = 3$.

**Solution:**

1. Write the line in general form: $x - y + 1 = 0$. Then $A = 1$, $B = -1$, $C_0 = 1$.
2. Distance: $d = |1 \cdot 0 + (-1) \cdot 0 + 1| / \sqrt{1 + 1} = 1/\sqrt{2} = \sqrt{2}/2 \approx 0.71$.
3. Since $d \approx 0.71 < 3 = r$, the line is **secant** (cuts in 2 points).

**Verification:** Substitute $y = x + 1$ into $x^2 + y^2 = 9$: $2x^2 + 2x - 8 = 0$, discriminant $\Delta = 4 + 64 = 68 > 0$ — two distinct real roots. ✓

**Source.** [Stitz–Zeager Precalculus §7.2](https://www.stitz-zeager.com/szprecalculus07042013.pdf) — CC-BY-NC-SA.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Tangent to the circle at a point on the curve" numero="4">

**Problem:** Find the equation of the tangent line to the circle $x^2 + y^2 = 25$ at point $P_0 = (3, 4)$.

**Strategy:** Verify that $P_0$ is on the circle and apply the tangent formula.

**Solution:**

1. Verify: $3^2 + 4^2 = 9 + 16 = 25$. $P_0$ is on the circle. ✓
2. Apply the formula with $a = b = 0$, $r^2 = 25$: $x_0 \cdot x + y_0 \cdot y = r^2$, so $3x + 4y = 25$.
3. Explicit form: $y = -\dfrac{3}{4}x + \dfrac{25}{4}$.

**Verification:** Radial vector $\vec{CP_0} = (3, 4)$. Direction vector of tangent: $(4, -3)$ (coefficient of $x$ becomes $y$ with opposite sign). Dot product: $3 \cdot 4 + 4 \cdot (-3) = 0$. Perpendicular. ✓

**Source.** [Stitz–Zeager Precalculus §7.2](https://www.stitz-zeager.com/szprecalculus07042013.pdf) — CC-BY-NC-SA.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="2D trilateration — localization by 3 antennas" numero="5">

**Problem:** Three cell phone antennas measure distances to a phone: $A_1 = (0, 0)$ measures $d_1 = 5$ km; $A_2 = (8, 0)$ measures $d_2 = 5$ km; $A_3 = (4, 7)$ measures $d_3 = 4$ km. Locate the phone.

**Strategy:** Each antenna defines a circle. Subtracting pairs of equations cancels the quadratic terms, producing linear equations.

**Solution:**

1. Three circles:
   - $C_1$: $x^2 + y^2 = 25$
   - $C_2$: $(x-8)^2 + y^2 = 25 \Rightarrow x^2 - 16x + 64 + y^2 = 25$
   - $C_3$: $(x-4)^2 + (y-7)^2 = 16 \Rightarrow x^2 - 8x + 16 + y^2 - 14y + 49 = 16$

2. $C_2 - C_1$: $-16x + 64 = 0 \Rightarrow x = 4$.

3. $C_3 - C_1$: $-8(4) + 16 - 14y + 49 = -9 \Rightarrow -32 + 16 - 14y + 49 = -9 \Rightarrow 33 - 14y = -9 \Rightarrow y = 3$.

4. Position: $(4, 3)$.

**Verification:** $d((4,3), A_1) = 5$ ✓; $d((4,3), A_2) = \sqrt{16 + 9} = 5$ ✓; $d((4,3), A_3) = \sqrt{0 + 16} = 4$ ✓.

**Source.** [Stitz–Zeager Precalculus §7.2](https://www.stitz-zeager.com/szprecalculus07042013.pdf) — CC-BY-NC-SA.
</Exemplo>
