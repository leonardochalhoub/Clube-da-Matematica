<Porta nivel="formal" titulo="Definition and criteria">

### Classification of relative position

Let $r: y = m_r x + n_r$ and $s: y = m_s x + n_s$ be two non-vertical lines in the Cartesian plane.

<Definicao titulo="The four cases — slope-intercept form and general form">

| Position | Criterion (slope-intercept form) | Criterion (general form $Ax + By + C = 0$) |
|---|---|---|
| Distinct parallel | $m_r = m_s$ and $n_r \neq n_s$ | $A_r B_s = A_s B_r$ and $A_r C_s \neq A_s C_r$ |
| Coincident | $m_r = m_s$ and $n_r = n_s$ | $A_r B_s = A_s B_r$ and $A_r C_s = A_s C_r$ |
| Concurrent (non-perp.) | $m_r \neq m_s$ | $A_r B_s - A_s B_r \neq 0$ |
| Perpendicular | $m_r \cdot m_s = -1$ | $A_r A_s + B_r B_s = 0$ |

**Vertical lines:** the line $x = a$ has no defined slope. Two vertical lines $x = a$ and $x = b$ are parallel if $a \neq b$ and coincident if $a = b$. A vertical and a horizontal line $y = c$ are perpendicular.

</Definicao>

> "Two distinct lines are parallel when they have the same slope and perpendicular when the product of their slopes equals $-1$." — [Stitz–Zeager Precalculus, §2.1, p. 168](https://www.stitz-zeager.com/szprecalculus07042013.pdf)

### Point of intersection

<Definicao titulo="Solution of the 2×2 system">

When $m_r \neq m_s$, the lines meet at exactly one point $(x^*, y^*)$:

<Equation explicacao="Set the two expressions for y equal and solve for x." latex="m_r x + n_r = m_s x + n_s \implies x^* = \frac{n_s - n_r}{m_r - m_s}" />

<Equation explicacao="Substitute x* into either equation to get y*." latex="y^* = m_r x^* + n_r" />

</Definicao>

### Criterion for perpendicularity via dot product

<Teorema titulo="Condition m₁ · m₂ = −1">

Let $\vec{u}_1 = (1, m_1)$ and $\vec{u}_2 = (1, m_2)$ be direction vectors of $r$ and $s$. Then:

<Equation explicacao="Perpendicularity is equivalent to zero dot product, which is equivalent to m₁ m₂ = −1." latex="r \perp s \iff \vec{u}_1 \cdot \vec{u}_2 = 0 \iff 1 + m_1 m_2 = 0 \iff m_1 m_2 = -1" />

**Proof.** $\vec{u}_1 \cdot \vec{u}_2 = 1 \cdot 1 + m_1 \cdot m_2 = 1 + m_1 m_2$. This is zero if and only if $m_1 m_2 = -1$. ∎

</Teorema>

### Distance from point to line

<Definicao titulo="Point-to-line distance formula">

Given the point $P_0 = (x_0, y_0)$ and the line $r: Ax + By + C = 0$ (with $A^2 + B^2 > 0$):

<Equation explicacao="Distance is the length of the perpendicular dropped from P₀ to the line r — the shortest possible distance from a point to a line." latex="d(P_0,\, r) = \frac{|A x_0 + B y_0 + C|}{\sqrt{A^2 + B^2}}" />

</Definicao>

> "The distance $d$ from the point $(x_1, y_1)$ to the line $ax + by + c = 0$ is $d = |ax_1 + by_1 + c|/\sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$." — [Stitz–Zeager Precalculus, §2.1, p. 172](https://www.stitz-zeager.com/szprecalculus07042013.pdf)

### Distance between parallel lines

For lines $r: Ax + By + C_1 = 0$ and $s: Ax + By + C_2 = 0$ (same $A$ and $B$, hence parallel):

<Equation explicacao="Difference of the constant terms divided by the norm of the normal vector." latex="d(r,\ s) = \frac{|C_1 - C_2|}{\sqrt{A^2 + B^2}}" />

### Angle between concurrent lines

<Equation explicacao="θ is the acute angle between the lines; the absolute value ensures 0° ≤ θ ≤ 90°." latex="\tan\theta = \left|\frac{m_r - m_s}{1 + m_r m_s}\right|" />

<Insight>

**Connection to linear systems.** The relative position of two lines is exactly the geometric interpretation of a 2×2 system: unique solution = concurrent lines; no solution = distinct parallel lines; infinitely many solutions = coincident lines. In Linear Algebra (Trimesters 10–12) this logic generalizes to $n$ equations in $n$ unknowns — but the intuition comes from here.

</Insight>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

Imagine you're looking at two **railroad tracks**. They run straight, side by side, never touching. This is called **parallel**.

Now take two rulers and place one on top of the other making a **plus sign** "+". They cross making a perfect 90-degree corner. This is called **perpendicular**.

And when two streets in a city cross at one point — but not at a right corner — they are simply **concurrent**: they meet at one place.

Two tracks that are the same track, one on top of the other, are **coincident** — they are the same line, exactly.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

Two lines in the plane can do four different things:

1. **Parallel**: they go in the same direction and never meet. $y = 2x + 1$ and $y = 2x - 5$ both have slope 2 — they never cross.
2. **Coincident**: they are the same line in disguise. $y = 2x + 1$ and $2y = 4x + 2$ are the same line.
3. **Concurrent**: they cross at one point. $y = x$ and $y = 3x - 2$ meet somewhere.
4. **Perpendicular**: they cross making a right angle (90°). $y = 2x$ and $y = -x/2$ are perpendicular — note that $2 \times (-1/2) = -1$.

**How to tell them apart?** Look at the number $m$ (the slope):
- $m_1 = m_2$: parallel (or coincident if $n$ is also equal).
- $m_1 \times m_2 = -1$: perpendicular.
- Anything else: concurrent.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old">

This lesson connects geometry and algebra directly. Two geometric objects (lines) have a relationship you determine with a single algebraic calculation (compare $m_1$ and $m_2$).

**The four cases and how to recognize them:**

| Situation | Criterion | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Parallel | $m_1 = m_2$, $n_1 \neq n_2$ | $y = 3x + 1$ and $y = 3x - 7$ |
| Coincident | $m_1 = m_2$, $n_1 = n_2$ | $y = 3x + 1$ and $2y = 6x + 2$ |
| Concurrent | $m_1 \neq m_2$ | $y = x$ and $y = 2x - 1$ |
| Perpendicular | $m_1 \cdot m_2 = -1$ | $y = 4x$ and $y = -x/4$ |

**Why $m_1 \cdot m_2 = -1$?** Think of the inclination angle $\alpha$ of the first line, with $m_1 = \tan\alpha$. If the second forms $90°$ with the first, its angle is $\alpha + 90°$. By the trig identity, $\tan(\alpha + 90°) = -\cot\alpha = -1/\tan\alpha = -1/m_1$. So $m_2 = -1/m_1$, that is, $m_1 m_2 = -1$.

**Finding the intersection point:** Set the two equations equal. For example, $r: y = 2x + 1$ and $s: y = -x/2 + 5$:

$$2x + 1 = -\frac{x}{2} + 5 \implies \frac{5x}{2} = 4 \implies x = \frac{8}{5}, \quad y = 2 \cdot \frac{8}{5} + 1 = \frac{21}{5}$$

The lines cross at $\bigl(\tfrac{8}{5}, \tfrac{21}{5}\bigr)$.

**Distance from point to line** — a fundamental formula you'll use in dozens of situations:

$$d = \frac{|Ax_0 + By_0 + C|}{\sqrt{A^2 + B^2}}$$

Example: distance from $(1, 1)$ to the line $3x + 4y - 20 = 0$:

$$d = \frac{|3 \cdot 1 + 4 \cdot 1 - 20|}{\sqrt{9 + 16}} = \frac{13}{5} = 2.6$$

<Insight>

**Why does this matter beyond this lesson?** The relative position of lines is the geometric interpretation of systems of linear equations. One solution = concurrent lines. No solution = parallel. Infinitely many solutions = coincident. In Linear Algebra (Trimesters 10–12) you will generalize to $n$ equations in $n$ unknowns — but the geometric intuition comes from here.

</Insight>

</Porta>

### 2×2 system and complete geometric interpretation

<Eq>{`\\begin{pmatrix} a_1 & b_1 \\\\ a_2 & b_2 \\end{pmatrix} \\begin{pmatrix} x \\\\ y \\end{pmatrix} = \\begin{pmatrix} c_1 \\\\ c_2 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>

| Geometric case | Algebraic case | Determinant $\Delta = a_1 b_2 - a_2 b_1$ |
|---|---|---|
| Concurrent lines | Unique solution | $\Delta \neq 0$ |
| Coincident lines | Infinitely many solutions | $\Delta = 0$, consistent system |
| Distinct parallel lines | No solution | $\Delta = 0$, inconsistent system |

**Cramer's rule** (for 2×2, when $\Delta \neq 0$):

<Eq>{`x^* = \\frac{\\begin{vmatrix} c_1 & b_1 \\\\ c_2 & b_2 \\end{vmatrix}}{\\Delta}, \\qquad y^* = \\frac{\\begin{vmatrix} a_1 & c_1 \\\\ a_2 & c_2 \\end{vmatrix}}{\\Delta}`}</Eq>

### Proof of the point-to-line distance formula

The line $Ax + By + C = 0$ has normal vector $\vec{n} = (A, B)$ with $|\vec{n}| = \sqrt{A^2 + B^2}$. Given $P_0 = (x_0, y_0)$ and any point $Q = (x_Q, y_Q)$ on the line (so $Ax_Q + By_Q + C = 0$), the distance from $P_0$ to the line is the magnitude of the projection of $\overrightarrow{QP_0}$ onto $\hat{n}$:

$$d = \left|\overrightarrow{QP_0} \cdot \hat{n}\right| = \frac{|A(x_0 - x_Q) + B(y_0 - y_Q)|}{\sqrt{A^2+B^2}} = \frac{|Ax_0 + By_0 + C|}{\sqrt{A^2+B^2}}$$

(In the last equality we use $Ax_Q + By_Q = -C$.)

### Immediate applications

- **Market equilibrium**: supply and demand are lines; equilibrium is their intersection — a 2×2 system.
- **GIS/GPS**: orthogonal distance from a vehicle to the planned route.
- **SVM (Machine Learning)**: the margin of the support vector classifier is $2/\|\vec{w}\|$; distance from each point to the separator hyperplane uses exactly the formula from this lesson.
- **Linear regression**: residuals = vertical distances from points to the fitted line.

### Perpendicular bisector as a locus

Given $A = (x_A, y_A)$ and $B = (x_B, y_B)$, the perpendicular bisector of $\overline{AB}$ is the set of equidistant points. Expanding $(x - x_A)^2 + (y - y_A)^2 = (x - x_B)^2 + (y - y_B)^2$ and canceling quadratic terms leaves a linear equation — the perpendicular bisector is perpendicular to $\overline{AB}$ and passes through the midpoint $M = \bigl(\tfrac{x_A+x_B}{2}, \tfrac{y_A+y_B}{2}\bigr)$.

</Porta>

### Orthogonality in inner product spaces

In the plane $\mathbb{R}^2$, "perpendicular lines" is the simplest case of **orthogonality of subspaces**: two subspaces $V, W \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ are orthogonal if $\langle v, w \rangle = 0$ for all $v \in V$, $w \in W$. The orthogonal complement $V^\perp$ satisfies $V \oplus V^\perp = \mathbb{R}^n$.

The point-to-line distance formula is the magnitude of the **orthogonal projection** of $\overrightarrow{QP_0}$ onto the normal direction $\hat{n}$. In $\mathbb{R}^n$, the distance from a point to a hyperplane $\vec{n} \cdot \vec{x} = c$ is $d = |\vec{n} \cdot \vec{x}_0 - c| / \|\vec{n}\|$ — identical in structure.

### Near-singular systems (ill-conditioned)

When two lines are nearly parallel ($m_1 \approx m_2$), the determinant $\Delta \approx 0$ and the condition number $\kappa(A)$ explodes. Small perturbations in the right-hand side shift the intersection enormously. Partial pivoting and matrix preconditioning mitigate this in numerical Gauss–Jordan.

### PCA and orthogonality

Principal components (PCA) are orthogonal eigenvectors of the covariance matrix. The spectral decomposition $\Sigma = Q \Lambda Q^T$ uses orthogonality in the precise sense. The first principal component is the direction of maximum variance; the second is perpendicular to the first. The generalization of "perpendicular" from this lesson appears throughout multivariate analysis.

### Quantitative applications

- **SVM (linear kernel)**: hyperplane $\vec{w} \cdot \vec{x} + b = 0$; distance from point to hyperplane is $(\vec{w} \cdot \vec{x}_i + b)/\|\vec{w}\|$; maximizing margin = minimizing $\|\vec{w}\|^2$.
- **Finance (factor pricing)**: multifactor regression decomposes return into orthogonal components (idiosyncratic + systematic).
- **Robotics**: trajectory planning by potential fields uses the gradient of distance to obstacles — the derivative of the point-to-line distance function extended to 3D space.

### Symbolic verification with SymPy

```python
import sympy as sp
from sympy import Line, Point

r = Line(Point(0, 1), Point(1, 3))   # y = 2x + 1
s = Line(Point(0, 5), Point(2, 4))   # y = -x/2 + 5

print(r.is_perpendicular(s))         # True  (2 * (-1/2) = -1)
print(r.intersection(s))             # [Point2D(8/5, 21/5)]
print(s.distance(Point(0, 0)))       # distance from origin to line s
```

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practical application — step by step">

### Recipe: classify two lines in 3 steps

**Step 1.** Put both in the form $y = mx + n$ and read $m_1, m_2$.

**Step 2.** Compare:
- $m_1 = m_2$? Go to Step 3.
- $m_1 \cdot m_2 = -1$? **Perpendicular** — find the intersection point.
- Otherwise? **Concurrent** — solve the system.

**Step 3.** Compare $n_1$ and $n_2$:
- $n_1 \neq n_2$? **Distinct parallel** (never meet).
- $n_1 = n_2$? **Coincident** (same line).

### Example: market equilibrium (Brazilian data)

An ice cream distributor has supply and demand curves as a function of price $p$ (in R\$/unit):

- Supply: $q_S = 3p - 6$ (produces more when price rises)
- Demand: $q_D = 20 - 2p$ (buys less when price rises)

Equilibrium: $3p - 6 = 20 - 2p \Rightarrow 5p = 26 \Rightarrow p^* = 5.20$. Quantity: $q^* = 3(5.2) - 6 = 9.6$ units. Geometrically, it is the intersection point of the two lines — the slopes are $3$ and $-2$, so $3 \cdot (-2) \neq -1$: concurrent non-perpendicular.

### Example: orthogonal distance to a street

You are at $(3, 4)$ (coordinates in km, from GPS). The main street follows $5x - 12y + 26 = 0$.

$$d = \frac{|5 \cdot 3 - 12 \cdot 4 + 26|}{\sqrt{25 + 144}} = \frac{|15 - 48 + 26|}{13} = \frac{7}{13} \approx 0.54 \text{ km}$$

### Common pitfalls

- **Vertical lines**: $x = 3$ has no defined $m$. Use the general form $Ax + By + C = 0$.
- **Nearly parallel**: if $m_1 = 2.0001$ and $m_2 = 2$, they are technically concurrent, but the intersection is very far away.
- **Don't confuse "concurrent" with "perpendicular"**: perpendicular lines are a special case of concurrent.

</Porta>

## Worked examples

<Exemplo titulo="Classify a pair of lines by slopes" numero="1">

**Problem.** Classify the relative position of lines $r: y = 3x + 2$ and $s: y = 3x - 7$.

**Strategy.** Compare the slopes and, if equal, compare the y-intercepts.

**Solution.**

1. Read the coefficients: $m_r = 3$, $m_s = 3$, $n_r = 2$, $n_s = -7$.
2. Since $m_r = m_s = 3$: same slope — the lines are **parallel or coincident**.
3. Check intercepts: $n_r = 2 \neq -7 = n_s$.
4. Conclusion: **distinct parallel** — they never meet.

**Verification.** Try solving the system: $(y = 3x + 2)$ and $(y = 3x - 7)$ imply $3x + 2 = 3x - 7$, that is $2 = -7$. Contradiction — system has no solution, confirming: distinct parallel.

**Source.** [Stitz–Zeager Precalculus §2.1, p. 167](https://www.stitz-zeager.com/szprecalculus07042013.pdf) — CC-BY-NC-SA.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Verify perpendicularity and find intersection" numero="2">

**Problem.** Given $r: y = 2x + 1$ and $s: y = -\tfrac{1}{2}x + 5$, verify if they are perpendicular and find the intersection point.

**Strategy.** Check the product of the slopes. If $m_r \cdot m_s = -1$: perpendicular. Then solve the 2×2 system.

**Solution.**

1. Identify: $m_r = 2$, $m_s = -\tfrac{1}{2}$.
2. Product: $m_r \cdot m_s = 2 \cdot (-\tfrac{1}{2}) = -1$. Confirmed: **perpendicular**.
3. Set the expressions for $y$ equal:

$$2x + 1 = -\frac{x}{2} + 5$$

4. Multiply by 2: $4x + 2 = -x + 10 \Rightarrow 5x = 8 \Rightarrow x = \tfrac{8}{5}$.
5. Substitute: $y = 2 \cdot \tfrac{8}{5} + 1 = \tfrac{21}{5}$.

**Verification.** Check in $s$: $y = -\tfrac{1}{2} \cdot \tfrac{8}{5} + 5 = -\tfrac{4}{5} + \tfrac{25}{5} = \tfrac{21}{5}$. Correct.

**Source.** [OpenStax College Algebra 2e §4.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/4-1-linear-functions) — CC-BY 4.0.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Point-to-line distance — apply the formula" numero="3">

**Problem.** Calculate the distance from the point $P_0 = (2, 3)$ to the line $r: 3x + 4y - 12 = 0$.

**Strategy.** Apply $d = |Ax_0 + By_0 + C| / \sqrt{A^2 + B^2}$ with $A = 3$, $B = 4$, $C = -12$.

**Solution.**

1. Identify: $A = 3$, $B = 4$, $C = -12$, $x_0 = 2$, $y_0 = 3$.
2. Numerator: $|3 \cdot 2 + 4 \cdot 3 - 12| = |6 + 12 - 12| = 6$.
3. Denominator: $\sqrt{9 + 16} = \sqrt{25} = 5$.
4. Distance: $d = \tfrac{6}{5} = 1.2$.

**Verification.** The perpendicular from $P_0$ has direction $(3, 4)$ (the normal vector). Parametrizing: $(2 + 3t, 3 + 4t)$. Substituting: $3(2+3t) + 4(3+4t) - 12 = 0 \Rightarrow 25t = -6 \Rightarrow t = -6/25$. Distance $= |t| \cdot 5 = \tfrac{6}{5}$. Confirms.

**Source.** [Stitz–Zeager Precalculus §2.1, p. 172](https://www.stitz-zeager.com/szprecalculus07042013.pdf) — CC-BY-NC-SA.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Parallel and perpendicular line through a point" numero="4">

**Problem.** Given the line $r: y = -\tfrac{3}{4}x + 2$, find: (a) the line parallel to $r$ through point $P = (8, 1)$; (b) the line perpendicular to $r$ through the same point.

**Strategy.** (a) A parallel line preserves $m$. (b) A perpendicular line uses $m' = -1/m$.

**Solution.**

**Part (a) — parallel:**

1. $m = -\tfrac{3}{4}$. The parallel has the same $m$.
2. Point-slope form: $y - 1 = -\tfrac{3}{4}(x - 8)$.
3. Simplify: $y = -\tfrac{3}{4}x + 6 + 1 = -\tfrac{3}{4}x + 7$.

**Part (b) — perpendicular:**

1. $m' = -1/(-\tfrac{3}{4}) = \tfrac{4}{3}$.
2. Point-slope form: $y - 1 = \tfrac{4}{3}(x - 8)$.
3. Simplify: $y = \tfrac{4}{3}x - \tfrac{32}{3} + 1 = \tfrac{4}{3}x - \tfrac{29}{3}$.

**Verification.** Product of slopes: $(-\tfrac{3}{4}) \cdot \tfrac{4}{3} = -1$. Correct.

<Exemplo titulo="Supply and demand equilibrium — real-world modeling" numero="5">

**Problem.** Market with: supply $S(p) = 3p - 6$ and demand $D(p) = 20 - 2p$. Find equilibrium price and quantity. Interpret geometrically.

**Strategy.** Equilibrium occurs where supply equals demand, that is, the intersection of the two lines on the $p \times q$ plane.

**Solution.**

1. Set equal: $3p - 6 = 20 - 2p$.
2. Solve: $5p = 26 \Rightarrow p^* = \tfrac{26}{5} = 5.2$.
3. Quantity: $q^* = 3 \cdot 5.2 - 6 = 15.6 - 6 = 9.6$.

**Geometric interpretation.** $m_S = 3$ and $m_D = -2$, so $3 \neq -2$: concurrent lines with exactly one equilibrium. $m_S \cdot m_D = -6 \neq -1$: not perpendicular.

**Verification.** $D(5.2) = 20 - 10.4 = 9.6 = S(5.2)$. Correct.

</Exemplo>
