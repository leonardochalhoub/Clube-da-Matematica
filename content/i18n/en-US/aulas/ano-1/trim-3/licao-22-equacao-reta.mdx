### Slope

<Definicao titulo="Slope of a line">

Let $\ell$ be a non-vertical line in the plane $\mathbb{R}^2$. Given any two distinct points $P_1 = (x_1, y_1)$ and $P_2 = (x_2, y_2)$ on $\ell$ with $x_1 \neq x_2$, the **slope** (or inclination) of $\ell$ is:

<Equation explicacao="The slope m is the ratio of the change in y to the change in x between any two points on the line. Geometrically, m equals the tangent of the angle alpha that the line makes with the positive x-axis." latex="m = \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1} = \tan\alpha" />

The value of $m$ **does not depend on the choice** of points $P_1, P_2$ — any pair of distinct points on $\ell$ yields the same quotient.

</Definicao>

> "The slope of a line passing through two points $(x_1, y_1)$ and $(x_2, y_2)$ is $m = (y_2 - y_1)/(x_2 - x_1)$, assuming $x_1 \neq x_2$." — [OpenStax College Algebra 2e, §4.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/4-1-linear-functions)

### Forms of the line equation

<Definicao titulo="Equivalent forms of the line equation">

| Form | Equation | When to use |
|---|---|---|
| Slope-intercept | $y = mx + n$ | graph, explicit $y$, interpolation |
| General | $Ax + By + C = 0,\ (A,B)\neq(0,0)$ | vertical lines, symmetry, distances |
| Point-slope | $y - y_0 = m(x - x_0)$ | given point + slope |
| Parametric | $x = x_0 + at,\ y = y_0 + bt,\ t\in\mathbb{R}$ | trajectory, animation, directions |
| Intercept | $x/p + y/q = 1,\ p,q\neq 0$ | intercepts $(p,0)$ and $(0,q)$ given |

**Useful conversions:** for the general form $Ax + By + C = 0$ with $B \neq 0$, we have $m = -A/B$ and $n = -C/B$.

Vertical lines ($x = c$) **do not admit** slope-intercept form — only general form ($x - c = 0$, with $B = 0$).

</Definicao>

> "The general equation of a line is $Ax + By + C = 0$, where $A$ and $B$ are not both zero. [...] If $B \neq 0$, the equation can be put in slope-intercept form $y = (-A/B)x + (-C/B)$." — [Stitz–Zeager Precalculus, §2.1](https://www.stitz-zeager.com/szprecalculus07042013.pdf)

### Parallelism and perpendicularity

<Teorema titulo="Criteria for parallelism and perpendicularity">

Let $\ell_1$ and $\ell_2$ be non-vertical lines with slopes $m_1$ and $m_2$.

<Equation explicacao="Two lines are parallel if and only if they have the same slope and different y-intercepts. They are perpendicular if and only if the product of their slopes is negative one." latex="\ell_1 \parallel \ell_2 \iff m_1 = m_2 \quad (\text{and } n_1 \neq n_2)" />

<Equation explicacao="Perpendicularity condition: the product of slopes is negative one." latex="\ell_1 \perp \ell_2 \iff m_1 \cdot m_2 = -1" />

**Proof of perpendicularity.** A direction vector of $\ell_1$ is $\mathbf{u}_1 = (1, m_1)$ and of $\ell_2$ is $\mathbf{u}_2 = (1, m_2)$. Perpendicularity requires $\mathbf{u}_1 \cdot \mathbf{u}_2 = 0$: $1 \cdot 1 + m_1 \cdot m_2 = 0$, so $m_1 m_2 = -1$. ∎

</Teorema>

### Distance from a point to a line

<Definicao titulo="Point-to-line distance">

For the line $\ell: Ax + By + C = 0$ and the point $P_0 = (x_0, y_0)$:

<Equation explicacao="The distance from P zero to the line is the length of the perpendicular segment from P zero to the line. The vector (A, B) is normal to the line, and the denominator is its norm." latex="d(P_0,\ \ell) = \frac{|A x_0 + B y_0 + C|}{\sqrt{A^2 + B^2}}" />

The distance is zero if and only if $P_0 \in \ell$.

</Definicao>

<Insight>

**Why is the slope equal to $\tan\alpha$?** If you walk 1 unit horizontally, you rise $m$ units vertically. The triangle formed has horizontal leg 1 and vertical leg $m$. The tangent of angle $\alpha$ with the horizontal is exactly $m/1 = m$. Geometry and trigonometry together — that is Analytic Geometry.

</Insight>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

Imagine a **playground slide**. It goes up or down smoothly — always with the same steepness. That is a **line**.

To describe the slide with numbers, you only need to know two things:

1. **Where it starts on the vertical axis.** We call this the y-intercept.
2. **How steep it is.** For each step forward, how many steps up does it go? We call this the slope.

With these two pieces of information, you know where the slide is anywhere — without needing to look.

**Example:** a slide starts at height 3 and rises 2 meters for each meter it advances. The equation is $y = 2x + 3$.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

A line in the coordinate plane is described by the equation $y = mx + n$.

- The **$m$** is the **slope**: it says how much $y$ goes up (or down) each time $x$ increases by 1.
- The **$n$** is where the line **crosses the $y$ axis** — the point $(0, n)$.

**Real example:** taxi app fare. Fixed charge of $5, plus $2 per kilometer traveled. The cost equation is:

$$C = 2x + 5$$

where $x$ is distance in kilometers. When $x = 0$, you pay $5 (just the base fare). When $x = 10$, you pay $25.

**How to find the line through two points:**

Given the points $(1, 4)$ and $(3, 10)$:

1. Calculate the slope: $m = (10 - 4)/(3 - 1) = 6/2 = 3$.
2. Use the equation $y = 3x + n$ and substitute a point: $4 = 3 \cdot 1 + n$, so $n = 1$.
3. Final equation: $y = 3x + 1$.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old">

The equation of a line is the bridge between algebra and geometry. Each line in the plane has an equation that describes **all and only** its points.

**Slope.** $m = \tan\alpha$, where $\alpha \in [0°, 180°)$ is the angle the line makes with the positive x-axis.

- $m > 0$: line increasing (rises from left to right).
- $m < 0$: line decreasing (falls).
- $m = 0$: horizontal line.
- Vertical line: $m$ does not exist (the tangent of $90°$ is infinite).

**General form.** $Ax + By + C = 0$ treats $x$ and $y$ symmetrically. It is the only form that represents vertical lines ($B = 0$, $x = -C/A$) without issues.

**Complete example.** Line through $(2, 3)$ and $(5, 9)$:

$$m = \frac{9 - 3}{5 - 2} = \frac{6}{3} = 2$$

Using point-slope with $(2, 3)$:

$$y - 3 = 2(x - 2) \implies y = 2x - 1$$

General form: $2x - y - 1 = 0$.

**Connection to affine function (Lesson 3).** $f(x) = ax + b$ is the same as $y = mx + n$, with $a = m$ and $b = n$. The line **is** the graph of the affine function.

<Insight>

**Parallel and perpendicular at your fingertips.** Line parallel to $y = 2x + 1$ passing through $(0, 5)$: same $m = 2$, change $n$: $y = 2x + 5$. Line perpendicular to $y = 2x + 1$ passing through $(0, 5)$: swap $m$ for $-1/2$: $y = -\tfrac{1}{2}x + 5$. Confirm: $2 \times (-1/2) = -1$. Correct.

</Insight>

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

The equation of a line is the foundation of all linear analysis. In $\mathbb{R}^n$, the analog is the **hyperplane** $\mathbf{a}^\top \mathbf{x} = b$.

### Simple linear regression

Given $n$ pairs $(x_i, y_i)$, the least-squares line $\hat{y} = \hat{m}x + \hat{n}$ (OLS) has estimators:

$$\hat{m} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})^2}, \qquad \hat{n} = \bar{y} - \hat{m}\bar{x}$$

The Pearson correlation coefficient $r = \hat{m} \cdot s_x / s_y$ measures the fit quality; $r^2$ is the proportion of variance in $y$ explained by $x$.

### Linear programming

Each linear constraint $a_1 x + a_2 y \leq b$ defines a half-plane. The feasible set is the finite intersection of half-planes — a convex polytope. The simplex traverses vertices of the polytope to optimality.

### Point-to-line distance and projections

For $\ell: Ax + By + C = 0$, the distance formula

$$d(P_0, \ell) = \frac{|Ax_0 + By_0 + C|}{\sqrt{A^2 + B^2}}$$

comes from vector projection: the vector $\mathbf{n} = (A, B)$ is normal to $\ell$, and the scalar projection of $\overrightarrow{P_1 P_0}$ onto $\hat{\mathbf{n}}$ gives the distance.

### Kinematics and rectilinear motion

Position $s(t) = s_0 + vt$ — a line in the $(t, s)$ plane with $m = v$ (velocity) and $n = s_0$ (initial position). Slope = time derivative. This will be the first formal motivation for the concept of derivative (Year 2, Term 5).

### Hesse normal form

$$x\cos\theta + y\sin\theta = p, \quad p \geq 0$$

where $p$ is the distance from the origin to the line and $\theta$ is the angle of the normal with the x-axis. Used in computational geometry and computer vision (RANSAC, Hough transform).

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

Line = 1-dimensional affine subspace of $\mathbb{R}^n$. All of modern projective geometry begins here.

### Projective vision

In homogeneous coordinates $(X : Y : W)$ with $x = X/W$, $y = Y/W$, the line becomes $AX + BY + CW = 0$ — pure linear form. Points at infinity ($W = 0$) become accessible. Two parallel lines in the affine plane meet at a point at infinity in the projective plane. Foundation of OpenGL, ray tracing, and all 3D perspective transformation (pinhole camera, Zhang calibration).

### Projective duality

In the projective plane $\mathbb{P}^2$, points and lines are dual: every theorem about points has a version about lines by swapping "passes through" $\leftrightarrow$ "contains". Pascal $\leftrightarrow$ Brianchon. Crucial in algebraic geometry and curve theory.

### Applications in ML and quantitative finance

- **Linear regression:** $\hat{y} = X\beta$. Normal system $X^\top X \beta = X^\top y$ solves via QR or SVD (numerically stable; never invert $X^\top X$ directly).
- **SVM:** the optimal classifier is the maximum-margin hyperplane. Margin $= 2 / \|\mathbf{w}\|$. Quadratic dual via Lagrange multipliers.
- **CAPM:** $E[R_i] = R_f + \beta_i (E[R_m] - R_f)$ — line in the $(\beta, E[R])$ plane. The slope is the market premium. Fama-French extends to hyperplane in three or more factors.
- **PCA:** projections onto hyperplanes that minimize residual variance. The first principal component is the best-fit line in $\mathbb{R}^p$ by orthogonal least squares.

### Numerical stability

In IEEE 754 floating-point, nearly vertical lines ($|m| \gg 1$) suffer catastrophic cancellation in $\Delta y = y_2 - y_1$ when $y_1 \approx y_2$. Use the general form $Ax + By + C = 0$ with unit $(A, B)$. Robust computational geometry (CGAL, Shewchuk predicates) uses arbitrary-precision arithmetic to avoid incorrect orientations.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practical application — how to build any line equation">

**Quick recipe: choose the path by what you have.**

| Information available | Strategy | Resulting form |
|---|---|---|
| 2 points $(x_1, y_1)$ and $(x_2, y_2)$ | $m = \Delta y / \Delta x$, then point-slope | slope-intercept |
| 1 point + slope $m$ | $y - y_0 = m(x - x_0)$ directly | point-slope |
| Intercepts $(p, 0)$ and $(0, q)$ | $x/p + y/q = 1$ | intercept |
| General form $\to$ slope-intercept | $y = -A/B \cdot x - C/B$ | slope-intercept |
| Line parallel to $y = mx + n$ through $(x_0, y_0)$ | same $m$, calculate new $n$ | slope-intercept |
| Line perpendicular to $y = mx + n$ through $(x_0, y_0)$ | swap $m$ for $-1/m$ | slope-intercept |

### Step-by-step algorithm (two points)

Given $(1, 2)$ and $(4, 8)$:

1. $m = (8 - 2)/(4 - 1) = 6/3 = 2$.
2. Point-slope with $(1, 2)$: $y - 2 = 2(x - 1)$.
3. Expand: $y = 2x$.
4. Check with $(4, 8)$: $2 \cdot 4 = 8$. Correct.

### Everyday applications with US data

**Electricity bill (US average, 2026 estimate):** flat rate $35 plus $0.95 per kWh. Monthly cost $C(q) = 0.95q + 35$, where $q$ is consumption in kWh. With $q = 200$ kWh: $C = 225$ dollars. Slope $m = 0.95$ is the marginal cost per kWh.

**Celsius to Fahrenheit conversion:** The line passes through $(0, 32)$ and $(100, 212)$. Slope $m = (212 - 32)/100 = 1.8$. Equation: $F = 1.8\,C + 32$.

**Accessible ramp (ADA standards):** Maximum allowable slope is $m \leq 1/12 \approx 8.33\%$. A ramp 1.2 m high requires minimum length $L = 1.2 / (1/12) = 14.4$ m.

</Porta>

## Worked Examples

<Exemplo titulo="Equation through two points (application)" numero="1">

**Problem.** Find the equation of the line passing through points $P_1 = (1, 2)$ and $P_2 = (4, 8)$.

**Strategy.** With two points, calculate the slope $m = \Delta y / \Delta x$ and use point-slope form.

**Solution.**

1. **Slope:**

$$m = \frac{8 - 2}{4 - 1} = \frac{6}{3} = 2$$

2. **Point-slope form** with $(x_0, y_0) = (1, 2)$:

$$y - 2 = 2(x - 1) \implies y = 2x$$

3. **General form:** $2x - y = 0$.

**Check.** For $P_2 = (4, 8)$: $y = 2 \cdot 4 = 8$. Correct.

**Source.** [OpenStax College Algebra 2e, §4.1, example 4.1.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/4-1-linear-functions) — CC-BY 4.0.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Convert general form to slope-intercept (intermediate)" numero="2">

**Problem.** Convert $3x - 4y + 12 = 0$ to slope-intercept form. Identify the slope, the y-intercept, and the two axis intercepts.

**Strategy.** Isolate $y$ to get slope-intercept form; then set $x = 0$ and $y = 0$ for intercepts.

**Solution.**

1. **Isolate $y$:**

$$3x - 4y + 12 = 0 \implies 4y = 3x + 12 \implies y = \frac{3}{4}x + 3$$

2. **Identifications:** $m = 3/4$; y-intercept: $(0, 3)$.

3. **x-intercept** (set $y = 0$):

$$0 = \frac{3}{4}x + 3 \implies x = -4$$

x-intercept: $(-4, 0)$.

**Check.** $(0, 3)$: $3(0) - 4(3) + 12 = 0$. $(-4, 0)$: $3(-4) - 4(0) + 12 = 0$. Both correct.

**Source.** [Stitz–Zeager Precalculus, §2.1, example 2.1.2](https://www.stitz-zeager.com/szprecalculus07042013.pdf) — CC-BY-NC-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Parallel and perpendicular lines (intermediate)" numero="3">

**Problem.** Given $\ell: y = 2x - 3$, find the line parallel to $\ell$ through $Q = (1, 5)$ and the line perpendicular to $\ell$ through $Q$.

**Strategy.** Parallel lines have the same slope. Perpendicular: $m_\perp = -1/m$.

**Solution.**

(a) **Parallel:** $m = 2$. Point-slope with $(1, 5)$:

$$y - 5 = 2(x - 1) \implies y = 2x + 3$$

(b) **Perpendicular:** $m_\perp = -1/2$. Point-slope with $(1, 5)$:

$$y - 5 = -\frac{1}{2}(x - 1) \implies y = -\frac{1}{2}x + \frac{11}{2}$$

**Check.** Product of slopes: $2 \times (-1/2) = -1$. Correct.

**Source.** [Stitz–Zeager Precalculus, §2.1, exercises 23–28](https://www.stitz-zeager.com/szprecalculus07042013.pdf) — CC-BY-NC-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Linear modeling — t-shirt break-even (modeling)" numero="4">

**Problem.** A small business has fixed cost $400/month and variable cost $12 per unit. The selling price is $30/unit. (a) Write the total cost $C(q)$ and revenue $R(q)$ lines. (b) Find the break-even point.

**Strategy.** Cost and revenue are linear functions of $q$; break-even occurs when $C(q) = R(q)$.

**Solution.**

(a) $C(q) = 12q + 400$ and $R(q) = 30q$.

(b) At break-even:

$$30q = 12q + 400 \implies 18q = 400 \implies q \approx 22.2$$

Since $q$ must be an integer, at least **23 units** are needed. With $q = 23$: $R = 690$, $C = 676$, profit $14.

**Check.** Slopes: $m_C = 12$ (marginal cost) and $m_R = 30$ (marginal revenue). Since $m_R > m_C$, the business makes money after break-even.

**Source.** [OpenStax College Algebra 2e, §4.2, example 4.2.2](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/4-2-modeling-with-linear-functions) — CC-BY 4.0.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Point-to-line distance and closest point (challenge)" numero="5">

**Problem.** A road follows the line $\ell: 3x - 4y + 25 = 0$. A store is at $P = (2, -1)$. (a) Calculate the distance from the store to the road. (b) Find the point on the road closest to the store.

**Strategy.** Use the point-to-line distance formula and, for the closest point, find the intersection of the perpendicular through $P$ with $\ell$.

**Solution.**

(a) **Distance:**

$$d = \frac{|3(2) - 4(-1) + 25|}{\sqrt{9 + 16}} = \frac{|6 + 4 + 25|}{5} = \frac{35}{5} = 7$$

(b) **Closest point.** The perpendicular to $\ell$ through $P = (2, -1)$ has normal direction $(A, B) = (3, -4)$, so slope $-4/3$:

$$y + 1 = -\frac{4}{3}(x - 2) \implies y = -\frac{4}{3}x + \frac{5}{3}$$

System with $\ell$ (slope-intercept form $y = \frac{3}{4}x + \frac{25}{4}$):

$$-\frac{4}{3}x + \frac{5}{3} = \frac{3}{4}x + \frac{25}{4}$$

Multiply by 12: $-16x + 20 = 9x + 75 \implies 25x = -55 \implies x = -\dfrac{11}{5}$.

$$y = \frac{3}{4}\left(-\frac{11}{5}\right) + \frac{25}{4} = \frac{-33 + 125}{20} = \frac{92}{20} = \frac{23}{5}$$

Closest point: $\left(-\dfrac{11}{5},\ \dfrac{23}{5}\right)$.

**Check.** Distance from $P$ to this point: $\sqrt{(2 + 11/5)^2 + (-1 - 23/5)^2} = \sqrt{(21/5)^2 + (28/5)^2} = \frac{1}{5}\sqrt{441 + 784} = \frac{35}{5} = 7$. Confirmed.
