### Plane analytic geometry

<Definicao titulo="Fundamental formulas">

Given points $P_1 = (x_1, y_1)$ and $P_2 = (x_2, y_2)$ in the plane $\mathbb{R}^2$:

<Equation explicacao="Euclidean distance between two points — the planar case of the Pythagorean theorem." latex="d(P_1, P_2) = \sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2}" />

<Equation explicacao="Midpoint of segment P1P2 — arithmetic average coordinate by coordinate." latex="M = \left(\frac{x_1 + x_2}{2},\; \frac{y_1 + y_2}{2}\right)" />

<Equation explicacao="General line equation. Normal vector: n = (A, B). Direction vector: d = (−B, A). The sign of Ax₀ + By₀ + C indicates which half-plane contains point P₀." latex="Ax + By + C = 0, \quad \text{dist}(P_0, r) = \frac{|Ax_0 + By_0 + C|}{\sqrt{A^2 + B^2}}" />

</Definicao>

> "The equation $Ax + By = C$ represents a line in the plane whenever $A$ and $B$ are not both zero. If $B \neq 0$, we can solve for $y$ to obtain the slope-intercept form $y = mx + b$; if $B = 0$, the line is vertical." — [OpenStax College Algebra 2e, §2.2](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/2-2-linear-equations-in-one-variable)

<Definicao titulo="Relative position of two lines">

| Case | Algebraic condition |
|---|---|
| Coincident | <Eq>{`A_1{:}B_1{:}C_1`}</Eq> proportional to <Eq>{`A_2{:}B_2{:}C_2`}</Eq> |
| Parallel distinct | $A_1/A_2 = B_1/B_2 \neq C_1/C_2$ |
| Intersecting | $A_1 B_2 - A_2 B_1 \neq 0$ |
| Perpendicular | $m_1 m_2 = -1$ (or $A_1 A_2 + B_1 B_2 = 0$) |

</Definicao>

### Conics

<Definicao titulo="The four standard conics">

<Equation explicacao="Circle with center (a, b) and radius r. Special case of ellipse with eccentricity e = 0." latex="(x - a)^2 + (y - b)^2 = r^2" />

<Equation explicacao="Ellipse with major semiaxis a (horizontal) and minor semiaxis b. The focal distance c satisfies c² = a² − b²." latex="\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1, \quad a > b > 0" />

<Equation explicacao="Parabola with horizontal axis, focus at (p, 0), and directrix x = −p. If p < 0, it opens leftward." latex="y^2 = 4px" />

<Equation explicacao="Hyperbola with horizontal transverse axis. Asymptotes: y = ±(b/a)x. The focal distance c satisfies c² = a² + b²." latex="\frac{x^2}{a^2} - \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1" />

Eccentricity $e$ classifies: $e = 0$ (circle), $0 < e < 1$ (ellipse), $e = 1$ (parabola), $e > 1$ (hyperbola).

</Definicao>

### Vectors in the plane

<Definicao titulo="Dot product and projection">

<Equation explicacao="Dot product: combines magnitudes and angle θ between vectors. If positive: acute angle. If zero: perpendicular. If negative: obtuse angle." latex="\vec{u} \cdot \vec{v} = u_1 v_1 + u_2 v_2 = |\vec{u}|\,|\vec{v}|\cos\theta" />

<Equation explicacao="Vector projection of v onto u: the component of v in the direction of u, expressed as a scalar multiple of u." latex="\text{proj}_{\vec{u}} \vec{v} = \frac{\vec{u} \cdot \vec{v}}{|\vec{u}|^2}\,\vec{u}" />

<Equation explicacao="Work done by force F over displacement d. W > 0: force aids motion. W < 0: force opposes." latex="W = \vec{F} \cdot \vec{d} = |\vec{F}|\,|\vec{d}|\cos\theta \quad [\text{J} = \text{N}\cdot\text{m}]" />

</Definicao>

<Teorema titulo="Cauchy-Schwarz inequality in R²">

For any vectors $\vec{u}, \vec{v} \in \mathbb{R}^2$:

<Equation numero="CS" explicacao="The dot product never exceeds the product of magnitudes. Equality holds when vectors are parallel (θ = 0° or 180°)." latex="|\vec{u} \cdot \vec{v}| \leq |\vec{u}|\,|\vec{v}|" />

**Proof.** Since $\vec{u} \cdot \vec{v} = |\vec{u}||\vec{v}|\cos\theta$ and $|\cos\theta| \leq 1$, we have $|\vec{u} \cdot \vec{v}| = |\vec{u}||\vec{v}||\cos\theta| \leq |\vec{u}||\vec{v}|$. Equality holds when $|\cos\theta| = 1$, that is, $\theta = 0°$ or $180°$. $\square$

</Teorema>

### Linear systems

<Definicao titulo="Cramer's rule — 2×2 system">

For <Eq>{`\\begin{pmatrix} a_1 & b_1 \\\\ a_2 & b_2 \\end{pmatrix} \\begin{pmatrix} x \\\\ y \\end{pmatrix} = \\begin{pmatrix} c_1 \\\\ c_2 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq> with $D = a_1 b_2 - a_2 b_1 \neq 0$:

<Equation explicacao="Cramer's rule: each unknown is the ratio of the modified determinant to the main determinant. When D = 0, the system is parallel (no solution) or coincident (infinitely many)." latex="x = \frac{D_x}{D}, \quad y = \frac{D_y}{D}, \quad D_x = \begin{vmatrix} c_1 & b_1 \\ c_2 & b_2 \end{vmatrix}, \quad D_y = \begin{vmatrix} a_1 & c_1 \\ a_2 & c_2 \end{vmatrix}" />

</Definicao>

### Connections among topics

- **Relative position of lines $\Leftrightarrow$ 2×2 linear system**: coincident, parallel, single intersection map to $D = 0$ (infinitely many solutions), $D = 0$ (no solution), $D \neq 0$.
- **Tangent to circle** at point $P$ is the line perpendicular to radius $CP$ at $P$ — uses dot product (condition $\vec{CP} \cdot \vec{v}_{\text{tangent}} = 0$).
- **Direction vector** of line $Ax + By + C = 0$ is $\vec{d} = (-B, A)$; normal vector is $\vec{n} = (A, B)$.
- **Conics as quadratic forms**: every conic satisfies $Ax^2 + Bxy + Cy^2 + Dx + Ey + F = 0$. The discriminant $B^2 - 4AC$ classifies the conic.

<Insight>

The equation $Ax + By + C = 0$ is central because it controls the entire algebra of the plane: the sign of $Ax_0 + By_0 + C$ tells which side of the line point $P_0$ is on; the absolute value normalized by $\sqrt{A^2 + B^2}$ gives distance. Conics generalize this to degree 2 — and modern analytic geometry (computer graphics, GPS, machine learning) uses exactly these objects in higher dimensions.

</Insight>

Trimester 3 corresponds to "Coordinate Geometry" in the Anglo-Saxon curriculum, but there are richer layers that standard high school does not explore.

**Elementary linear algebra in disguise:**

| Trimester 3 object | Interpretation in Linear Algebra |
|---|---|
| Plane <Eq>{`\\mathbb{R}^2`}</Eq> | 2D vector space over <Eq>{`\\mathbb{R}`}</Eq> |
| Equation <Eq>{`Ax + By + C = 0`}</Eq> | affine hyperplane in <Eq>{`\\mathbb{R}^2`}</Eq> |
| Normal vector $(A, B)$ | gradient of the linear form $Ax + By$ |
| 2×2 system | invertible linear map (if $\det \neq 0$) |
| Dot product | symmetric bilinear form (Euclidean inner product) |
| Conic <Eq>{`\\mathbf{x}^T M \\mathbf{x} = 1`}</Eq> | quadratic form — Trimester 12 handles eigenvalues |
| Parallelism | linear dependence of normal vectors |

**Cross-domain connections:**
- **2D kinematics**: ballistic trajectory is parabola $y = x\tan\alpha - gx^2/(2v_0^2\cos^2\alpha)$.
- **Statics**: node equilibrium $\sum F_x = 0$, $\sum F_y = 0$ is 2×2 system with unknown tensions.
- **Machine learning**: cosine similarity $\cos\theta = \vec{u}\cdot\vec{v}/(|\vec{u}||\vec{v}|)$ is normalized dot product in $\mathbb{R}^n$.
- **Markowitz** (1952, [Nobel 1990](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/1990/)): efficient frontier is a hyperbola in risk-return space.
- **Computer graphics**: affine transformations (translation, rotation, scaling) are $3 \times 3$ matrices in homogeneous coordinates.

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

Descartes (1637, *La Géométrie*) united geometry and algebra via the coordinate plane. What we teach in Trimester 3 is essentially that fusion — but with 400 years of refinement.

**Conics as quadratic forms.** Every conic satisfies $Ax^2 + Bxy + Cy^2 + Dx + Ey + F = 0$. The discriminant $B^2 - 4AC$ classifies: negative for ellipse (special case $A = C$, $B = 0$: circle), zero for parabola, positive for hyperbola. Diagonalization of the matrix <Eq>{`\\begin{pmatrix} A & B/2 \\\\ B/2 & C \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq> by eigenvalues removes the cross term $Bxy$ and reduces to canonical form — this is Applied Linear Algebra (Trimester 12).

**Dot product as inner product.** The dot product in $\mathbb{R}^2$ is the case $n = 2$ of the Euclidean inner product $\langle \vec{u}, \vec{v} \rangle = \sum_i u_i v_i$. The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality $|\langle \vec{u}, \vec{v} \rangle| \leq |\vec{u}||\vec{v}|$ is the foundation of all functional analysis. In finance, [Black-Scholes-Merton (Nobel 1997)](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/1997/) uses Euclidean distance in $(\ln S, t)$ to transform the parabolic PDE into the heat equation.

**Linear systems in context.** Solving $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ via Cramer's rule is $O(n!)$ — impractical for large $n$. In professional practice: $n \leq 3$ uses Cramer or Gaussian elimination; $n \sim 10^3$ uses LU factorization ($O(n^3)$); $n \sim 10^6$ uses iterative methods (conjugate gradient, GMRES); $n \sim 10^9$ (neural networks) uses stochastic gradient descent. What the student learns now is the **alphabet** — the elementary operations that underpin all industrial algorithms.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practice — how to solve integrating problems">

### Recipe in 5 steps

1. **Read twice** — identify what is asked and what is given.
2. **List the topics**: line, circle, vector, system? Usually 2 or 3.
3. **Sketch** the situation in the coordinate plane.
4. **Write equations** for each constraint (one per line).
5. **Solve and interpret** — verify that your answer makes sense in the sketch.

### Common pitfalls

- **Confusing reduced and general form of a line**: $y = mx + n$ does not work for vertical lines ($x = k$); the form $Ax + By + C = 0$ covers all cases.
- **Forgetting to check relative position** before solving system: no solution system implies parallel lines; infinitely many solutions implies coincident lines.
- **Sign of dot product**: $\vec{u} \cdot \vec{v} < 0$ implies obtuse angle, not calculation error.
- **Physical units**: work in J = N·m; always check dimensional consistency.
- **Ellipse vs. circle**: circle is an ellipse with $a = b$; do not mix eccentricity formulas.

### Quick verification example (SymPy)

```python
import sympy as sp
x, y = sp.symbols('x y')

# Intersection of line with circle
line   = sp.Eq(y, 2*x - 1)
circle = sp.Eq(x**2 + y**2, 25)
print(sp.solve([line, circle], [x, y]))

# 2×2 system via Cramer
A = sp.Matrix([[3, -1], [1, 2]])
b = sp.Matrix([7, 4])
print(A.inv() @ b)
```

### What to do if stuck

Analytic geometry is cumulative. If an integrating problem gets stuck, go back to the specific lesson on the topic that blocks you. Identify the gap, close it, return to the integrating exercise.

## Worked examples

<Exemplo titulo="Line equation and point-to-line distance">

**Problem.** The line $r$ passes through $A = (1, 3)$ and $B = (4, 9)$. (a) Determine the general equation of $r$. (b) Calculate the distance from point $P = (0, 0)$ to line $r$.

**Strategy.** Use the slope formula to obtain the slope-intercept equation, convert to general form, and apply the point-to-line distance formula.

**Solution.**

(a) Slope: $m = (9 - 3)/(4 - 1) = 6/3 = 2$.

Slope-intercept equation: $y - 3 = 2(x - 1) \Rightarrow y = 2x + 1$.

General form: $2x - y + 1 = 0$. Thus $A = 2$, $B = -1$, $C = 1$.

(b) Distance from $P = (0,0)$ to line $2x - y + 1 = 0$:

$$d = \frac{|2 \cdot 0 + (-1) \cdot 0 + 1|}{\sqrt{2^2 + (-1)^2}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{5}} = \frac{\sqrt{5}}{5}$$

**Verification.** The midpoint of $AB$ is $M = (5/2,\; 6)$. Check that $M$ lies on the line: $2 \cdot 5/2 - 6 + 1 = 5 - 6 + 1 = 0$. Correct.

**Source.** [Stitz–Zeager Precalculus §2.1](https://stitz-zeager.com/szprecalc07042013.pdf), p. 175 — CC-BY-NC-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Relative position of line and circle">

**Problem.** Determine the intersections of line $y = x + 1$ with circle $x^2 + y^2 = 25$. Classify the relative position.

**Strategy.** Substitute the line equation into the circle equation to get a quadratic in $x$. The discriminant $\Delta$ classifies the position.

**Solution.**

Substitute $y = x + 1$:

$$x^2 + (x+1)^2 = 25 \Rightarrow 2x^2 + 2x - 24 = 0 \Rightarrow x^2 + x - 12 = 0$$

Quadratic formula: $x = (-1 \pm \sqrt{1 + 48})/2 = (-1 \pm 7)/2$.

$x_1 = 3 \Rightarrow y_1 = 4$. Point $P_1 = (3, 4)$.

$x_2 = -4 \Rightarrow y_2 = -3$. Point $P_2 = (-4, -3)$.

$\Delta = 49 > 0$: line is **secant** to the circle (two intersection points).

**Verification.** $3^2 + 4^2 = 25$ and $(-4)^2 + (-3)^2 = 25$. Both lie on the circle.

**Source.** [OpenStax Algebra and Trigonometry 2e §10.1](https://openstax.org/books/algebra-and-trigonometry-2e/pages/10-1-the-ellipse), ex. 9 — CC-BY 4.0.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Dot product and angle between vectors">

**Problem.** Given $\vec{u} = (3, 4)$ and $\vec{v} = (1, -2)$. (a) Calculate $\vec{u} \cdot \vec{v}$. (b) Find the angle $\theta$ between them. (c) Calculate the vector projection of $\vec{v}$ onto $\vec{u}$.

**Strategy.** Apply directly the formulas for dot product, arccosine, and vector projection. Verify with the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality.

**Solution.**

(a) $\vec{u} \cdot \vec{v} = 3 \cdot 1 + 4 \cdot (-2) = 3 - 8 = -5$.

(b) $|\vec{u}| = \sqrt{9 + 16} = 5$. $|\vec{v}| = \sqrt{1 + 4} = \sqrt{5}$.

$$\cos\theta = \frac{-5}{5\sqrt{5}} = \frac{-1}{\sqrt{5}} \approx -0.447 \Rightarrow \theta \approx 116.6°$$

The angle is obtuse (negative dot product).

(c) Vector projection of $\vec{v}$ onto $\vec{u}$:

$$\text{proj}_{\vec{u}} \vec{v} = \frac{\vec{u} \cdot \vec{v}}{|\vec{u}|^2}\,\vec{u} = \frac{-5}{25}(3, 4) = \left(-\frac{3}{5},\; -\frac{4}{5}\right)$$

**Verification.** $|\vec{u} \cdot \vec{v}| = 5 \leq |\vec{u}||\vec{v}| = 5\sqrt{5} \approx 11.18$. Cauchy-Schwarz satisfied.

**Source.** [OpenStax Algebra and Trigonometry 2e §8.8](https://openstax.org/books/algebra-and-trigonometry-2e/pages/8-8-vectors), ex. 21 — CC-BY 4.0.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Work and force equilibrium">

**Problem.** A box of 10 kg rests on an inclined plane at $30°$ to the horizontal. (a) Decompose weight $\vec{P}$ into components parallel and perpendicular to the plane. (b) What is the minimum friction force to maintain rest?

**Strategy.** Decompose the weight vector using projections onto axes parallel and perpendicular to the inclined plane.

**Solution.**

Weight: $|\vec{P}| = mg = 10 \times 9.8 = 98$ N.

(a) Component parallel to plane (downslope): $P_\parallel = 98\sin 30° = 49$ N.

Component perpendicular (pressure on plane): $P_\perp = 98\cos 30° = 98 \cdot \sqrt{3}/2 \approx 84.9$ N.

(b) For equilibrium: friction $f = P_\parallel = 49$ N, directed upslope.

**Verification.** $P_\parallel^2 + P_\perp^2 = 49^2 + 84.9^2 \approx 2401 + 7208 = 9609 \approx 98^2 = 9604$. Consistent (difference due to rounding of $\sqrt{3}$).

**Source.** [OpenStax University Physics Volume 1 §5.7](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/5-7-drawing-free-body-diagrams), ex. 19 — CC-BY 4.0.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Integrating problem — line, circle, and tangency">

**Problem.** Segment $AB$ with $A = (0, 0)$ and $B = (6, 8)$ is a diameter of circle $\mathcal{C}$. A line $r: y = -x + k$ is tangent to $\mathcal{C}$. Find the possible values of $k$.

**Strategy.** Calculate center and radius of the circle from the diameter. Use the tangency condition: distance from center to line equals the radius.

**Solution.**

**Step 1 — Center and radius.** Center: midpoint of $AB = (3, 4)$. Radius: $r = d(A, O) = \sqrt{9 + 16} = 5$.

**Step 2 — Tangency condition.** The line $r: x + y - k = 0$ has $A = B = 1$. Distance from center $(3, 4)$ to line:

$$d = \frac{|3 + 4 - k|}{\sqrt{2}} = 5 \Rightarrow |7 - k| = 5\sqrt{2}$$

**Step 3 — Solve for $k$.** $7 - k = \pm 5\sqrt{2}$, so $k_1 = 7 - 5\sqrt{2}$ and $k_2 = 7 + 5\sqrt{2}$.

There are two parallel tangent lines with equations $y = -x + (7 - 5\sqrt{2})$ and $y = -x + (7 + 5\sqrt{2})$.

**Verification.** Distance between parallel tangents: $|k_2 - k_1|/\sqrt{2} = 10\sqrt{2}/\sqrt{2} = 10 = 2r$. Correct.

**Source.** [Stitz–Zeager Precalculus §7.2](https://stitz-zeager.com/szprecalc07042013.pdf), p. 504 — CC-BY-NC-SA.

</Exemplo>
