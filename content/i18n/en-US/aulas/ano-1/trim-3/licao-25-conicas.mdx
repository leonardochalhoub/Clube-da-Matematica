### Unified definition by focus and directrix

<Definicao titulo="Conic (focus-directrix definition)">
A **conic** is the locus of points $P$ in the plane such that the ratio between the distance to a fixed point $F$ (focus) and the distance to a fixed line $\ell$ (directrix) is a constant $e \geq 0$:

$$\frac{|PF|}{d(P, \ell)} = e \quad \text{(excentricidade)}$$

| Conic | Eccentricity | Geometric condition |
|---|---|---|
| Circle | $e = 0$ | directrix at infinity |
| Ellipse | $0 < e < 1$ | focus interior to the curve |
| Parabola | $e = 1$ | focus and vertex equidistant from the directrix |
| Hyperbola | $e > 1$ | two separate branches |

</Definicao>

> "A conic is the curve of intersection of a cone with a plane, or equivalently, the locus with a constant ratio between the distance to a focus and the distance to a directrix." — [Stitz–Zeager Precalculus §7.1](https://www.stitz-zeager.com/szprecalculus07042013.pdf)

---

### Ellipse

<Definicao titulo="Ellipse">
The locus of points whose **sum** of distances to two foci is a constant $2a$:
$$|PF_1| + |PF_2| = 2a, \quad a > 0$$

**Horizontal major axis** (foci at $(\pm c, 0)$, center at the origin, $a > b > 0$):

<Equation explicacao="Canonical ellipse with major axis along the x-axis. The value c satisfies c² = a² - b²." latex="\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1, \quad a > b > 0" />

**Vertical major axis** (foci at $(0, \pm c)$):

<Equation explicacao="Ellipse with vertical major axis — the larger denominator is under y²." latex="\frac{x^2}{b^2} + \frac{y^2}{a^2} = 1, \quad a > b > 0" />

**Centered at $(h, k)$:**

<Equation explicacao="Translated ellipse. Complete the squares to reduce to canonical form." latex="\frac{(x-h)^2}{a^2} + \frac{(y-k)^2}{b^2} = 1" />

**Fundamental relations:**

<Equation explicacao="For the ellipse: b² = a² - c². Eccentricity between 0 and 1." latex="c^2 = a^2 - b^2, \quad e = \frac{c}{a} \in (0, 1)" />

- Major-axis vertices: $(\pm a, 0)$; minor axis: $(0, \pm b)$.
- Distance between foci: $2c$. Length of the major axis: $2a$.
- Latus rectum (focal chord perpendicular to the major axis): $\ell_r = 2b^2/a$.

</Definicao>

---

### Parabola

<Definicao titulo="Parabola">
The locus of points **equidistant** from a focus $F$ and a directrix $\ell$:
$$|PF| = d(P, \ell), \quad e = 1$$

**Horizontal axis** (opens to the right if $p > 0$):

<Equation explicacao="Canonical parabola with vertex at the origin. Focus at (p, 0), directrix x = -p." latex="y^2 = 4px" />

**Vertical axis** (opens upward if $p > 0$):

<Equation explicacao="Canonical parabola with vertex at the origin. Focus at (0, p), directrix y = -p." latex="x^2 = 4py" />

**Vertex at $(h, k)$:**

<Equation explicacao="Translated parabola. Complete the square in the quadratic variable to obtain this form." latex="(y - k)^2 = 4p(x - h) \quad \text{ou} \quad (x - h)^2 = 4p(y - k)" />

- Latus rectum: $|4p|$.
- Sign of $p$: $p > 0$ opens right/up; $p < 0$ opens left/down.

</Definicao>

---

### Hyperbola

<Definicao titulo="Hyperbola">
The locus of points whose **absolute difference** of distances to two foci is a constant $2a$:
$$\bigl||PF_1| - |PF_2|\bigr| = 2a, \quad a > 0$$

**Horizontal transverse axis** (foci at $(\pm c, 0)$):

<Equation explicacao="Hyperbola with branches opening left and right. For the hyperbola: c² = a² + b²." latex="\frac{x^2}{a^2} - \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1" />

**Vertical transverse axis** (foci at $(0, \pm c)$):

<Equation explicacao="Hyperbola with branches opening up and down." latex="\frac{y^2}{a^2} - \frac{x^2}{b^2} = 1" />

**Fundamental relations:**

<Equation explicacao="For the hyperbola: c² = a² + b², unlike the ellipse. Eccentricity greater than 1." latex="c^2 = a^2 + b^2, \quad e = \frac{c}{a} > 1" />

- Vertices: $(\pm a, 0)$. Asymptotes: $y = \pm (b/a)x$ (horizontal hyperbola).
- Latus rectum: $2b^2/a$.

</Definicao>

---

### General form and classification by discriminant

<Definicao titulo="General degree-2 conic equation">

Every conic satisfies $Ax^2 + Bxy + Cy^2 + Dx + Ey + F = 0$ with $\Delta = B^2 - 4AC$:

| Discriminant | Type (no rotation, $B = 0$) |
|---|---|
| $\Delta < 0$ | Ellipse (or circle if $A = C$ and $B = 0$) |
| $\Delta = 0$ | Parabola |
| $\Delta > 0$ | Hyperbola |

</Definicao>

<Insight>
The **reflection** property unifies the conics in engineering: in an ellipse, any ray leaving $F_1$ reflects to $F_2$. In a parabola, rays parallel to the axis reflect to the focus. In a hyperbola, rays directed at $F_1$ reflect as if they came from $F_2$. All reflecting telescopes, parabolic antennas, and whispering galleries exploit these properties.
</Insight>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

Grab a **flashlight** and point the light at the floor. It makes a **circle** of light.

Now **tilt** the flashlight a little. The circle becomes a **little egg** — that's an **ellipse**.

Tilt it **a lot more**: the little egg gets longer and longer. When it stretches off to infinity on one side, it's a **parabola**.

Tilt it **even more**: the light splits into **two curves** that go in different directions — that's a **hyperbola**.

These are the four **conics**. The name comes from the fact that if you cut an **ice-cream cone** (a cone) with a straight knife, the four shapes that appear are exactly these.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

The **conics** appear when you cut a cone with a plane:

1. **Circle**: horizontal cut.
2. **Ellipse**: tilted cut — egg shape.
3. **Parabola**: cut parallel to one side of the cone.
4. **Hyperbola**: a cut that catches **both** sides of the double cone (two "hats" stacked tip to tip).

**Where they appear:**
- The **Earth's orbit** around the Sun is an **ellipse** (Kepler, 1609).
- The trajectory of a **kicked ball** (no wind) is a **parabola**.
- The **satellite TV dish**: every signal arriving parallel to the axis reflects to a single point — the focus. The converter sits exactly there.
- **LORAN** (radio navigation): the time difference between two signals defines a hyperbola.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old teen">

Conics were studied by **Apollonius of Perga** around 200 BC — two thousand years before any application in physics. Kepler (1609) discovered that planets follow **ellipses**, and Newton (1687) explained why with the law of universal gravitation.

**The 4 conics and their equations (origin at center/vertex):**

| Conic | Canonical equation | Eccentricity $e$ |
|---|---|---|
| Circle | $x^2 + y^2 = r^2$ | $e = 0$ |
| Ellipse | $x^2/a^2 + y^2/b^2 = 1$ | $0 < e < 1$ |
| Parabola | $y^2 = 4px$ | $e = 1$ |
| Hyperbola | $x^2/a^2 - y^2/b^2 = 1$ | $e > 1$ |

**Eccentricity $e = c/a$**: measures how "flattened" the ellipse is. Earth: $e \approx 0{,}017$ (nearly circular). Halley's Comet: $e \approx 0{,}967$ (highly eccentric).

**Numerical example — ellipse $x^2/25 + y^2/9 = 1$:**
$a = 5$, $b = 3$, $c = \sqrt{25 - 9} = 4$. Foci: $(\pm 4, 0)$. Eccentricity: $e = 4/5 = 0{,}8$.

**Numerical example — parabola $y^2 = 12x$:**
$4p = 12 \Rightarrow p = 3$. Focus: $(3, 0)$. Directrix: $x = -3$.

**Numerical example — hyperbola $x^2/4 - y^2/9 = 1$:**
$a = 2$, $b = 3$, $c = \sqrt{13}$. Asymptotes: $y = \pm(3/2)x$.

<Insight>
**Why does the ellipse have two foci?** Because it is defined by the **sum** of distances to two fixed points. If you pin two tacks to a sheet of paper, tie a string around them, and pull with a pencil keeping the string taut, you draw a perfect ellipse. The two tacks are the foci; the length of the string is $2a$.
</Insight>

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

Conics are the analytic solutions of the two-body problem with a force $F \propto 1/r^2$ (gravitation, Coulomb). Every gravitational trajectory is a conic — the total energy determines which one.

### Derivation of the canonical ellipse equation

Foci $F_1 = (-c, 0)$ and $F_2 = (c, 0)$, sum $= 2a$. From the definition:

$$\sqrt{(x+c)^2 + y^2} + \sqrt{(x-c)^2 + y^2} = 2a$$

Isolating the second root and squaring:

$$a^2 - cx = a\sqrt{(x-c)^2 + y^2}$$

Squaring again and rearranging:

$$\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{a^2 - c^2} = 1 \quad \Rightarrow \quad \frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1, \quad b^2 = a^2 - c^2$$

### Applications by conic

**Ellipse:**
- Orbits of artificial satellites and planets (Kepler's 1st Law).
- Whispering gallery: an elliptical ceiling reflects sound from $F_1$ to $F_2$ — the U.S. Capitol.
- Frequency filters (wavelet transform space).
- Covariance ellipse in the Kalman Filter: $\mathbf{x}^T P^{-1} \mathbf{x} = 1$.

**Parabola:**
- Satellite dish antenna: reflection of rays parallel to the focus.
- Reflecting telescope (Newton, 1668) — parabolic primary mirror.
- Ballistic trajectory (without air resistance).
- Least squares method: the squared-error surface is a parabola (or a paraboloid in $\mathbb{R}^n$).

**Hyperbola:**
- LORAN-C navigation system: the time-difference of arrival of signals defines a hyperbola; two hyperbolas determine position.
- Markowitz frontier: in the risk-return space $(\sigma, \mu)$, the efficient portfolio frontier is a **hyperbola**.
- Trajectory of a non-periodic comet: $e > 1$ (passes once and escapes).
- 'Oumuamua (2017): $e \approx 1{,}20$ — the first identified interstellar object.

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

Conics are **plane algebraic curves of degree 2** — the simplest case of algebraic geometry. In homogeneous coordinates $[X:Y:W]$, every conic is $\mathbf{x}^T M \mathbf{x} = 0$ with $M \in \text{Sym}(3, \mathbb{R})$.

### Projective view

The classification by Sylvester signature of $M$:
- Signature $(2,1)$ or $(1,2)$: ellipse/hyperbola.
- $\det M = 0$: degenerate conic (pair of lines or point).

In the projective plane $\mathbb{P}^2(\mathbb{R})$, ellipse, circle, and hyperbola are **projectively equivalent** — the distinction depends only on how the conic intersects the line at infinity $W = 0$: zero real points (ellipse), one point (parabola), two points (hyperbola).

### Confocal conics and elliptic coordinates

Families of ellipses and hyperbolas with the **same foci** form orthogonal nets — elliptic coordinates $(\mu, \nu)$:

$$x = c \cosh\mu \cos\nu, \quad y = c \sinh\mu \sin\nu$$

Fundamental in electrostatics, potential flow, and the quantum mechanics of the hydrogen atom.

### Quantitative applications

- **Markowitz frontier**: in the $(\sigma, \mu)$ plane, the efficient frontier is a hyperbola. The tangent line leaving $(0, r_f)$ (risk-free rate) is the Capital Market Line (CAPM).
- **Volatility smile**: in options, the implied volatility smile is often fitted by a parabola in strike (Gatheral's second-order model).
- **Kalman filter**: the covariance ellipse $\mathbf{x}^T P^{-1} \mathbf{x} = 1$ represents the uncertainty of the estimated state. The Bayesian update step produces a new ellipse of smaller area.
- **Trust-region in optimization**: each step is constrained to a Euclidean ball — the intersection of a level ellipse with a sphere, which yields a new ellipse.

### Apollonius and history

Apollonius of Perga (c. 262–190 BC) wrote *Conics* in 8 books — 7 survived. The names *ellipse* ("deficiency"), *parabola* ("comparison"), and *hyperbola* ("excess") come from the terms he used to describe the relation between the ordinate and the parameter of the section. Kepler's laws (1609), derived by Newton (1687) from the gravitational law $F \propto 1/r^2$, are direct applications of Book IV of Apollonius — two thousand years later.

The [2019 Nobel Prize in Physics](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/2019/summary/) was awarded to Michel Mayor and Didier Queloz for the discovery of the first exoplanet in an elliptical orbit around a solar-type star — confirming Kepler's laws beyond the Solar System for the first time.

### Recipe: identify and analyze any conic (no rotation, $B = 0$)

**Step 1 — Identify the type from the coefficients:**

| Equation structure | Conic |
|---|---|
| $x^2$ and $y^2$ with the same coefficient and sign | circle |
| $x^2$ and $y^2$ with different coefficients and the same sign | ellipse |
| Only $x^2$ (no $y^2$) or only $y^2$ (no $x^2$) | parabola |
| $x^2$ and $y^2$ with opposite signs | hyperbola |

**Step 2 — Complete the squares** in the quadratic variables to find the center/vertex.

**Step 3 — Compute $a, b, c$:**
- Ellipse: $b^2 = a^2 - c^2$.
- Hyperbola: $b^2 = c^2 - a^2$.
- Parabola: $4p =$ linear coefficient of the non-quadratic variable.

**Step 4 — Compute eccentricity** $e = c/a$ and identify foci, directrix, asymptotes.

### Complete example: off-origin conic

$4x^2 + 9y^2 - 16x + 54y + 61 = 0$:

$$4(x^2 - 4x) + 9(y^2 + 6y) = -61$$
$$4(x-2)^2 - 16 + 9(y+3)^2 - 81 = -61$$
$$\frac{(x-2)^2}{9} + \frac{(y+3)^2}{4} = 1$$

Ellipse: center $(2, -3)$, $a = 3$ (horizontal), $b = 2$, $c = \sqrt{5}$, $e = \sqrt{5}/3 \approx 0{,}745$.

### Special attention

- **Rotated conic** ($B \neq 0$): you must rotate the axes first. Angle: $\cot(2\theta) = (A-C)/B$.
- **Degenerate conic**: if $\det M = 0$, the equation represents a pair of lines, a point, or the empty set.
- **Sign of $p$ in the parabola**: $p > 0$ opens right/up; $p < 0$ opens left/down.
- **Ellipse or hyperbola?** Remember: in the ellipse $c^2 = a^2 - b^2$; in the hyperbola $c^2 = a^2 + b^2$. Swapping this sign is the most common error.

## Worked examples

<Exemplo titulo="Identify and extract the elements of an ellipse" numero="1">

**Problem:** For the ellipse $\dfrac{x^2}{25} + \dfrac{y^2}{9} = 1$, determine the center, vertices, foci, and eccentricity.

**Strategy:** Compare with the canonical form $x^2/a^2 + y^2/b^2 = 1$ and apply $c^2 = a^2 - b^2$.

**Solution:**

1. $a^2 = 25 \Rightarrow a = 5$; $b^2 = 9 \Rightarrow b = 3$. Since $a^2 > b^2$, the major axis is **horizontal**.
2. $c = \sqrt{a^2 - b^2} = \sqrt{16} = 4$.
3. Center: $(0, 0)$. Major-axis vertices: $(\pm 5, 0)$. Minor-axis vertices: $(0, \pm 3)$.
4. Foci: $(\pm 4, 0)$. Eccentricity: $e = 4/5 = 0{,}8$.

**Check:** $c^2 = 16 = 25 - 9 = a^2 - b^2$ ✓. $0 < e < 1$ confirms an ellipse ✓.

**Fonte.** [Stitz–Zeager Precalculus §7.4, Example 7.4.1](https://www.stitz-zeager.com/szprecalculus07042013.pdf) — CC-BY-NC-SA.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Write the ellipse equation given vertices and foci" numero="2">

**Problem:** Determine the equation of the ellipse with vertices at $(\pm 5, 0)$ and foci at $(\pm 3, 0)$.

**Strategy:** Extract $a$ from the vertices and $c$ from the foci, compute $b^2 = a^2 - c^2$, write the equation.

**Solution:**

1. Vertices at $(\pm 5, 0)$: horizontal major axis, $a = 5$.
2. Foci at $(\pm 3, 0)$: $c = 3$.
3. $b^2 = a^2 - c^2 = 25 - 9 = 16$.
4. Equation: $\dfrac{x^2}{25} + \dfrac{y^2}{16} = 1$.

**Check:** $(5, 0)$: $25/25 + 0 = 1$ ✓. $c = \sqrt{25-16} = 3$ ✓.

**Fonte.** [OpenStax Algebra and Trigonometry 2e §10.1, Example 10.4](https://openstax.org/books/algebra-and-trigonometry-2e/pages/10-1-the-ellipse) — CC-BY 4.0.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Parabola — focus, directrix, and latus rectum" numero="3">

**Problem:** For the parabola $x^2 = -12y$, determine the vertex, focus, directrix, and length of the latus rectum.

**Strategy:** Compare with the form $x^2 = 4py$ and extract $p$. The sign of $p$ indicates the opening direction.

**Solution:**

1. Comparing $x^2 = -12y$ with $x^2 = 4py$: $4p = -12 \Rightarrow p = -3$.
2. Since $p < 0$, the parabola **opens downward**.
3. Vertex: $(0, 0)$. Focus: $(0, p) = (0, -3)$. Directrix: $y = -p = 3$.
4. Latus rectum: $|4p| = 12$.

**Check:** On the latus rectum ($y = -3$): $x^2 = -12(-3) = 36 \Rightarrow x = \pm 6$. Distance from $(6, -3)$ to the focus $(0,-3)$: 6. Distance to the directrix $y = 3$: $|{-3-3}| = 6$. Equal ✓ — confirms $e = 1$.

**Fonte.** [Stitz–Zeager Precalculus §7.3, Example 7.3.3](https://www.stitz-zeager.com/szprecalculus07042013.pdf) — CC-BY-NC-SA.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Hyperbola — vertices, foci, and asymptotes" numero="4">

**Problem:** For the hyperbola $\dfrac{x^2}{4} - \dfrac{y^2}{9} = 1$, determine the vertices, foci, eccentricity, and asymptotes.

**Strategy:** Compare with $x^2/a^2 - y^2/b^2 = 1$ and apply $c^2 = a^2 + b^2$ (different sign from the ellipse).

**Solution:**

1. $a^2 = 4 \Rightarrow a = 2$; $b^2 = 9 \Rightarrow b = 3$.
2. $c = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2} = \sqrt{4 + 9} = \sqrt{13}$.
3. Vertices: $(\pm 2, 0)$. Foci: $(\pm\sqrt{13}, 0)$.
4. Eccentricity: $e = \sqrt{13}/2 \approx 1{,}80 > 1$ — confirmed hyperbola.
5. Asymptotes: $y = \pm\dfrac{b}{a}x = \pm\dfrac{3}{2}x$.

**Check:** The asymptotes are the diagonals of the fundamental rectangle with sides $2a = 4$ and $2b = 6$. Never confuse $c^2 = a^2 + b^2$ (hyperbola) with $c^2 = a^2 - b^2$ (ellipse).

**Fonte.** [OpenStax Algebra and Trigonometry 2e §10.2, Example 10.4](https://openstax.org/books/algebra-and-trigonometry-2e/pages/10-2-the-hyperbola) — CC-BY 4.0.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Off-origin conic — elliptical whispering gallery" numero="5">

**Problem:** Identify the conic $9x^2 + 4y^2 - 36x + 8y + 4 = 0$, determine its center, foci, and distance between foci. An architect wants to use this ellipse as a whispering gallery — where should the loudspeakers go?

**Strategy:** Complete the squares in $x$ and in $y$ separately to reduce to canonical form.

**Solution:**

1. Group: $9(x^2 - 4x) + 4(y^2 + 2y) = -4$.
2. Complete in $x$: $9[(x-2)^2 - 4] + 4(y^2 + 2y) = -4 \Rightarrow 9(x-2)^2 - 36 + 4(y^2 + 2y) = -4$.
3. Complete in $y$: $+4[(y+1)^2 - 1] \Rightarrow 9(x-2)^2 + 4(y+1)^2 = 36$.
4. Divide by 36: $\dfrac{(x-2)^2}{4} + \dfrac{(y+1)^2}{9} = 1$.
5. $a^2 = 9$ (in $y$ — **vertical** axis), $b^2 = 4$. $a = 3$, $b = 2$, $c = \sqrt{5}$.
6. Center: $(2, -1)$. Foci: $(2,\ -1 \pm \sqrt{5})$, that is, $(2, -1+\sqrt{5}) \approx (2, 1{,}24)$ and $(2, -1-\sqrt{5}) \approx (2, -3{,}24)$.
7. Distance between foci: $2c = 2\sqrt{5} \approx 4{,}47$ m.

**Check:** $c^2 = 9 - 4 = 5$ ✓. The architect installs a loudspeaker at each focus — all sound emitted at one focus converges to the other.

**Fonte.** [Stitz–Zeager Precalculus §7.4, Example 7.4.3](https://www.stitz-zeager.com/szprecalculus07042013.pdf) — CC-BY-NC-SA.
</Exemplo>
