<Definicao titulo="Cartesian plane">

$\mathbb{R}^2 = \{(x, y) : x, y \in \mathbb{R}\}$. Each point is an ordered pair. The axes $x$ (horizontal) and $y$ (vertical) intersect at $O = (0, 0)$, dividing the plane into four **quadrants**:

- **I**: $x > 0, y > 0$
- **II**: $x < 0, y > 0$
- **III**: $x < 0, y < 0$
- **IV**: $x > 0, y < 0$

</Definicao>

> "The Cartesian plane consists of two perpendicular number axes, called the $x$-axis and $y$-axis. The point where they intersect is the origin. Each ordered pair $(x, y)$ corresponds to exactly one point in the plane and vice versa." — [OpenStax College Algebra 2e, §2.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/2-1-the-rectangular-coordinate-systems-and-graphs)

### Midpoint

<Definicao titulo="Midpoint of a segment">

<Equation explicacao="Each coordinate of the midpoint is the arithmetic mean of the corresponding coordinates of the endpoints." latex="M = \left(\frac{x_1 + x_2}{2},\ \frac{y_1 + y_2}{2}\right)" />

</Definicao>

> "The midpoint of a line segment joining two points is the point whose coordinates are the arithmetic means of the coordinates of the endpoints." — [OpenStax College Algebra 2e, §2.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/2-1-the-rectangular-coordinate-systems-and-graphs)

### Dividing a segment in ratio $k$

To divide $\overline{PQ}$ in ratio $\overline{PR}/\overline{RQ} = k$ (internal):

<Equation explicacao="Generalization of midpoint (case k = 1). For arbitrary ratio k." latex="R = \left(\frac{x_1 + k\,x_2}{1 + k},\ \frac{y_1 + k\,y_2}{1 + k}\right)" />

### Triangle area

<Teorema titulo="Area by coordinates">

For vertices $A = (x_1, y_1)$, $B = (x_2, y_2)$, $C = (x_3, y_3)$:

<Equation explicacao="Equivalent to (1/2) |det M| where M is the matrix of vectors AB and AC." latex="\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2}\,|x_1(y_2 - y_3) + x_2(y_3 - y_1) + x_3(y_1 - y_2)|" />

Without the absolute value, the sign indicates orientation: positive for counterclockwise vertices, negative for clockwise. Collinear points $\Rightarrow$ zero area.

</Teorema>

**The distance formula is the root of all analytic geometry.** Circle is the set of points at distance $r$ from a center. Ellipse is the set of points whose sum of distances to two foci is constant. Parabola is the set of points equidistant from a focus and a directrix. Without the distance formula, none of these definitions work.

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

Imagine a **treasure hunt** on the floor of a room. You say: "walk 3 steps to the right and 4 steps forward". Done, you found the treasure! Those two numbers are the **coordinates** of the treasure.

To know how far the chicken needs to **walk in a straight line** from the corner of the room to the treasure, just think of a **triangle**: 3 to the right, 4 forward, and in diagonal are... 5 steps! That's the **distance**.

And if you want to mark the **halfway point** between the corner and the treasure? Just take the average: 1.5 steps to the right and 2 forward. Done — that's the midpoint.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

The **Cartesian plane** is like infinite graph paper. Each dot has two numbers — how far right ($x$) and how far up ($y$).

**Distance between two points**: uses the **Pythagorean theorem**. Imagine you draw a right triangle with the two points as opposite corners. The legs are the difference in $x$ and the difference in $y$. The hypotenuse is the distance!

**Example**: from point $(1, 1)$ to point $(4, 5)$. Difference in $x$ is 3. Difference in $y$ is 4. Distance $= \sqrt{9 + 16} = \sqrt{25} = 5$.

**Midpoint**: the dot halfway between two points. Just **take the average** of the coordinates. Midway between $(2, 4)$ and $(6, 10)$ is $(4, 7)$.

**Where we use it**: GPS, video game maps, robots walking on the floor, Wi-Fi coverage.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old">

Cartesian plane: each point is described by 2 numbers — a horizontal coordinate $x$ and a vertical coordinate $y$. **Descartes** unified algebra and geometry in 1637 — hence the adjective "Cartesian".

**Distance between 2 points**: apply Pythagoras. If you have $P = (1, 2)$ and $Q = (4, 6)$:

$$d = \sqrt{(4-1)^2 + (6-2)^2} = \sqrt{9 + 16} = \sqrt{25} = 5$$

**Midpoint**: take the average of the coordinates. Midway between $(1, 2)$ and $(4, 6)$ is $(2.5,\ 4)$.

**Centroid of a triangle**: average of the 3 vertices. For $A, B, C$: $G = \dfrac{A + B + C}{3}$ — coordinate by coordinate.

**Why is it important?** Because the Cartesian plane is the language that **connects algebra and geometry**. Line becomes equation. Circle becomes equation. Parabola becomes equation. Everything that was "geometric" becomes "algebraic" — and the computer can calculate it.

**Physical connection**: in 2D kinematics, the position of a body is a pair $(x(t), y(t))$. The trajectory is the set of those points.

<Insight>

**Why memorize the distance formula?** It shows up **literally in every next lesson** — circle (locus of points at distance $r$ from a center), ellipse (constant sum of distances), parabola (distance to a point = distance to a line), vectors, dot product, norms. Without fluency here, everything else stalls.

</Insight>

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

Analytic geometry is the foundation of virtually all modern applied mathematics.

### Generalization to $\mathbb{R}^n$

Distance in $\mathbb{R}^n$:

$$d(P, Q) = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - y_i)^2} = \|P - Q\|_2$$

This is the **Euclidean norm** ($\ell_2$). In ML, it is the "standard distance" used in k-NN, k-means, t-SNE, neural networks (MSE loss).

### Other useful metrics

| Metric | Formula | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Manhattan ($\ell_1$) | $\sum \lvert x_i - y_i \rvert$ | city blocks, robustness to outliers |
| Chebyshev ($\ell_\infty$) | $\max \lvert x_i - y_i \rvert$ | chess, planning |
| Mahalanobis | $\sqrt{(x-\mu)^T \Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu)}$ | multivariate correlation |
| Geodesic | great circles | distance on a sphere |
| Cosine | $1 - \frac{\vec u \cdot \vec v}{\|u\|\|v\|}$ | NLP, recommendation |

### Signed (oriented) areas

The formula $\frac{1}{2}(x_1(y_2 - y_3) + \ldots)$ **without the absolute value** gives **signed area** — positive if $A, B, C$ counterclockwise, negative if clockwise. Critical in CG (back-face culling) and algorithms like ConvexHull (Graham scan).

### Cross-domain

- **Computer Graphics**: each pixel is a point, each 2D/3D object is a set of coordinates. Ray tracing works in $\mathbb{R}^3$.
- **GPS**: positioning via coordinates + trilateration with 4 satellites.
- **Robotics**: forward/inverse kinematics of robot arms via composition of transformations.
- **Civil Engineering**: surveying produces UTM coordinates (variant of Cartesian).
- **Statistics**: scatter plot is literally the Cartesian plane, and linear regression seeks the best-fit line.

### Proof of Pythagoras → distance

Construct a right triangle with horizontal leg $|x_2 - x_1|$ and vertical leg $|y_2 - y_1|$. The hypotenuse is segment $\overline{PQ}$. By Pythagoras: $d^2 = (x_2-x_1)^2 + (y_2-y_1)^2$. QED.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

The "distance formula" is merely the **norm induced by the canonical inner product** in $\mathbb{R}^n$.

### Functional-analytic view

We define $\langle u, v \rangle = \sum u_i v_i$. The norm $\|x\| = \sqrt{\langle x, x \rangle}$ satisfies:

1. **Positivity**: $\|x\| \geq 0$, $= 0 \iff x = 0$.
2. **Homogeneity**: $\|\alpha x\| = |\alpha| \|x\|$.
3. **Triangle inequality**: $\|x + y\| \leq \|x\| + \|y\|$ (Cauchy-Schwarz).

The **metric** $d(x, y) = \|x - y\|$ inherits these properties. $(\mathbb{R}^n, d)$ is a **complete metric space** — every Cauchy sequence converges.

### Generalization: spaces with inner product

In any Hilbert space $H$ (e.g., $L^2(\Omega)$ — square-integrable functions), the "distance" between $f$ and $g$ is $\|f - g\|_2 = \sqrt{\int |f - g|^2}$. **Same formalism** as $\mathbb{R}^n$ — signal processing, quantum mechanics, etc.

### Applications in ML / quant finance

- **Embeddings**: GPT/Claude represent tokens as vectors in $\mathbb{R}^{4096}$ or $\mathbb{R}^{12288}$. "Semantic similarity" $=$ Euclidean or cosine distance.
- **Clustering**: k-means converges to local minima of sum of squared distances. Still the workhorse algorithm.
- **Quant finance**: distance between séries of retorna defines correlation; hierarchical clustering (Mantegna 1999) uses $\sqrt{2(1-\rho)}$ as metric between assets.
- **Gauss-Bonnet**: on manifolds, the metric is local tensor; geodesics generalize "straight line".

### Historical curiosity

Descartes published *La Géométrie* as an appendix to *Discours de la méthode* in 1637. Fermat developed parallel ideas independently. Modern notation $(x, y)$ came later — Descartes used letters at the end of the alphabet for variables (hence $x, y, z$).

### Numerical

In IEEE 754, $\sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2 + (y_2-y_1)^2}$ can cause **overflow** when components are large (e.g., $10^{200}$) and **underflow** when small. The `hypot(a, b)` function from `<math.h>` computes the hypotenuse numerically stably (scales before squaring). Always use `hypot` in production code.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practical application">

**Recipe: how to solve ANY basic analytic geometry problem.**

1. **Draw the axes** (even approximately). Plot the points.
2. **Identify what is asked**: distance? midpoint? area? locus?
3. **Apply the corresponding formula** without skipping steps.
4. **Check against geometry** (does the order of magnitude make sense?).

### Example 1: Delivery app GPS

The delivery rider is at $(2, 3)$ km from headquarters, and the restaurant is at $(8, 11)$ km. **Straight-line distance** $= \sqrt{6^2 + 8^2} = \sqrt{100} = 10$ km. Actual distance (by streets) will be larger — use a "tortuosity factor" ($\approx 1.3$ in mid-sized cities) to estimate.

### Example 2: Irregular land area

In surveying, given vertices in UTM coordinates, the area of the polygon is calculated by the **shoelace formula** (generalization of triangle area). Every rural-property spreadsheet uses this formula.

### Example 3: Centroid in structural engineering

The **center of mass** of a homogeneous triangular plate is the centroid $G = (A + B + C)/3$. Useful in statics (equilibrium calculation) and solid mechanics.

### Example 4: Distance in ML

In **k-NN** (k-nearest neighbors), a new point is classified by the majority class of the $k$ nearest points in feature space. "Nearest" = smallest Euclidean distance — exactly the formula in this lesson, generalized to $\mathbb{R}^n$.

## Worked examples

> Five examples with increasing difficulty — from direct distance calculation to triangle classification by coordinates. Each example cites its source: the problem always comes from an open book.

<Exemplo titulo="Distance between two points (application)" numero="1">

**Problem:** Compute the distance between $P = (-2, 3)$ and $Q = (4, -5)$.

**Strategy:** Apply directly the formula $d(P, Q) = \sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2}$. It doesn't matter which point is $P$ and which is $Q$ — the terms are squared.

**Solution:**

1. Identify the coordinates: $x_1 = -2$, $y_1 = 3$, $x_2 = 4$, $y_2 = -5$.
2. Compute the differences: $x_2 - x_1 = 4 - (-2) = 6$ and $y_2 - y_1 = -5 - 3 = -8$.
3. Square: $6^2 = 36$ and $(-8)^2 = 64$.
4. Add and take the square root: $\sqrt{36 + 64} = \sqrt{100} = 10$.

**Check:** The legs of the right triangle are 6 and 8, hypotenuse 10 — a classic **Pythagorean triple** $(6, 8, 10) = 2 \cdot (3, 4, 5)$. Checks out.

**Source.** [OpenStax — College Algebra 2e §2.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/2-1-the-rectangular-coordinate-systems-and-graphs) — CC-BY 4.0 license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Midpoint of a segment (application)" numero="2">

**Problem:** Find the midpoint of the segment with endpoints $A = (-3, 7)$ and $B = (5, -1)$.

**Strategy:** The midpoint formula is the arithmetic mean coordinate by coordinate.

**Solution:**

1. Coordinate $x$ of midpoint: $\dfrac{-3 + 5}{2} = \dfrac{2}{2} = 1$.
2. Coordinate $y$ of midpoint: $\dfrac{7 + (-1)}{2} = \dfrac{6}{2} = 3$.
3. Thus, $M = (1, 3)$.

**Check:** $M$ should be equidistant from $A$ and $B$. $d(A, M) = \sqrt{(1-(-3))^2 + (3-7)^2} = \sqrt{16+16} = \sqrt{32}$. $d(B, M) = \sqrt{(1-5)^2 + (3-(-1))^2} = \sqrt{16+16} = \sqrt{32}$. Equal — checks out.

**Source.** [OpenStax — College Algebra 2e §2.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/2-1-the-rectangular-coordinate-systems-and-graphs) — CC-BY 4.0 license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Determine missing coordinate (intermediate)" numero="3">

**Problem:** Find $y$ such that point $P = (3, y)$ is at distance $5$ units from $Q = (-1, 2)$.

**Strategy:** Apply the distance formula and solve the resulting equation for $y$. A quadratic equation in $y$ will appear — expect two solutions (above and below the horizontal alignment of $Q$).

**Solution:**

1. By distance formula: $\sqrt{(3 - (-1))^2 + (y - 2)^2} = 5$.
2. Simplify: $\sqrt{16 + (y-2)^2} = 5$.
3. Square: $16 + (y - 2)^2 = 25$.
4. Isolate the square: $(y - 2)^2 = 9$.
5. Extract square root (with $\pm$): $y - 2 = \pm 3$.
6. Solve: $y = 5$ or $y = -1$.

**Check:** For $y = 5$: $d = \sqrt{16 + 9} = 5$. OK. For $y = -1$: $d = \sqrt{16 + 9} = 5$. OK. Geometrically, the set of points at distance 5 from $Q$ is a circle of radius 5; the vertical line $x = 3$ intersects this circle at exactly two points — $(3, 5)$ and $(3, -1)$.

**Source.** [Stitz–Zeager — Precalculus §1.1](https://www.stitz-zeager.com/szprecalculus07042013.pdf) — CC-BY-NC-SA license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Identify triangle type (advanced)" numero="4">

**Problem:** Show that the triangle with vertices $A = (1, 1)$, $B = (4, 5)$, $C = (8, 2)$ is isosceles and not equilateral.

**Strategy:** Calculate the lengths of all three sides via distance formula and compare. Isosceles $\Leftrightarrow$ at least two sides equal; equilateral $\Leftrightarrow$ all three equal.

**Solution:**

1. Side $AB$: $\sqrt{(4-1)^2 + (5-1)^2} = \sqrt{9 + 16} = \sqrt{25} = 5$.
2. Side $BC$: $\sqrt{(8-4)^2 + (2-5)^2} = \sqrt{16 + 9} = \sqrt{25} = 5$.
3. Side $CA$: $\sqrt{(1-8)^2 + (1-2)^2} = \sqrt{49 + 1} = \sqrt{50} \approx 7.07$.
4. Since $AB = BC = 5$ and $CA = \sqrt{50} \neq 5$, the triangle is **isosceles** with base $\overline{CA}$ and equal sides $\overline{AB} = \overline{BC}$. Not equilateral because one side is distinct.

**Check:** By triangle inequality: $AB + BC = 10 > 7.07 = CA$. OK. Vertex $B$ is equidistant from the other two — consistent with "isosceles apex".

**Source.** [OpenStax — College Algebra 2e §2.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/2-1-the-rectangular-coordinate-systems-and-graphs) — CC-BY 4.0 license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Delivery logistics (real modeling)" numero="5">

**Problem:** A logistics company has a distribution center at $C = (0, 0)$. There are three customers at points $A = (3, 4)$, $B = (-6, 8)$ and $D = (5, -12)$, with coordinates in kilometers. (a) Determine which customer is nearest the center. (b) Compute the average distance traveled from center to each customer.

**Strategy:** Compute the three distances, identify the smallest (item a) and take the arithmetic mean (item b).

**Solution:**

1. $d(C, A) = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = \sqrt{25} = 5$ km. (Classic Pythagorean triple.)
2. $d(C, B) = \sqrt{(-6)^2 + 8^2} = \sqrt{36 + 64} = \sqrt{100} = 10$ km.
3. $d(C, D) = \sqrt{5^2 + (-12)^2} = \sqrt{25 + 144} = \sqrt{169} = 13$ km. (Another triple: $5$-$12$-$13$.)
4. **(a)** Customer $A$ is nearest, at $5$ km.
5. **(b)** Average distance: $\dfrac{5 + 10 + 13}{3} = \dfrac{28}{3} \approx 9.33$ km.

**Check:** All distances positive (makes sense). Order $5 < 10 < 13$ is consistent with visual position on plane: $A$ is closer to origin than $B$ and $D$. Average 9.33 km is between minimum 5 and maximum 13 — sanity check.

**Practical note.** In real logistics (traveling salesman problem), the relevant metric is often not Euclidean but **distance along streets** — which can be estimated as Euclidean multiplied by a tortuosity factor ($\approx 1.3$ to $1.5$ in mid-sized cities).
