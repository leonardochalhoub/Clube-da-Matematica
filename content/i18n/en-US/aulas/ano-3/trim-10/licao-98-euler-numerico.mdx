<Porta nivel="formal" titulo="Derivation and error analysis">

### Initial value problem

Given the IVP:

$$y' = f(x, y), \quad y(x_0) = y_0$$

We wish to approximate $y(x)$ on $x \in [x_0, X]$ without a closed-form expression.

### Discretization

Divide the interval into $N$ equal subintervals:

$$h = \frac{X - x_0}{N}, \qquad x_n = x_0 + n\,h, \quad n = 0, 1, \ldots, N$$

<Definicao titulo="Explicit Euler method (forward Euler)">

Approximation $y_n \approx y(x_n)$ given by the recurrence:

<Equation explicacao="Each y_n+1 is estimated by the tangent line to the graph of y at (x_n, y_n), with slope f(x_n, y_n)." latex="y_{n+1} = y_n + h\,f(x_n,\, y_n)" />

Geometrically: we follow the tangent to the solution curve for one step of length $h$.

</Definicao>

> "The simplest numerical method for solving $y' = f(x,y)$, $y(x_0) = y_0$, is *Euler's method*. We replace $y'$ with the difference quotient $(y_{n+1} - y_n)/h$ and evaluate $f$ at $x_n$: this gives $y_{n+1} = y_n + hf(x_n, y_n)$." — [Lebl, Notes on Diffy Qs §1.7](https://www.jirka.org/diffyqs/html/numer_section.html)

### Error analysis via Taylor series

<Definicao titulo="Local truncation error">

By Taylor expansion around $x_n$:

$$y(x_n + h) = y(x_n) + h\,y'(x_n) + \frac{h^2}{2}\,y''(\xi_n), \quad \xi_n \in (x_n, x_{n+1})$$

Since $y' = f(x,y)$, the Euler method uses only the first two terms. The **local error** (per step) is:

$$e_n = y(x_{n+1}) - y_{n+1} = \frac{h^2}{2}\,y''(\xi_n) = O(h^2)$$

</Definicao>

<Definicao titulo="Global truncation error">

In $N = (X-x_0)/h$ steps, local errors accumulate:

$$E = |y(X) - y_N| \leq C\,h \cdot N \cdot e_{\text{local}} = O(h)$$

The **Euler method is first-order**: halving $h$ reduces the global error by 2 (not by 4).

</Definicao>

<Teorema titulo="Convergence of the Euler method">

If $f$ is Lipschitz in $y$ (constant $L$) and $y''$ is bounded by $M$ on $[x_0, X]$, then:

$$|y(x_n) - y_n| \leq \frac{Mh}{2L}\bigl(e^{L(x_n-x_0)} - 1\bigr)$$

In particular, $|y(X) - y_N| \to 0$ as $h \to 0$: the method **converges**.

</Teorema>

<Insight>
The Euler method is at once the simplest and the least precise. Its pedagogical value is immense: the entire theory of single-step methods (Runge-Kutta) is a generalization of the basic idea of "following the tangent." RK4 is not four times more complicated — it is the same idea applied four times with intelligent weights.
</Insight>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

Imagine you are on a map and want to reach a treasure. You don't know the full path, but you know **which direction to point right now**.

You take one small step in that direction. Stop. Look again at where you are pointing. Take another step. Repeat.

If the steps are too big, you will miss the path. If they are too small, you arrive almost correctly but it takes forever.

The **Euler method** does exactly this with equations — it takes steps following the direction the equation indicates.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

Imagine you want to know the position of a falling ball, but you don't know the exact formula. You know the velocity at each instant: $v(t) = f(t, x)$.

The Euler method says:
1. Start at $x_0$, $t_0$.
2. The velocity now is $v_0 = f(t_0, x_0)$.
3. Take a step: $x_1 = x_0 + h \times v_0$, $t_1 = t_0 + h$.
4. Repeat: the velocity at $t_1$ is $v_1 = f(t_1, x_1)$. Take another step: $x_2 = x_1 + h \times v_1$.

The smaller the step $h$, the more accurate. The total error is proportional to $h$ — halving the step halves the error.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For the high school student">

The **Euler method** numerically solves any first-order ODE:

$$y' = f(x, y), \quad y(x_0) = y_0$$

**Algorithm:**

1. Choose a step $h$ (smaller is more accurate but slower).
2. For $n = 0, 1, 2, \ldots, N-1$:
   - Compute $f(x_n, y_n)$ — the slope at the current point.
   - $y_{n+1} = y_n + h \cdot f(x_n, y_n)$
   - $x_{n+1} = x_n + h$

**Why does it work?** The differential equation says the slope of the curve at $(x_n, y_n)$ is $f(x_n, y_n)$. The tangent line with that slope takes us to an estimate of the next point.

**Quick example:** $y' = y$, $y(0) = 1$, $h = 0.1$, want $y(0.3)$.

- $y_1 = 1 + 0.1 \times 1 = 1.1$
- $y_2 = 1.1 + 0.1 \times 1.1 = 1.21$
- $y_3 = 1.21 + 0.1 \times 1.21 = 1.331$

Exact: $e^{0.3} \approx 1.3499$. Error $\approx 0.019$ (1.4%).

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For the engineering student">

Given the IVP $y' = f(x,y)$, $y(x_0) = y_0$, the **explicit Euler** is:

$$y_{n+1} = y_n + h\,f(x_n, y_n)$$

**Stability analysis.** For the test equation $y' = \lambda y$ ($\lambda \in \mathbb{C}$):

$$y_{n+1} = (1 + h\lambda)\,y_n$$

The numerical solution decays if $|1 + h\lambda| < 1$. For $\lambda = -a + i b$ ($a > 0$): the stability region is the disk $|1 + h\lambda| < 1$ in the $h\lambda$ plane. Explicit Euler has **finite stability region** — unstable for large $h$ even if the ODE is stable. This is the fundamental problem with the method for stiff equations.

**Implicit Euler:** $y_{n+1} = y_n + h\,f(x_{n+1}, y_{n+1})$ — solve the implicit equation for $y_{n+1}$. For the test equation: $|1/(1-h\lambda)| < 1$ whenever $\Re(\lambda) < 0$ — **A-stable**.

**Heun's method (RK2):**

$$k_1 = f(x_n, y_n), \quad k_2 = f(x_n + h, y_n + h k_1), \quad y_{n+1} = y_n + \frac{h}{2}(k_1 + k_2)$$

Global error $O(h^2)$. Interpret $k_2$ as the slope at the Euler point and average the two slopes.

**Classical RK4 (4th-order Runge-Kutta):**

$$k_1 = f(x_n, y_n), \quad k_2 = f\!\left(x_n + \tfrac{h}{2}, y_n + \tfrac{h}{2}k_1\right)$$
$$k_3 = f\!\left(x_n + \tfrac{h}{2}, y_n + \tfrac{h}{2}k_2\right), \quad k_4 = f(x_n + h, y_n + h k_3)$$
$$y_{n+1} = y_n + \frac{h}{6}(k_1 + 2k_2 + 2k_3 + k_4)$$

Global error $O(h^4)$. Industrial standard for non-stiff ODEs.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For the senior professional">

The Euler method dates to 1768 (published in *Institutionum calculi integralis*, Vol. I). Error analysis via Taylor series and formal convergence are due to Cauchy (1824). The modern theory of Runge-Kutta methods was developed by Carl Runge (1895) and Wilhelm Kutta (1901), who derived the RK4 coefficients by comparison with the truncated Taylor series of $y(x+h)$.

**Modern perspective.** The Euler method is the 1-stage, 1st-order Runge-Kutta method. The general RK theory is encoded in **Butcher tableaus** — matrices $(A, b, c)$ that describe the weights and nodes of each method. Euler: $A = [0]$, $b = [1]$, $c = [0]$. RK4: a $4 \times 4$ tableau with weights $[1/6, 1/3, 1/3, 1/6]$.

**Stiffness and implicit methods.** Stiff equations arise when the Jacobian $\partial f/\partial y$ has eigenvalues of very different magnitudes. Explicit methods like Euler require $h$ proportional to the smallest eigenvalue (the fastest) — very expensive. A-stable methods (implicit Euler, Crank-Nicolson, BDF methods) ignore the stability issue and use $h$ proportional to the time scale of interest.

**Engineering applications:** simulation of electrical power distribution networks (ANEEL uses PSCAD/EMTP — implicit Euler with step 10–50 μs), molecular chain dynamics in biochemistry (symplectic Euler conserves phase-space area), orbital dynamics (Yoshida symplectic integrators).

**In Brazil:** REAMAT (UFRGS) maintains the most-used Numerical Calculus textbook in PT-BR for engineering, with Python and Scilab implementations of all methods in this lesson.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Applications with Brazilian data">

**Growth of a Brazilian startup.** A fintech in Curitiba grows at rate $P'(t) = 0.15\,P(1 - P/10000)$ clients per month (logistic, capacity 10,000). With $P(0) = 500$, simulate via Euler with $h = 1$ month for 12 months.

Step 0: $f(0, 500) = 0.15 \times 500 \times (1 - 500/10000) = 71.25$. $P_1 = 500 + 71.25 \approx 571$.

Step 1: $f(1, 571) = 0.15 \times 571 \times (1 - 571/10000) \approx 80.7$. $P_2 \approx 652$.

After 12 iterations: $P(12) \approx 1,750$ clients. Exact logistic solution: $\approx 1,780$. Error: 1.7%.

**Sanitation — reservoir flow ODE.** The flow $Q(t)$ [m³/h] in a SABESP reservoir obeys $Q' = -kQ^{0.5}$, $Q(0) = 10,000$ m³. With $k = 0.02$, Euler with $h = 10$ h: $Q_1 = 10,000 - 0.02 \times 100 \times 10 = 9,980$ m³. Table of 24 steps gives $Q(240) \approx 7,100$ m³.

</Porta>

## Worked examples

<Exemplo numero="98.1" titulo="Euler in 4 steps — exponential ODE">

**Problem.** Use the Euler method with $h = 0.1$ to approximate $y(0.4)$ given $y' = -y$, $y(0) = 1$. Compare with the exact value.

**Strategy.** Apply the recurrence $y_{n+1} = y_n + h(-y_n) = y_n(1-h)$ four times.

**Solution.**

| $n$ | $x_n$ | $y_n$ | $f = -y_n$ | $y_{n+1}$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0 | 1.0000 | -1.0000 | 0.9000 |
| 1 | 0.1 | 0.9000 | -0.9000 | 0.8100 |
| 2 | 0.2 | 0.8100 | -0.8100 | 0.7290 |
| 3 | 0.3 | 0.7290 | -0.7290 | 0.6561 |

$y_4 = 0.6561$. Exact: $e^{-0.4} \approx 0.6703$. Relative error: $\approx 2.12\%$.

**Verification.** At each step, we multiply by $(1-h) = 0.9$, so $y_4 = (0.9)^4 = 0.6561$ ✓. Error grows with $h$ — for $h = 0.05$, error would drop to $\approx 1.06\%$.

**Source.** [Lebl, Notes on Diffy Qs §1.7, Example 1.7.1](https://www.jirka.org/diffyqs/html/numer_section.html) — CC-BY-SA

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="98.2" titulo="Euler on a nonlinear equation">

**Problem.** Estimate $y(1)$ for $y' = y^2$, $y(0) = 0.5$, using Euler with $h = 0.25$.

**Strategy.** The exact solution is $y = 1/(2-x)$ (blow-up at $x = 2$). With 4 steps up to $x = 1$.

**Solution.**

$n=0$: $f(0, 0.5) = 0.25$. $y_1 = 0.5 + 0.25 \times 0.25 = 0.5625$.

$n=1$: $f(0.25, 0.5625) = 0.31641$. $y_2 = 0.5625 + 0.25 \times 0.31641 = 0.6416$.

$n=2$: $f(0.5, 0.6416) = 0.4117$. $y_3 = 0.6416 + 0.25 \times 0.4117 = 0.7445$.

$n=3$: $f(0.75, 0.7445) = 0.5543$. $y_4 = 0.7445 + 0.25 \times 0.5543 = 0.8831$.

Exact: $y(1) = 1/(2-1) = 1$. Error: $11.7\%$.

**Verification.** The error is large because $f = y^2$ grows and tangent lines underestimate the curve. Reducing to $h = 0.1$ (10 steps) would reduce to $\approx 5.8\%$, confirming first order.

**Source.** [OpenStax Calculus Volume 2, §4.2, Example 4.2](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-2/pages/4-2-direction-fields-and-eulers-method) — CC-BY-NC-SA

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="98.3" titulo="Comparison Euler versus RK4">

**Problem.** For $y' = x + y$, $y(0) = 1$, approximate $y(0.2)$ with $h = 0.1$ using (a) Euler and (b) RK4. Exact solution: $y = 2e^x - x - 1$.

**Strategy.** Apply the formulas for each method and compare errors.

**Solution.**

Exact: $y(0.2) = 2e^{0.2} - 0.2 - 1 \approx 2 \times 1.2214 - 1.2 = 1.2428$.

**(a) Euler:**

$n=0$: $f(0,1) = 1$. $y_1 = 1 + 0.1 \times 1 = 1.1$.

$n=1$: $f(0.1, 1.1) = 0.1 + 1.1 = 1.2$. $y_2 = 1.1 + 0.1 \times 1.2 = 1.22$.

Euler error: $|1.2428 - 1.22| = 0.0228$.

**(b) RK4 (step $n=0 \to n=1$, $h=0.1$):**

$k_1 = 0 + 1 = 1$; $k_2 = (0.05) + (1 + 0.05) = 1.1$; $k_3 = 0.05 + (1 + 0.05) = 1.1$; $k_4 = 0.1 + (1 + 0.1) = 1.2$.

$y_1 = 1 + (0.1/6)(1 + 2.2 + 2.2 + 1.2) = 1 + 0.1 \times 1.1\overline{0}\approx 1.1103$.

RK4 error (two steps): $< 10^{-6}$ — orders of magnitude better than Euler.

**Verification.** Ratio of errors when $h$ is halved: Euler 2:1 (1st order); RK4 16:1 (4th order) ✓.

**Source.** [REAMAT UFRGS, Numerical Calculus §8.3](https://www.ufrgs.br/reamat/CalculoNumerico/livro-py/s_metodos_de_runge-kutta.html) — CC-BY-SA

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="98.4" titulo="Implicit Euler — stability in a stiff equation">

**Problem.** $y' = -100y$, $y(0) = 1$. Compare explicit Euler with $h = 0.03$ (unstable) and $h = 0.01$ (stable).

**Strategy.** Analyze $|1 + h\lambda|$ with $\lambda = -100$.

**Solution.**

Amplification factor for explicit Euler: $r = 1 + h\lambda = 1 - 100h$.

- $h = 0.03$: $r = 1 - 3 = -2$. $|r| = 2 > 1$ — **unstable**: $y_n = (-2)^n \to \pm\infty$.
- $h = 0.01$: $r = 1 - 1 = 0$. $|r| = 0 < 1$ — stable (on the boundary), $y_n = 0$ in 1 step (overdamped).
- $h = 0.02$: $r = 1 - 2 = -1$. $|r| = 1$ — oscillates without damping.

Stability region for Euler: $h < 2/|\lambda| = 0.02$ for this equation. The exact solution $e^{-100x}$ decays on the scale $x \sim 0.01$ — steps much smaller than the time scale of interest.

**Verification.** Implicit Euler: $r_{imp} = 1/(1-h\lambda) = 1/(1+100h)$. For any $h > 0$: $|r_{imp}| < 1$ ✓ — always stable.

**Source.** [REAMAT UFRGS, Numerical Calculus §8.4](https://www.ufrgs.br/reamat/CalculoNumerico/livro-py/s_estabilidade_de_metodos_numericos.html) — CC-BY-SA

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="98.5" titulo="Euler for system of ODEs — oscillator">

**Problem.** System $x' = v$, $v' = -x$ (harmonic oscillator), IC: $x(0) = 1$, $v(0) = 0$, $h = 0.1$, simulate 3 steps.

**Strategy.** Apply Euler to each component simultaneously.

**Solution.**

State: $x_n$, $v_n$. Functions $f_1 = v$ and $f_2 = -x$.

Step 0: $x_0 = 1$, $v_0 = 0$. Slopes: $\dot x = 0$, $\dot v = -1$.

$x_1 = 1 + 0.1 \times 0 = 1$; $v_1 = 0 + 0.1 \times (-1) = -0.1$.

Step 1: $x_1 = 1$, $v_1 = -0.1$. Slopes: $\dot x = -0.1$, $\dot v = -1$.

$x_2 = 1 + 0.1 \times (-0.1) = 0.99$; $v_2 = -0.1 + 0.1 \times (-1) = -0.2$.

Step 2: $x_2 = 0.99$, $v_2 = -0.2$. Slopes: $\dot x = -0.2$, $\dot v = -0.99$.

$x_3 = 0.99 + 0.1 \times (-0.2) = 0.97$; $v_3 = -0.2 + 0.1 \times (-0.99) = -0.299$.

Exact at $t = 0.3$: $x = \cos(0.3) \approx 0.9553$, $v = -\sin(0.3) \approx -0.2955$. Euler overestimates $x$ — does not conserve energy. Symplectic Euler solves this: compute $v_{n+1}$ first, then use it for $x_{n+1}$.

**Source.** [Lebl, Notes on Diffy Qs §1.7, Exercise 1.7.4](https://www.jirka.org/diffyqs/html/numer_section.html) — CC-BY-SA

</Exemplo>
