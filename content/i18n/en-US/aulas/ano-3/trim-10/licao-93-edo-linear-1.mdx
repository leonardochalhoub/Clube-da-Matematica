### Canonical form

<Definicao titulo="1st-order linear ODE">

An ODE is called <strong>1st-order linear</strong> if it can be written in the form

$$y' + p(x)\,y = q(x)$$

where $p$ and $q$ are continuous functions on an interval $I$. <strong>Linear</strong> means that $y$ and $y'$ appear raised to the power of 1, without products like $y \cdot y'$ or $y^2$.

</Definicao>

> "An equation of the form $y' + p(x)y = f(x)$ is called a **first order linear differential equation**." — [Lebl, Notes on Diffy Qs, §1.4](https://www.jirka.org/diffyqs/html/firstode_section.html)

### Integrating factor method

**Why it works.** We want $\mu y' + \mu p y$ to be the derivative of a product. Since $(\mu y)' = \mu' y + \mu y'$, we need $\mu' = \mu p$, or $\mu'/\mu = p$. Integrating: $\mu(x) = e^{\int p(x)\,dx}$.

<Teorema titulo="General solution via integrating factor">

Let $p, q$ be continuous on $I$. The general solution of $y' + p(x)y = q(x)$ is

$$y(x) = \frac{1}{\mu(x)}\left[\int \mu(x)\,q(x)\,dx + C\right], \quad \mu(x) = e^{\int p(x)\,dx}$$

For each initial condition $y(x_0) = y_0$ there exists exactly one solution defined on the entire interval $I$.

</Teorema>

### Homogeneous + particular structure

<Definicao titulo="Solution decomposition">

- $y_h = C\,e^{-\int p\,dx}$ — <strong>homogeneous solution</strong> (free regime, no forcing).
- $y_p$ — <strong>particular solution</strong> (response to forcing $q$; any $C$ in $\int \mu q\,dx$).
- $y = y_h + y_p$ — general solution.

</Definicao>

<Insight>

$y_h$ decays to zero when $p > 0$ (stable pole). In the steady state, only $y_p$ remains. In electrical circuits: $y_h$ is the transient; $y_p$ is the steady-state regime.

</Insight>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

Imagine a <strong>bucket</strong> with a hole in the bottom. Water flows in from a tap (input) and flows out through the hole (the water exits faster the fuller the bucket is). The question is: how much water is in the bucket at any given moment?

This problem of "water entering and leaving at the same time" is exactly a 1st-order linear ODE. We figure out how much is inside <strong>for every instant</strong>.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

In the last lesson, separable ODEs were easy to solve because you could put everything with $y$ on one side and everything with $x$ on the other.

But $y' + 3y = x$ <strong>cannot</strong> be separated like that. We need a different trick: the <strong>integrating factor</strong>.

The idea: multiply the equation by a special function $\mu$ such that the left side becomes exactly $(\mu y)'$. For $y' + 3y = x$, the factor is $\mu = e^{3x}$:

$$e^{3x} y' + 3 e^{3x} y = x e^{3x} \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; \bigl(e^{3x} y\bigr)' = x e^{3x}$$

Now integrate both sides and you're done. The recipe is always $\mu = e^{\int p\,dx}$.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old">

The equation $y' + p(x)y = q(x)$ is <strong>linear</strong> because $y$ appears only with exponent 1 (no $y^2$ or $y \cdot y'$). This is very important: linear equations have a guaranteed and unique solution.

### Step-by-step method

1. Put it in standard form $y' + p\,y = q$ (divide by the coefficient of $y'$ if necessary).
2. Calculate $\mu = e^{\int p\,dx}$ (any primitive, no constant).
3. The ODE becomes $(\mu y)' = \mu q$.
4. Integrate: $\mu y = \int \mu q\,dx + C$.
5. Isolate: $y = \frac{1}{\mu}\bigl(\int \mu q\,dx + C\bigr)$.
6. Use $y(x_0) = y_0$ to determine $C$.

### Complete example

$y' + 2x y = 4x$, $y(0) = 5$.

- $p = 2x$, so $\mu = e^{x^2}$.
- $(e^{x^2} y)' = 4x e^{x^2}$.
- $e^{x^2} y = 2 e^{x^2} + C \Rightarrow y = 2 + C e^{-x^2}$.
- $y(0) = 5 \Rightarrow C = 3$. Answer: $y = 2 + 3e^{-x^2}$.

### Solution structure

$y = \underbrace{C e^{-\int p\,dx}}_{y_h} + \underbrace{y_p}_{\text{particular}}$.

The $y_h$ part decays (if $p > 0$) or grows (if $p < 0$). The $y_p$ part is the response to the forcing $q$.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

1st-order linear ODEs govern <strong>all 1st-order dynamics</strong>: thermodynamics, control, chemistry, biology.

### Standard control form

$$\tau \dot y + y = K\,u(t)$$

$\tau$ = time constant; $K$ = static gain; $u$ = input.

### Responses to typical inputs

| Input $u(t)$ | Response $y(t)$ (zero IC) |
|---|---|
| Step $1$ | $K(1 - e^{-t/\tau})$ |
| Ramp $t$ | $K\bigl[t - \tau(1 - e^{-t/\tau})\bigr]$ |
| Impulse $\delta(t)$ | $(K/\tau)\,e^{-t/\tau}$ |
| $\sin\omega t$ | $\dfrac{K}{\sqrt{1+\omega^2\tau^2}}\sin(\omega t - \arctan\omega\tau)$ |

### Transfer function (Laplace)

For constant $p$: $(s + p) Y(s) = Q(s) + y(0)$. Transfer function $H(s) = 1/(s+p)$ — single pole at $s = -p$.

- $p > 0$: stable (pole in the left half-plane).
- $p < 0$: unstable.

### Superposition principle

Since the ODE is linear, if $y_1$ solves $\mathcal{L}y = q_1$ and $y_2$ solves $\mathcal{L}y = q_2$, then $\alpha y_1 + \beta y_2$ solves $\mathcal{L}y = \alpha q_1 + \beta q_2$. The basis of all Fourier and Laplace analysis.

### Immediate applications

| System | ODE | $\tau$ |
|---|---|---|
| RC Circuit | $RC\,\dot V + V = V_s$ | $RC$ |
| RL Circuit | $(L/R)\,\dot i + i = V/R$ | $L/R$ |
| CSTR Reactor | $(V/Q)\,\dot c + c = c_{in}$ | $V/Q$ |
| Thermometer | $\tau\,\dot T + T = T_{\text{amb}}$ | thermal $\tau$ |

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

The 1st-order linear ODE lives at the intersection of <strong>operator algebra</strong>, <strong>functional analysis</strong>, and <strong>classical control theory</strong>.

### Operator view

Define $\mathcal{L} = \tfrac{d}{dx} + p(x)$ — linear operator on $C^1(I)$. The ODE is $\mathcal{L}y = q$. The <strong>inverse</strong> of $\mathcal{L}$ is a convolution integral with the <strong>Green's function</strong>:

$$y(x) = \int_{x_0}^{x} G(x, s)\,q(s)\,ds + y_h(x), \quad G(x,s) = \exp\!\left(-\int_s^x p(t)\,dt\right)$$

### Low-pass filter

For constant $p$, $\tau = 1/p$: $H(j\omega) = 1/(1 + j\omega\tau)$.

$$|H(j\omega)| = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\omega^2\tau^2}}, \quad \angle H = -\arctan(\omega\tau)$$

Cutoff frequency $\omega_c = 1/\tau$. Attenuation of $-20$ dB/decade above $\omega_c$.

### Riccati equation

$y' = a(x)y^2 + b(x)y + c(x)$ — non-linear. With substitution $y = -u'/(au)$ it converts to a 2nd-order linear ODE. Connection to quantum mechanics (Darboux potential).

### Distributions and Green

$G'(x,s) + pG(x,s) = \delta(x-s)$, with $G = H(x-s)\,e^{-p(x-s)}$ (Heaviside). Causal: the output at $x$ depends only on $s \leq x$.

### Stability via Lyapunov

For $\dot y + py = 0$ with $p > 0$: $V(y) = y^2/2$ is a Lyapunov function. $\dot V = -py^2 \leq 0$ — global exponential stability.

### Nobel in cosmology

In slow-roll inflation models, the perturbation $\delta\phi$ of the inflationary scalar field obeys a 1st-order linear ODE in Hubble derivatives. Saul Perlmutter, Brian Schmidt, and Adam Riess received the [2011 Nobel Prize in Physics](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/2011/summary/) for the discovery of the accelerating expansion of the universe — the perturbative formalism supporting it uses exactly this structure.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practical application">

### Complete recipe

1. <strong>Canonical form</strong>: $y' + p(x)y = q(x)$. If you have $a(x)y' + b(x)y = c(x)$, divide everything by $a(x)$.
2. <strong>Calculate</strong> $\mu = e^{\int p\,dx}$ (do not add $+C$ here).
3. <strong>Multiply</strong>: $(\mu y)' = \mu q$.
4. <strong>Integrate</strong>: $\mu y = \int \mu q\,dx + C$.
5. <strong>Isolate</strong>: $y = \mu^{-1}\left(\int \mu q\,dx + C\right)$.
6. <strong>Use</strong> $y(x_0) = y_0$ to find $C$.

### Classic errors

- Not dividing by the coefficient of $y'$ before identifying $p$.
- Sign error: $y' - 2y = q$ has $p = -2$, so $\mu = e^{-2x}$.
- Adding $+C$ when calculating $\mu$ (unnecessary — any constant cancels out).
- Forgetting integration by parts in $\int \mu q\,dx$ when $\mu q$ is a product of a polynomial and an exponential.

### Model: bank account with continuous deposit

Balance $S$ earns $r = 5\%$ per year and receives a continuous deposit of $d$ per year:

$$\dot S = rS + d \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \dot S - rS = d$$

Factor: $\mu = e^{-rt}$. Solution: $S(t) = S_0 e^{rt} + \dfrac{d}{r}(e^{rt} - 1)$.

For $S_0 = 0$, $d = 12,000$/year, $r = 0.05$: after 10 years, $S(10) = 12,000 \cdot (e^{0.5}-1)/0.05 \approx 155,262$.

### Numerical workflow (Python)

```python
from scipy.integrate import odeint
import numpy as np

p, q = lambda x: 2*x, lambda x: 4*x
rhs = lambda y, x: q(x) - p(x)*y[0]

x = np.linspace(0, 3, 300)
sol = odeint(rhs, [5], x)
# Check: y = 2 + 3*exp(-x**2)
```

## Solved examples

<Exemplo titulo="Simplest ODE with IC" numero="1">

**Problem.** Solve $y' + 2y = 6$, $y(0) = 1$.

**Strategy.** Identify $p = 2$ (constant), calculate $\mu$, integrate.

**Resolution.**

$p = 2$, $q = 6$. Factor: $\mu = e^{2x}$.

$(e^{2x} y)' = 6 e^{2x}$.

$e^{2x} y = 3 e^{2x} + C \;\Rightarrow\; y = 3 + C e^{-2x}$.

$y(0) = 1 \;\Rightarrow\; 1 = 3 + C \;\Rightarrow\; C = -2$.

$$y(x) = 3 - 2 e^{-2x}$$

**Verification.** $y' = 4 e^{-2x}$. Then $y' + 2y = 4e^{-2x} + 6 - 4e^{-2x} = 6$. $y(0) = 3 - 2 = 1$. Correct.

**Source.** [Lebl, Notes on Diffy Qs, §1.4, Example 1.4.1](https://www.jirka.org/diffyqs/html/firstode_section.html) — CC-BY-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Integrating factor with variable p" numero="2">

**Problem.** Solve $y' + 2xy = 4x$, $y(0) = 5$.

**Strategy.** $p = 2x$ is not constant; $\mu = e^{x^2}$.

**Resolution.**

$\mu = e^{\int 2x\,dx} = e^{x^2}$.

$(e^{x^2} y)' = 4x e^{x^2}$.

$e^{x^2} y = \int 4x e^{x^2}\,dx = 2e^{x^2} + C \;\Rightarrow\; y = 2 + Ce^{-x^2}$.

$y(0) = 5 \;\Rightarrow\; C = 3$.

$$y(x) = 2 + 3e^{-x^2}$$

**Verification.** $y' = -6x e^{-x^2}$. $y' + 2xy = -6xe^{-x^2} + 2x(2+3e^{-x^2}) = 4x$. Correct.

**Source.** [Lebl, Notes on Diffy Qs, §1.4, Example 1.4.2](https://www.jirka.org/diffyqs/html/firstode_section.html) — CC-BY-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="ODE with variable y coefficient (trigonometry)" numero="3">

**Problem.** Solve $y' + y\tan x = \sec x$.

**Strategy.** $p = \tan x$; $\int \tan x\,dx = -\ln|\cos x|$; so $\mu = \sec x$.

**Resolution.**

$\mu = e^{-\ln|\cos x|} = \sec x$.

$(\sec x\cdot y)' = \sec^2 x$.

$y\sec x = \tan x + C \;\Rightarrow\; y = \sin x + C\cos x$.

**Verification.** $y' = \cos x - C\sin x$. $y' + y\tan x = \cos x - C\sin x + (\sin x + C\cos x)\tan x = \cos x - C\sin x + \sin x \tan x + C\sin x = \cos x + \sin x\tan x = \cos x + \sin^2 x/\cos x = (cos^2 x + \sin^2 x)/\cos x = \sec x$. Correct.

**Source.** [Trench, Elementary Differential Equations, §2.1, Example 2.1.3](https://digitalcommons.trinity.edu/mono/9/) — open access.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="RC circuit with step source" numero="4">

**Problem.** RC circuit: $R = 2\,\text{k}\Omega$, $C = 50\,\mu\text{F}$, source $V_s = 12\,\text{V}$ (switched at $t = 0$), $V_C(0) = 0$. Find $V_C(t)$ and calculate $\tau$.

**Strategy.** The RC circuit ODE is $\dot V_C + V_C/(RC) = V_s/(RC)$. Identify $p = 1/(RC)$, $q = V_s/(RC)$.

**Resolution.**

$\tau = RC = 2000 \times 50 \times 10^{-6} = 0.1\,\text{s}$.

ODE: $\dot V_C + V_C/\tau = V_s/\tau$.

$\mu = e^{t/\tau}$. $(e^{t/\tau} V_C)' = (V_s/\tau)e^{t/\tau}$.

$e^{t/\tau} V_C = V_s e^{t/\tau} + C \;\Rightarrow\; V_C = V_s + Ce^{-t/\tau}$.

$V_C(0) = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; C = -V_s$.

$$V_C(t) = 12\bigl(1 - e^{-t/0.1}\bigr)\,\text{V}$$

At $t = \tau = 0.1\,\text{s}$: $V_C \approx 12(1 - 1/e) \approx 7.58\,\text{V}$ (63% of 12 V). At $5\tau = 0.5\,\text{s}$: $V_C \approx 11.9\,\text{V}$ (99%).

**Verification.** $\dot V_C = 12/0.1 \cdot e^{-10t} = 120 e^{-10t}$. $\dot V_C + 10 V_C = 120 e^{-10t} + 120(1 - e^{-10t}) = 120 = V_s/\tau$. Correct.

**Source.** [OpenStax Calculus Volume 2, §4.5, Example 4.29](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-2/pages/4-5-first-order-linear-equations) — CC-BY-NC-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Mixing: tank with salt" numero="5">

**Problem.** A 200 L tank initially contains 50 g of dissolved salt. Brine enters at 2 g/L at a rate of 4 L/min; the mixture exits at the same rate. Amount of salt $Q(t)$ after 1 hour.

**Strategy.** Mass balance: $\dot Q = \text{input} - \text{output} = 4\cdot 2 - 4\cdot(Q/200)$.

**Resolution.**

$\dot Q + Q/50 = 8$, $Q(0) = 50$.

$p = 1/50$, $\mu = e^{t/50}$.

$(e^{t/50} Q)' = 8 e^{t/50}$.

$Q = 400 + Ce^{-t/50}$.

$Q(0) = 50 \;\Rightarrow\; C = -350$.

$$Q(t) = 400 - 350\,e^{-t/50}\;\text{(g)}$$

At $t = 60\,\text{min}$: $Q(60) = 400 - 350\,e^{-1.2} \approx 400 - 350 \times 0.3012 \approx 294.6\,\text{g}$.

**Verification.** $\dot Q = 7 e^{-t/50}$. $\dot Q + Q/50 = 7e^{-t/50} + 8 - 7e^{-t/50} = 8$. Correct.

**Source.** [Lebl, Notes on Diffy Qs, §1.5, Example 1.5.1](https://www.jirka.org/diffyqs/html/auteq_section.html) — CC-BY-SA.

</Exemplo>
