### Malthus model (1798)

<Definicao titulo="Malthusian growth">

$$\dot P = rP, \quad P(0) = P_0$$

- $r > 0$: exponential growth $P(t) = P_0 e^{rt}$.
- $r < 0$: exponential decay.
- Unique equilibrium: $P^* = 0$ (unstable for $r > 0$, stable for $r < 0$).

</Definicao>

> "If the rate of change of the population is proportional to the population itself, we get the Malthusian model." — [Lebl, Notes on Diffy Qs, §1.8](https://www.jirka.org/diffyqs/html/auteq_section.html)

### Logistic model (Verhulst, 1838)

<Definicao titulo="Verhulst's logistic equation">

$$\dot P = rP\left(1 - \frac{P}{K}\right)$$

- $r$: intrinsic growth rate (dimension: 1/time).
- $K$: **carrying capacity**.
- The equation is **separable and nonlinear**.

</Definicao>

> "The logistic equation is another separable equation... The assumption is that the rate of growth of the population is proportional to the current population, but decreases as the population approaches the carrying capacity." — [OpenStax Calculus Volume 2, §4.4](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-2/pages/4-4-the-logistic-equation)

### Closed-form solution

Via partial fractions:

$$P(t) = \frac{K}{1 + \left(\dfrac{K - P_0}{P_0}\right)e^{-rt}}$$

### Equilibrium analysis

<Teorema titulo="Equilibria and stability of the logistic equation">

The two equilibria of $\dot P = rP(1 - P/K)$ with $r, K > 0$ are:

- $P^* = 0$: **unstable** (any positive perturbation grows).
- $P^* = K$: **stable** (any perturbation is absorbed).

The inflection point of the sigmoid curve occurs at $P = K/2$.

</Teorema>

<Insight>

The inflection point at $K/2$ is the point of maximum absolute growth rate. Before $K/2$, $\ddot P > 0$ (acceleration); after, $\ddot P < 0$ (deceleration). Optimal action point in fisheries and forest management.

</Insight>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

Imagine a fishery in a pond. At first there are few fish and they reproduce quickly. But when the pond fills up, there is not enough food for everyone and reproduction slows down.

In **Malthus**' model: fish keep growing forever (as if the pond were infinite).

In **Verhulst**'s model: there is a limit — the pond can support at most $K$ fish. When it is full, it stops growing.

The logistic curve has an **S shape**: it starts slow, accelerates in the middle, then saturates.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

Thomas Malthus (1798) was a priest-economist who said: "populations double in fixed periods." This is **exponential growth**: $P(t) = P_0 e^{rt}$.

The problem: exponential growth never stops, which is impossible in reality.

Pierre-François Verhulst (1838) corrected this: the closer you get to the limit $K$ (food, space, resources), the slower growth becomes. The equation is:

$$\dot P = rP\left(1 - \frac{P}{K}\right)$$

When $P$ is small, the factor $(1 - P/K) \approx 1$ and growth is nearly exponential. When $P \to K$, the factor $\to 0$ and growth stops.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old teen">

### The two models side by side

**Malthus**: $\dot P = rP$. Separable, simple solution $P_0 e^{rt}$. Grows without limit.

**Verhulst**: $\dot P = rP(1 - P/K)$. Also separable, but with a damping factor.

### Solving Verhulst (step by step)

Separate variables and use partial fractions:

$$\frac{dP}{P(1 - P/K)} = r\,dt$$

$$\frac{K\,dP}{P(K - P)} = r\,dt \;\Rightarrow\; \left(\frac{1}{P} + \frac{1}{K-P}\right)dP = r\,dt$$

Integrating: $\ln P - \ln(K-P) = rt + C_0$, or $\ln\left(\dfrac{P}{K-P}\right) = rt + C_0$.

Exponentiating: $\dfrac{P}{K-P} = A e^{rt}$ where $A = (K-P_0)/P_0$ from the IC. Solving:

$$P(t) = \frac{K}{1 + Ae^{-rt}}, \quad A = \frac{K - P_0}{P_0}$$

### Behavior

- $P_0 < K/2$: S-curve with inflection point at $P = K/2$.
- $P_0 > K$: exponential decay down to $K$.
- $t \to \infty$: $P \to K$ always (if $r > 0$).

### Stability

Linear analysis: $f(P) = rP(1-P/K)$. $f'(K) = -r < 0$: stable. $f'(0) = r > 0$: unstable.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

### Stability analysis via linearization

Equilibria $P^* = 0$ and $P^* = K$. Perturbation $p = P - P^*$:

**At $P^* = K$**: $\dot p = f'(K)\,p = -r\,p$. Exponentially stable with rate $r$.

**At $P^* = 0$**: $\dot p = f'(0)\,p = r\,p$. Unstable.

### Inflection point as management target

$\ddot P = 0 \Rightarrow P = K/2$. Maximum of $\dot P$: in sustainable fisheries management, it is recommended to maintain $P \approx K/2$ to maximize annual harvest (Maximum Sustainable Yield, MSY).

MSY = $\dfrac{rK}{4}$ (harvest rate at the peak).

### Generalized logistic model

Generalization: $\dot P = rP^m(1-(P/K)^n)$. Reduces to logistic for $m = n = 1$.

### Competition and predation models (extension)

Lotka-Volterra (2 species):

$$\dot x = ax - bxy, \quad \dot y = -cy + dxy$$

Equivalence: each $\dot x = ax - bxy = x(a - by)$ is a logistic in $x$ with dynamic capacity $a/b$ varying with $y$. Solution: closed trajectories in $(x, y)$.

### Application to epidemiology (SIR)

The SIR model of Kermack-McKendrick (1927) has $\dot I = \beta SI - \gamma I = I(\beta S - \gamma)$ — analogous to logistic with $S$ replacing $K$.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

### History and context

Malthus published "An Essay on the Principle of Population" (1798) as a political response to Condorcet and Godwin — not as a mathematical treatise. Verhulst (1838) read Malthus, recognized the impossibility of unlimited growth, and proposed the logistic equation that now bears his name.

The logistic equation reappears in:
- **Statistical thermodynamics**: the Fermi-Dirac equation $n(\varepsilon) = 1/(e^{(\varepsilon-\mu)/kT}+1)$ is logistic in $\varepsilon$.
- **Neuroscience**: sigmoid activation functions in neural networks.
- **Epidemiology**: the herd immunity threshold at $R_0 = 1$ is analogous to the unstable equilibrium $P^* = 0$.

### Bifurcation diagram

For $\dot P = rP(1-P/K) - H$ (harvesting $H$):
- $H < rK/4$: two equilibria (one stable, one unstable).
- $H = rK/4$: saddle-node bifurcation (the two equilibria collide).
- $H > rK/4$: no equilibrium — inevitable extinction.

This is the **fishery collapse point** — a phenomenon that Elinor Ostrom studied in commons governance ([Nobel Prize in Economics 2009](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/2009/summary/)).

### Richards equation

Generalization: $\dot P = rP\left[1 - (P/K)^m\right]$. The exponent $m$ controls the asymmetry of the sigmoid curve. For $m = 1$: Verhulst logistic. For $m \to 0$: Gompertz.

### Logistic map (discrete time)

$P_{n+1} = rP_n(1 - P_n)$ — May's logistic map (1976). For $r > 3.57$: deterministic chaos. Connection with Feigenbaum universality: $\delta = 4.669...$

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practical application">

### How to recognize and use the model

**Malthus** (exponential): when there is no known resource limitation. E.g., bacterial growth in early hours, pandemic at start (before interventions).

**Verhulst** (logistic): when saturation exists. E.g., diffusion of new product in a market, spread of news on social media, growth of a colony on a Petri plate.

### Fitting real data: IBGE

Brazil in 1960: $P_0 = 70$ M. In 2000: $P = 170$ M. Average rate: $r \approx 2.2\%$/year.

With $K = 250$ M (capacity estimate): $A = (250-70)/70 \approx 2.57$.

$$P(t) = \frac{250}{1 + 2.57\,e^{-0.022t}}$$

In 2024 ($t = 64$): $P \approx 217$ M — close to real data (214 M, IBGE 2023).

### Cheat sheet

| Quantity | Malthus | Verhulst |
|---|---|---|
| $P(t)$ | $P_0 e^{rt}$ | $K/(1 + A e^{-rt})$ |
| $P(\infty)$ | $\infty$ | $K$ |
| Inflection | none | $t^* = \ln(A)/r$ |
| $P(t^*)$ | — | $K/2$ |

### Python workflow

```python
from scipy.integrate import odeint
import numpy as np

r, K, P0 = 0.022, 250, 70
f = lambda P, t: r * P * (1 - P/K)
t = np.linspace(0, 100, 500)
sol = odeint(f, P0, t)
# Compare with closed-form solution
P_exato = K / (1 + ((K - P0)/P0) * np.exp(-r*t))
```

## Worked examples

<Exemplo titulo="Malthusian growth with doubling" numero="1">

**Problem.** A bacterial colony doubles every 20 minutes. How many bacteria are there after 2 hours if $P_0 = 1000$?

**Strategy.** Malthus model: $P(t) = P_0 e^{rt}$. Calculate $r$ from the doubling period $T_2 = 20$ min.

**Solution.**

$P(20) = 2P_0 \Rightarrow e^{20r} = 2 \Rightarrow r = \ln 2 / 20 \approx 0.0347\,\text{min}^{-1}$.

At $t = 120$ min: $P(120) = 1000 \cdot e^{120 \cdot \ln 2/20} = 1000 \cdot 2^6 = 64,000$.

**Verification.** In 2 hours = 6 doubling periods: $1000 \times 2^6 = 64,000$. Correct.

**Source.** [Lebl, Notes on Diffy Qs, §1.3, Example 1.3.1](https://www.jirka.org/diffyqs/html/auteq_section.html) — CC-BY-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Logistic with carrying capacity" numero="2">

**Problem.** A deer population in a reserve has $K = 1200$, $r = 0.4$/year, $P(0) = 200$. Find $P(t)$ and calculate when $P = 900$.

**Strategy.** Closed-form formula for logistic. For $P = 900$: solve in $t$.

**Solution.**

$A = (K - P_0)/P_0 = (1200 - 200)/200 = 5$.

$$P(t) = \frac{1200}{1 + 5e^{-0.4t}}$$

For $P = 900$: $900(1 + 5e^{-0.4t}) = 1200 \Rightarrow 1 + 5e^{-0.4t} = 4/3 \Rightarrow e^{-0.4t} = 1/15$.

$t = \ln(15)/0.4 \approx 2.71/0.4 \approx 6.8$ years.

**Verification.** $P(6.8) = 1200/(1 + 5e^{-2.71}) \approx 1200/(1 + 5 \times 0.0667) \approx 1200/1.333 \approx 900$. Correct.

**Source.** [OpenStax Calculus Volume 2, §4.4, Example 4.14](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-2/pages/4-4-the-logistic-equation) — CC-BY-NC-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Derivation of logistic solution by partial fractions" numero="3">

**Problem.** Derive the closed-form formula of the logistic from $\dot P = rP(1 - P/K)$, $P(0) = P_0$.

**Strategy.** Separate variables and decompose into partial fractions.

**Solution.**

$$\frac{dP}{P(1-P/K)} = r\,dt \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{K\,dP}{P(K-P)} = r\,dt$$

Partial fractions: $\dfrac{K}{P(K-P)} = \dfrac{1}{P} + \dfrac{1}{K-P}$.

Integrating: $\ln P - \ln(K-P) = rt + C_0 \Rightarrow \ln\!\left(\dfrac{P}{K-P}\right) = rt + C_0$.

Let $A = e^{C_0}$: $\dfrac{P}{K-P} = A e^{rt}$.

Solving: $P = Ae^{rt}(K-P) \Rightarrow P(1 + Ae^{rt}) = KAe^{rt} \Rightarrow P = \dfrac{KAe^{rt}}{1+Ae^{rt}} = \dfrac{K}{1 + A^{-1}e^{-rt}}$.

For $t = 0$: $P_0 = K/(1+1/A) \Rightarrow A = (K-P_0)/P_0$.

$$P(t) = \frac{K}{1 + \frac{K-P_0}{P_0}\,e^{-rt}}$$

**Verification.** $P(0) = K/(1+(K-P_0)/P_0) = KP_0/K = P_0$. $\lim_{t\to\infty} P(t) = K$. Correct.

**Source.** [Lebl, Notes on Diffy Qs, §1.7, Example 1.7.1](https://www.jirka.org/diffyqs/html/auteq_section.html) — CC-BY-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Inflection point and maximum sustainable yield" numero="4">

**Problem.** For the logistic with $r = 0.3$/year, $K = 500$, $P_0 = 50$: (a) find the time of inflection $t^*$; (b) calculate the maximum sustainable harvest rate (MSY).

**Strategy.** Inflection when $P = K/2 = 250$. MSY = maximum value of $\dot P$.

**Solution.**

**(a)** With $A = (500-50)/50 = 9$: $P(t^*) = 250 \Rightarrow 500/(1+9e^{-0.3t^*}) = 250 \Rightarrow 9e^{-0.3t^*} = 1 \Rightarrow t^* = \ln(9)/0.3 \approx 7.3$ years.

**(b)** $\dot P_{\max} = rK/4 = 0.3 \times 500/4 = 37.5$ individuals/year (occurs at $P = 250$).

**Verification.** $\dot P(250) = 0.3 \times 250 \times (1-250/500) = 75 \times 0.5 = 37.5$. Correct.

**Source.** [OpenStax Calculus Volume 2, §4.4, Exercise 186](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-2/pages/4-4-the-logistic-equation) — CC-BY-NC-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Comparison Malthus vs logistic on IBGE data" numero="5">

**Problem.** Brazil: $P_0 = 95$ M (1970), $r = 2.5\%$/year. Using $K = 250$ M, compare the Malthusian prediction with the logistic for 2024 ($t = 54$ years). Real data: 214 M.

**Strategy.** Calculate both formulas for $t = 54$.

**Solution.**

**Malthus**: $P(54) = 95 \cdot e^{0.025 \times 54} = 95 \cdot e^{1.35} \approx 95 \times 3.857 \approx 366$ M.

**Verhulst**: $A = (250-95)/95 \approx 1.632$. $P(54) = 250/(1 + 1.632\,e^{-1.35}) = 250/(1 + 1.632 \times 0.259) \approx 250/1.423 \approx 176$ M.

Relative error: Malthus +71%; Verhulst +18%.

**Observation.** No model captures migration, urbanization, and declining fertility rate. The logistic is better, but $r$ changed over time.

**Source.** [Lebl, Notes on Diffy Qs, §1.7, Exercise 1.7.4](https://www.jirka.org/diffyqs/html/auteq_section.html) — CC-BY-SA.

</Exemplo>
