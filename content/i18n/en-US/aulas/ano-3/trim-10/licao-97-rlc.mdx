### Kirchhoff's Voltage Law

In a series $RLC$ circuit with source $V(t)$, the sum of voltage drops equals the source:

$$V_L + V_R + V_C = V(t)$$

Using $V_L = L\,\dot I$, $V_R = RI$, $V_C = Q/C$ and $I = \dot Q$:

<Equation explicacao="Differential equation of the series RLC circuit — 2nd-order linear ODE with constant coefficients." latex="L\,\ddot{Q} + R\,\dot{Q} + \frac{Q}{C} = V(t)" />

<Definicao titulo="Canonical parameters of the RLC circuit">

Dividing by $L$:

$$\ddot{Q} + 2\zeta\omega_0\,\dot{Q} + \omega_0^2\,Q = \frac{V(t)}{L}$$

- **Undamped natural frequency:** $\omega_0 = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{LC}}$ [rad/s]
- **Damping coefficient:** $\zeta = \dfrac{R}{2}\sqrt{\dfrac{C}{L}} = \dfrac{R}{2L}\cdot\dfrac{1}{\omega_0}$
- **Quality factor:** $Q_f = \dfrac{1}{2\zeta} = \dfrac{1}{R}\sqrt{\dfrac{L}{C}}$
- **Damped frequency:** $\omega_d = \omega_0\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}$ (valid for $\zeta < 1$)

</Definicao>

> "The equation $LQ'' + RQ' + Q/C = V(t)$ is the standard form of the RLC circuit equation and has exactly the same mathematical form as the damped mass-spring system $mx'' + cx' + kx = F(t)$, with $L$ playing the role of mass, $R$ the damping constant, and $1/C$ the spring constant." — [Lebl, Notes on Diffy Qs §2.6](https://www.jirka.org/diffyqs/html/sec_rlc.html)

### Classification by characteristic equation

Homogeneous equation ($V = 0$): $L\lambda^2 + R\lambda + 1/C = 0$.

$\Delta = R^2 - 4L/C$

<Definicao titulo="Three free response regimes">

- **Overdamped** ($\zeta > 1$, $\Delta > 0$): two negative real $\lambda$. Slow exponential return to equilibrium.
  $$Q_h(t) = C_1 e^{\lambda_1 t} + C_2 e^{\lambda_2 t}$$
- **Critically damped** ($\zeta = 1$, $\Delta = 0$): double $\lambda = -R/(2L)$. Fastest return without oscillation.
  $$Q_h(t) = (C_1 + C_2 t)\,e^{\lambda t}$$
- **Underdamped** ($\zeta < 1$, $\Delta < 0$): $\lambda = -\zeta\omega_0 \pm i\omega_d$. Damped oscillation.
  $$Q_h(t) = e^{-\zeta\omega_0 t}\bigl(C_1\cos\omega_d t + C_2\sin\omega_d t\bigr)$$

</Definicao>

### Steady-state response (forced)

For $V(t) = V_0\cos(\omega t)$, particular solution:

$$Q_p(t) = \frac{V_0/L}{\sqrt{(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)^2 + (2\zeta\omega_0\omega)^2}}\cos(\omega t - \phi)$$

with $\tan\phi = \dfrac{2\zeta\omega_0\omega}{\omega_0^2-\omega^2}$.

<Teorema titulo="Resonance in the RLC circuit">

The current amplitude $|I| = \omega|Q_p|$ is maximum when $\omega = \omega_0$ (series resonance). At resonance, the impedance is purely resistive: $Z = R$. The peak current is $I_{\max} = V_0/R$.

The quality factor $Q_f = \omega_0 L/R$ measures how much the circuit amplifies at resonance: bandwidth $\Delta\omega = \omega_0/Q_f$.

</Teorema>

<Insight>
An AM radio tunes into a station by adjusting $C$ (variable capacitor) until $\omega_0 = 2\pi f_{\text{station}}$. The bandwidth $\Delta\omega$ determines selectivity: high $Q_f$ — narrow and selective channel; low $Q_f$ — passes multiple stations at once.
</Insight>

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

Imagine a **playground swing**. Three parts control it:

- The **current** you give the swing (to make it move) — this is the **inductor**, which stores motion energy.
- The **invisible spring** that wants to bring it back to the center — this is the **capacitor**, which stores electrical energy.
- The **air friction** that makes it stop — this is the **resistor**.

When you push at the right rhythm for the swing, it goes higher and higher. This is called **resonance**. Your car radio uses exactly this to pick up only one station at a time.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

An **RLC circuit** has three components connected in series:

- **R (resistor)**: turns electricity into heat. It brakes.
- **L (inductor)**: coil of wire. Resists changes in current, like a heavy mass.
- **C (capacitor)**: stores electrical charge, like a spring.

**Kirchhoff's law** says the sum of voltages is zero. This becomes an equation with derivatives:

$$L\,\ddot{Q} + R\,\dot{Q} + \frac{Q}{C} = V(t)$$

It is *mathematically identical* to the mass-spring oscillator you've already seen! Every solution that works for springs works for electrical circuits.

If there is no resistor ($R = 0$), the current oscillates forever — like an ideal pendulum. With a resistor, it dampens.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For the high school student">

The RLC equation is a special case of a **2nd-order linear ODE with constant coefficients**:

$$L\,\ddot{Q} + R\,\dot{Q} + \frac{Q}{C} = V(t)$$

**Step 1 — Homogeneous solution.** Set $V = 0$ and test $Q = e^{\lambda t}$:

$$L\lambda^2 + R\lambda + \frac{1}{C} = 0$$

$\Delta = R^2 - 4L/C$. There are three cases (overdamped, critical, underdamped).

**Step 2 — Particular solution.** For $V(t) = V_0\cos(\omega t)$, guess $Q_p = A\cos\omega t + B\sin\omega t$ and determine $A, B$ by substituting.

**Step 3 — General solution** = homogeneous + particular. Apply initial conditions $Q(0) = Q_0$, $I(0) = \dot Q(0) = I_0$ to find $C_1$ and $C_2$.

**Important quantities:**
- $\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{LC}$: natural frequency.
- $\zeta = R/(2\sqrt{L/C})$: damping factor.
- Resonance: $\omega = \omega_0$ maximizes current.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For the engineering student">

The series RLC circuit is described by:

$$L\,\ddot{Q} + R\,\dot{Q} + \frac{Q}{C} = V(t), \quad Q(0) = Q_0,\ \dot{Q}(0) = I_0$$

**Frequency domain analysis.** Laplace transform ($V$ general):

$$\left(Ls^2 + Rs + \frac{1}{C}\right)\hat{Q}(s) = \hat{V}(s) + LsQ_0 + LI_0 + RQ_0$$

Transfer function (current/voltage, zero initial conditions):

$$H_I(s) = \frac{s\hat{Q}(s)}{\hat{V}(s)} = \frac{s}{Ls^2 + Rs + 1/C}$$

**Impedance:** $Z(j\omega) = R + j\omega L + \frac{1}{j\omega C}$. Series resonance: $\Im(Z) = 0 \Rightarrow \omega_r = \omega_0$.

**Quality factor:** $Q_f = \omega_0 L/R = \frac{1}{\omega_0 RC}$. Measures selectivity — bandwidth $BW = \omega_0/Q_f$ [rad/s].

**Energy:** $E(t) = \frac{1}{2}LI^2 + \frac{Q^2}{2C}$. Dissipated power: $P_R = RI^2$. Reactive power: $Q_{reactive} = I^2(\omega L - 1/(\omega C))$.

**Steady-state response:** $\omega = \omega_0 \Rightarrow I_{\max} = V_0/R$ (limited only by the resistor). For $Q_f \gg 1$, voltage across the capacitor can exceed the source by a factor $Q_f$ — called *voltage gain at resonance*.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For the senior professional">

The series RLC circuit was formally analyzed by Lord Kelvin and Arthur Kennelly in the late 19th century, consolidating AC circuit theory. The systematic electromechanical analogy was explored by James Clerk Maxwell in *Treatise on Electricity and Magnetism* (1873) and later formalized by Gabriel Kron in the theory of electrical machines (1939).

**Systems perspective:** the series RLC is a second-order harmonic oscillator with two degrees of freedom (state $[Q, I]^T$). In state space:

$$\frac{d}{dt}\begin{pmatrix}Q\\I\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}0 & 1\\-\omega_0^2 & -2\zeta\omega_0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}Q\\I\end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix}0\\1/L\end{pmatrix}V(t)$$

The system matrix eigenvalues are the poles $s = -\zeta\omega_0 \pm j\omega_d$. In the complex $s$-plane, the distance to the origin is $\omega_0$, the angle with the negative real axis is $\arccos\zeta$.

**Modern applications:** LC filters in RF (AM radio 535–1605 kHz, FM 88–108 MHz), tank circuits in VCO oscillators, EMI suppression in DC-DC converters, component tolerance analysis (Monte Carlo in SPICE), resonance in electrical transmission networks (the problem of underdamping of 735 kV lines in Brazil after major load disturbances). The quality factor $Q_f$ reappears in optics (Fabry-Pérot cavities), quantum mechanics (resonant states), and cosmology (baryon oscillations before decoupling).

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Applications with real-world data">

**AM Radio Brazil.** CBN São Paulo station broadcasts at 1350 kHz. With $L = 0.5$ mH, what $C$ tunes the receiver?

$\omega_0 = 2\pi \times 1350 \times 10^3 \approx 8.48 \times 10^6$ rad/s.

$C = 1/(L\omega_0^2) = 1/(0.5 \times 10^{-3} \times (8.48 \times 10^6)^2) \approx 27.7$ pF.

A 10–50 pF adjustable capacitor covers the entire AM band (535–1605 kHz).

**INMETRO and EMI standards.** ABNT NBR 15601 requires that household devices do not emit above certain limits from 150 kHz to 30 MHz. Common-mode RLC filters in laptop power supplies attenuate electromagnetic interference — the filter's quality factor must be sufficiently low ($\zeta \approx 0.7$) to avoid resonance peaks that violate limits.

**Power grid.** Reactive compensators (capacitor banks + inductors in CTEEP and Eletrobras substations) form LC circuits tuned near 60 Hz for power factor correction, but with $\zeta$ high enough to avoid uncontrolled resonance.

</Porta>

## Solved examples

<Exemplo numero="97.1" titulo="Regime classification — component data">

**Problem.** Series RLC circuit: $L = 1$ H, $R = 6$ $\Omega$, $C = 1/8$ F. Classify the regime and write the general homogeneous solution.

**Strategy.** Calculate $\Delta = R^2 - 4L/C$; identify the regime; write $Q_h$.

**Resolution.**

$\Delta = 36 - 4(1)(8) = 36 - 32 = 4 > 0$ — **overdamped**.

Characteristic equation: $\lambda^2 + 6\lambda + 8 = 0 \Rightarrow \lambda = (-6 \pm 2)/2$, so $\lambda_1 = -2$, $\lambda_2 = -4$.

$$Q_h(t) = C_1 e^{-2t} + C_2 e^{-4t}$$

**Verification.** $Q_h' = -2C_1 e^{-2t} - 4C_2 e^{-4t}$, $Q_h'' = 4C_1 e^{-2t} + 16C_2 e^{-4t}$. Substituting into $Q'' + 6Q' + 8Q$: collecting $e^{-2t}$: $4C_1 - 12C_1 + 8C_1 = 0$ ✓. Collecting $e^{-4t}$: $16C_2 - 24C_2 + 8C_2 = 0$ ✓.

**Source.** [Lebl, Notes on Diffy Qs §2.6, Example 2.6.1](https://www.jirka.org/diffyqs/html/sec_rlc.html) — CC-BY-SA

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="97.2" titulo="Underdamped regime with initial conditions">

**Problem.** $L = 1$ H, $R = 2$ $\Omega$, $C = 1/2$ F, $V = 0$. Conditions: $Q(0) = 1$ C, $I(0) = 0$ A. Find $Q(t)$.

**Strategy.** Calculate $\Delta$, identify underdamped regime, apply IC to determine $C_1, C_2$.

**Resolution.**

$\Delta = 4 - 4(1)(2) = 4 - 8 = -4 < 0$ — **underdamped**.

$\lambda = -1 \pm i$, so $\alpha = 1$, $\omega_d = 1$.

$$Q_h(t) = e^{-t}(C_1 \cos t + C_2 \sin t)$$

IC: $Q(0) = C_1 = 1$.

$\dot Q = e^{-t}[(-C_1 + C_2\omega_d)\cos t + (-C_2 - C_1\omega_d)\sin t]$... with $\omega_d = 1$:

$\dot Q(0) = -C_1 + C_2 = 0 \Rightarrow C_2 = 1$.

$$Q(t) = e^{-t}(\cos t + \sin t) = \sqrt{2}\,e^{-t}\cos\!\left(t - \frac{\pi}{4}\right)$$

**Verification.** $Q(0) = 1$ ✓; $\dot Q(0) = -1 + 1 = 0$ ✓. Amplitude decays as $\sqrt{2}e^{-t}$ ✓.

**Source.** [Lebl, Notes on Diffy Qs §2.6, Exercise 2.6.2](https://www.jirka.org/diffyqs/html/sec_rlc.html) — CC-BY-SA

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="97.3" titulo="Forced response — steady state">

**Problem.** $L = 0.1$ H, $R = 10$ $\Omega$, $C = 10^{-3}$ F, $V(t) = 50\cos(100t)$ V. Find the steady-state current $I_p(t) = \dot Q_p$.

**Strategy.** Calculate $Z(j\omega)$ and use $\hat I = \hat V/Z$.

**Resolution.**

$\omega = 100$ rad/s. $\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{0.1 \times 10^{-3}} = 100$ rad/s — **resonance!**

$Z(j100) = 10 + j(100 \times 0.1 - 1/(100 \times 10^{-3})) = 10 + j(10 - 10) = 10$ $\Omega$.

$I_p(t) = \frac{50}{10}\cos(100t) = 5\cos(100t)$ A.

At resonance, the impedance is purely resistive and the current reaches its maximum value.

**Verification.** $|Z|_{\omega=100} = 10$ $\Omega$ ✓; $I_{\max} = V_0/R = 50/10 = 5$ A ✓.

**Source.** [OpenStax University Physics Vol. 2, §14.5 Example 14.5](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-2/pages/14-5-resonance-in-an-ac-circuit) — CC-BY

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="97.4" titulo="Quality factor and bandwidth">

**Problem.** AM radio receiver: $L = 0.5$ mH, $C = 25$ pF, $R = 5$ $\Omega$. Calculate $f_0$, $Q_f$ and $\Delta f$ (bandwidth).

**Strategy.** Apply the formulas for $f_0$, $Q_f$ and $BW$ directly.

**Resolution.**

$\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{0.5 \times 10^{-3} \times 25 \times 10^{-12}} = 1/\sqrt{1.25 \times 10^{-14}} \approx 8.94 \times 10^6$ rad/s.

$f_0 = \omega_0/(2\pi) \approx 1.42$ MHz (AM band).

$Q_f = \omega_0 L/R = (8.94 \times 10^6 \times 0.5 \times 10^{-3})/5 = 4470/5 = 894$.

$\Delta f = f_0/Q_f = 1.42 \times 10^6/894 \approx 1.59$ kHz.

AM channel has 10 kHz bandwidth — $Q_f = 894$ is too selective for AM, but suitable for stereo FM.

**Verification.** $\zeta = 1/(2Q_f) = 1/1788 \approx 5.6 \times 10^{-4} \ll 1$ — strongly underdamped ✓.

**Source.** [OpenStax University Physics Vol. 2, §14.6](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-2/pages/14-6-rlc-series-circuits-with-ac) — CC-BY

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="97.5" titulo="LC circuit without resistance — pure oscillation">

**Problem.** $L = 4$ H, $C = 1/16$ F, $R = 0$, $V = 0$. $Q(0) = 0$, $I(0) = 2$ A. Find $Q(t)$ and total energy.

**Strategy.** Undamped case: $\ddot Q + \omega_0^2 Q = 0$, general sine/cosine solution.

**Resolution.**

$\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{4 \times 1/16} = 1/\sqrt{1/4} = 2$ rad/s.

$Q(t) = A\cos(2t) + B\sin(2t)$.

$Q(0) = A = 0 \Rightarrow Q(t) = B\sin(2t)$.

$\dot Q(0) = 2B = 2 \Rightarrow B = 1$.

$$Q(t) = \sin(2t), \quad I(t) = 2\cos(2t)$$

Energy: $E = \frac{1}{2}L I^2 + \frac{Q^2}{2C} = \frac{1}{2}(4)(4\cos^2 2t) + \frac{\sin^2 2t}{2(1/16)} = 8\cos^2 2t + 8\sin^2 2t = 8$ J (constant ✓).

**Source.** [Trench, Elementary Differential Equations §6.2, Exercise 6.2.3](https://digitalcommons.trinity.edu/mono/9/) — open

</Exemplo>
