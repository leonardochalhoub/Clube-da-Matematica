### Ordinary Differential Equation

<Definicao titulo="ODE of order n">

An **ordinary differential equation (ODE) of order $n$** is an equation of the form

$$F\bigl(x,\, y,\, y',\, y'',\, \ldots,\, y^{(n)}\bigr) = 0$$

where $y = y(x)$ is the unknown function and $y^{(k)}$ denotes its $k$-th derivative. A **solution** on an interval $I \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ is a function $y: I \to \mathbb{R}$, $n$ times differentiable on $I$, that satisfies the equation for all $x \in I$.

</Definicao>

> "A differential equation is an equation that contains one or more functions of one independent variable and the derivatives of those functions." — [OpenStax Calculus Volume 2, §4.1](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-2/pages/4-1-basics-of-differential-equations)

### Classification

<Definicao titulo="Order, linearity, homogeneity">

- **Order:** the highest derivative that appears. $y'' + y = 0$ is second-order; $y' + y = x$ is first-order.
- **Linear vs. nonlinear:** linear if $y, y', \ldots, y^{(n)}$ appear *linearly* (no products among them, no nonlinear functions of them). $y' + xy = 1$ is linear; $y' + y^2 = 0$ is not.
- **Homogeneous vs. non-homogeneous** (for linear ODEs): homogeneous if the term without derivatives is zero. $y'' + 3y' + 2y = 0$ is homogeneous; $y'' + 3y' + 2y = \sin x$ is not.
- **Constant vs. variable coefficients:** $y'' + 3y' + 2y = 0$ has constant coefficients; $xy'' + y = 0$ has variable coefficient $x$.

</Definicao>

### General and particular solution

<Definicao titulo="General solution and initial condition">

- **General solution** of an ODE of order $n$: family of functions with $n$ arbitrary constants $C_1, C_2, \ldots, C_n$.
- **Initial condition (IC):** specification of $y(x_0) = y_0$ (and initial derivatives for order $\geq 2$). The IC selects the **particular solution** from the family.

</Definicao>

> "The general solution of $y' = f(x)$ is $y = F(x) + C$, where $F$ is an antiderivative of $f$ and $C$ is an arbitrary constant. To determine a unique value for $C$, an initial condition is required." — [OpenStax Calculus Volume 2, §4.1](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-2/pages/4-1-basics-of-differential-equations)

### Existence and uniqueness (Picard–Lindelöf)

<Teorema titulo="Local existence and uniqueness">

For the IVP $y' = f(x, y)$, $y(x_0) = y_0$: if $f$ and $\partial f/\partial y$ are continuous in an open neighborhood of $(x_0, y_0)$, then there exists an open interval $I$ containing $x_0$ in which the solution exists and is **unique**.

</Teorema>

**Practical consequence:** verify Picard–Lindelöf for every ODE before asserting uniqueness. The ODE $y' = \sqrt{|y|}$, $y(0) = 0$ violates the hypothesis (partial derivative discontinuous at $y = 0$) and has infinitely many solutions.

### The fundamental ODE: exponential growth/decay

<Equation
  latex="y' = ky \implies y(x) = y_0\, e^{kx}"

<Insight>

**Why is exponential the solution?** We want a function whose rate of change is proportional to itself. The exponential $e^{kx}$ has derivative $ke^{kx} = k \cdot e^{kx}$ — exactly itself multiplied by $k$. It is the unique family of functions with this property (up to a constant multiple). This fact justifies the ubiquitous appearance of the exponential in modeling.

</Insight>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

Imagine a bucket under a leaky faucet. Each minute, the same amount of water flows in.

The **rule of change** is: "each minute, 1 cup of water enters." If you know this rule, you can calculate how much water is in the bucket after 10 minutes — just multiply.

A differential equation is exactly that: a **rule for how something changes**. Instead of telling you directly "how much is there," it tells you "how it grows." And your job is to figure out "how much is there" from that rule.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

A regular equation tells you directly what $y$ is: for example, $y = 3x + 2$.

A **differential** equation tells you how $y$ changes: for example, $y' = 2x$ (the rate of change of $y$ is $2x$). To find $y$, you need to **integrate**:

$$y' = 2x \implies y = x^2 + C$$

Why does $C$ appear? Because when you differentiate $x^2 + 5$ you get $2x$, and when you differentiate $x^2 + 100$ you also get $2x$. The constant "disappears" when you differentiate, so when you integrate back, it reappears as an unknown.

To fix $C$, you need an **initial condition**: if we know $y(0) = 3$, we substitute $x = 0$: $3 = 0 + C$, so $C = 3$ and $y = x^2 + 3$.

ODEs describe how populations grow, how medicine leaves the body, how a capacitor discharges.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old teen">

An ODE is an equation involving a function and its derivative. The simplest model:

$$y' = ky$$

It means: the rate of change of $y$ is proportional to the current value of $y$. What behaves like this?

- **Bacteria**: $N' = rN$ — the more bacteria, the faster they multiply.
- **Continuous compound interest**: $S' = rS$ — the more money, the more interest.
- **Radioactive decay**: $N' = -\lambda N$ (negative sign because it decreases).

The solution to $y' = ky$ with $y(0) = y_0$ is $y(t) = y_0 e^{kt}$ — the exponential function. An ODE is the rigorous path to derive this formula from physical laws.

**Identifying ODEs in everyday life:**

| Phrase | Equivalent ODE |
|---|---|
| "grows 5% per year (continuous)" | $y' = 0.05\, y$ |
| "cools proportionally to temperature difference" | $T' = -k(T - T_a)$ |
| "oscillates without damping" | $y'' + \omega^2 y = 0$ |

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

ODEs are the standard language of modeling in engineering and applied sciences. Vibration mechanics, control, electromagnetism, chemical kinetics, pharmacokinetics — all formulate problems as ODEs.

### Most common types of ODE

| Type | Form | Application |
|---|---|---|
| Exponential growth | $y' = ky$ | population, continuous interest |
| Logistic | $y' = r\,y(1 - y/K)$ | growth with capacity limit |
| Newton's cooling | $T' = -k(T - T_a)$ | thermodynamics |
| Mass–spring (SHO) | $y'' + \omega^2 y = 0$ | mechanical vibrations |
| RC circuit | $V' + V/(RC) = 0$ | electronics |

### Modeling pipeline

1. **Identify the quantity** $y$ that evolves and the independent variable $x$ (time, position, etc.).
2. **Write the law of change**: translate physics/biology/economics into a derivative.
3. **Establish initial conditions** (state of the system at $t = 0$).
4. **Classify**: order, linear/nonlinear, homogeneous.
5. **Solve** (analytically if possible; numerically if not — Euler, RK4, implicit methods).
6. **Validate** with real data; iterate the model.

### Phase diagrams for autonomous ODEs

For $y' = f(y)$ (no explicit $x$), the graph of $f(y)$ reveals **equilibrium points** ($f = 0$) and their stability. In the logistic $y' = ry(1 - y/K)$: equilibria at $y = 0$ (unstable) and $y = K$ (stable attractor).

### Connection to Black–Scholes

The Black–Scholes PDE (Lesson 119) is a generalization:

$$\frac{\partial V}{\partial t} + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2} + rS\frac{\partial V}{\partial S} - rV = 0$$

PDE (partial differential equation) because it depends on two variables ($S$ and $t$). But the philosophy is the same: relation between function and its derivatives, solved with boundary conditions.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

### Qualitative theory: Poincaré and Lyapunov

When closed-form solution doesn't exist (common in nonlinear systems), we study:

- **Lyapunov stability**: equilibrium points, attractors, repellers, saddles.
- **Bifurcations**: how the phase portrait changes with parameters (Hopf, pitchfork, saddle-node).
- **Deterministic chaos**: exponential sensitivity to initial conditions (Lorenz 1963 — simplified atmospheric convection equations).

### Stiff ODEs and implicit methods

ODEs with multiple time scales ("stiff"): explicit Euler requires minuscule step $h$, prohibitive computational cost. Solution: **BDF** methods (backward differentiation formula), **Rosenbrock**, **Radau IIA**. In Python: `scipy.integrate.solve_ivp(..., method='BDF')`.

### Stochastic differential equations (SDEs)

$$dX = \mu(X, t)\,dt + \sigma(X, t)\,dW_t$$

Term $dW_t$: increment of Brownian motion. Foundation of quantitative finance (Black–Scholes model derives from Itô SDE), statistical physics, molecular biology. Numerical solution: Euler–Maruyama, Milstein scheme.

### Caution: ODEs without uniqueness

$y' = \sqrt{\lvert y \rvert}$, $y(0) = 0$ has infinitely many solutions — Picard–Lindelöf Lipschitz condition is violated at $y = 0$. Fundamental lesson: **always verify hypotheses** before asserting uniqueness; don't just "apply the theorem."

### Delay differential equations (DDEs)

$y'(t) = f(t, y(t), y(t - \tau))$. Appear in control (sensor lag), epidemiology (incubation period), economics (data lag). The phase space is infinite-dimensional — analysis is radically different.

### Quantum mechanics as an operator ODE

The Schrödinger equation $i\hbar\,\partial_t \psi = \hat{H}\psi$ is a linear ODE in Hilbert space $L^2(\mathbb{R}^3)$. The "Hamiltonian operator" $\hat{H}$ plays the role of $f(y)$ — the conceptual structure is the same as a first-order linear ODE, but in an infinite-dimensional function space.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Application with Brazilian data">

### Continuous interest: Selic and financial application

Target Selic rate: 14.75% per annum (May 2026). Model of continuous interest:

$$S' = 0.1475\, S, \quad S(0) = S_0$$

Solution: $S(t) = S_0\, e^{0.1475\, t}$ (with $t$ in years).

For $S_0 =$ US$ 2000 (approximately R$ 10,000):
- In 1 year: $S(1) = 2000 \cdot e^{0.1475} \approx$ US$ 2318 (approximately R$ 11,588)
- Time to double: $t^* = \ln 2 / 0.1475 \approx 4.7$ years

### Drug half-life

Ibuprofen: half-life $\approx 2$ h in adult organism. Decay ODE:

$$C' = -k\, C, \quad k = \frac{\ln 2}{2} \approx 0.347 \text{ h}^{-1}$$

For initial dose of 400 mg:
- After 6 h: $C(6) = 400\, e^{-0.347 \times 6} \approx 50$ mg (12.5% of dose)

### Coffee cooling

Coffee at 90°C, room at 25°C, constant $k = 0.05$ min$^{-1}$ (typical value for ceramic mug). Newton's law:

$$T' = -0.05\,(T - 25), \quad T(0) = 90$$

Solution: $T(t) = 25 + 65\, e^{-0.05\, t}$.

Temperature after 20 minutes: $T(20) = 25 + 65\, e^{-1} \approx 25 + 23.9 \approx 49$°C — still hot but drinkable.

### Identifying the type before solving

Before integrating any ODE:
1. What is the order? (highest derivative)
2. Is it linear? (y and derivatives appear linearly)
3. Is it separable? (can we write $g(y)\,dy = h(x)\,dx$) — preview Lesson 92
4. Does it have a closed-form solution? Or do we need a numerical method?

</Porta>

## Worked examples

<Exemplo titulo="Verification of solution and initial condition (application)" numero="1">

**Problem:** Verify that $y(x) = 3e^{2x}$ is a solution to $y' = 2y$ with $y(0) = 3$. Then write the general solution and the family of curves.

**Strategy:** To verify a solution, differentiate the candidate function and substitute it into the ODE. The IC determines the constant of the general solution.

**Solution:**

1. **Differentiate** $y = 3e^{2x}$: $y' = 6e^{2x}$.
2. **Substitute into the ODE**: $y' = 6e^{2x}$ and $2y = 2 \cdot 3e^{2x} = 6e^{2x}$. They are equal — verified.
3. **Check IC**: $y(0) = 3e^0 = 3$. Verified.
4. **General solution**: $y = Ce^{2x}$ (family of exponentials). For each value of $C \in \mathbb{R}$, we get a different curve satisfying $y' = 2y$.
5. **IC selects $C = 3$**: from $y(0) = Ce^0 = C = 3$.

**Verification:** $y' = 2Ce^{2x} = 2y$ for any $C$ — the ODE is satisfied by the entire family.

**Source.** [OpenStax — Calculus Volume 2 §4.1, Example 4.2](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-2/pages/4-1-basics-of-differential-equations) — license CC-BY-NC-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Solving by direct integration (intermediate)" numero="2">

**Problem:** Solve the IVP $y'' = 6x$, $y(0) = 1$, $y'(0) = 2$.

**Strategy:** Second-order ODE with no explicit $y$ or $y'$ — solved by two successive integrations, each introducing a constant. The two ICs determine both constants.

**Solution:**

1. **First integration**: $y'' = 6x \implies y' = 3x^2 + C_1$.
2. **Apply IC $y'(0) = 2$**: $2 = 3(0) + C_1 \implies C_1 = 2$. So $y' = 3x^2 + 2$.
3. **Second integration**: $y' = 3x^2 + 2 \implies y = x^3 + 2x + C_2$.
4. **Apply IC $y(0) = 1$**: $1 = 0 + 0 + C_2 \implies C_2 = 1$.
5. **Particular solution**: $y(x) = x^3 + 2x + 1$.

**Verification:** $y' = 3x^2 + 2$; $y'' = 6x$ — satisfies the ODE. $y(0) = 1$ and $y'(0) = 2$ — both ICs satisfied.

**Source.** [Jiří Lebl — Notes on Diffy Qs §0.2](https://www.jirka.org/diffyqs/html/introde_section.html) — license CC-BY-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Modeling: Newton's cooling (intermediate)" numero="3">

**Problem:** A cup of tea at 85°C is placed in a room at 20°C. The cooling constant is $k = 0.04$ min$^{-1}$. (a) Write and solve the ODE. (b) How many minutes does it take for the temperature to reach 50°C?

**Strategy:** Newton's law of cooling: cooling rate is proportional to the difference between object temperature and environment. Substitute $u = T - T_a$ to reduce to $u' = -ku$.

**Solution:**

1. **Set up the ODE**: $T' = -k(T - 20) = -0.04(T - 20)$, $T(0) = 85$.
2. **Substitution $u = T - 20$**: $u' = T' = -0.04\,u$, $u(0) = 65$.
3. **Solution**: $u(t) = 65\,e^{-0.04\,t}$, so $T(t) = 20 + 65\,e^{-0.04\,t}$.
4. **Find $t$ such that $T = 50$**:

$$50 = 20 + 65\,e^{-0.04\,t} \implies e^{-0.04\,t} = \frac{30}{65} \implies t = \frac{-\ln(30/65)}{0.04} \approx \frac{0.771}{0.04} \approx 19.3 \text{ min}$$

**Verification:** $T(19.3) = 20 + 65\,e^{-0.04 \times 19.3} = 20 + 65 \times 0.462 \approx 50$°C. Consistent.

**Source.** [OpenStax — Calculus Volume 2 §4.4, Example 4.14](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-2/pages/4-4-the-logistic-equation) — license CC-BY-NC-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Classification and linearity (intermediate)" numero="4">

**Problem:** Classify each ODE below as to order, linearity, and (if linear) homogeneity:
(a) $y''' - 2y' + y = e^x$ &nbsp; (b) $y'' + (y')^2 = x$ &nbsp; (c) $xy' - y = 0$

**Strategy:** For each equation: (i) identify the highest derivative for the order; (ii) check if $y$ and derivatives appear only linearly (no products among them, no nonlinear functions of them); (iii) for linear ODEs, check if the right-hand side is zero.

**Solution:**

(a) $y''' - 2y' + y = e^x$:
- Order: **3** (highest-order derivative is $y'''$).
- Linear: **yes** — $y$, $y'$, $y'''$ appear with degree 1, no products.
- Homogeneous: **no** — right-hand side $e^x \neq 0$.

(b) $y'' + (y')^2 = x$:
- Order: **2**.
- Linear: **no** — the term $(y')^2$ is quadratic in $y'$.

(c) $xy' - y = 0$:
- Order: **1**.
- Linear: **yes** — $y'$ and $y$ appear with degree 1. The coefficient of $y'$ is $x$ (variable), but this doesn't affect linearity in $y$.
- Homogeneous: **yes** — right-hand side is zero.

**Verification:** Compare with standard form $a_n(x)y^{(n)} + \cdots + a_0(x)y = g(x)$. Linear if all coefficients depend only on $x$.

**Source.** [Jiří Lebl — Notes on Diffy Qs §0.2](https://www.jirka.org/diffyqs/html/introde_section.html) — license CC-BY-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Modeling: radioactive decay (modeling)" numero="5">

**Problem:** Carbon-14 has a half-life of 5730 years. A fossil fragment contains 30% of the carbon-14 of a living organism today. Estimate the age of the fossil.

**Strategy:** Radioactive decay follows $N' = -\lambda N$. Half-life gives $\lambda$. With $N(t)/N_0 = 0.30$, solve for $t$.

**Solution:**

1. **Decay constant**: $\lambda = \ln 2 / \tau_{1/2} = \ln 2 / 5730 \approx 1.21 \times 10^{-4}$ year$^{-1}$.
2. **ODE solution**: $N(t) = N_0\,e^{-\lambda t}$.
3. **Apply condition $N(t)/N_0 = 0.30$**:

$$0.30 = e^{-\lambda t} \implies -\lambda t = \ln(0.30) \implies t = \frac{-\ln(0.30)}{\lambda} = \frac{\ln(10/3)}{1.21 \times 10^{-4}}$$

$$t = \frac{1.204}{1.21 \times 10^{-4}} \approx 9950 \text{ years}$$

4. **Answer**: the fossil is approximately 9,950 years old.

**Verification:** With $t = 5730$ years, $N/N_0 = e^{-(\ln 2 / 5730) \times 5730} = e^{-\ln 2} = 0.5$ (50%) — consistent with the definition of half-life.

**Source.** [Jiří Lebl — Notes on Diffy Qs §1.1](https://www.jirka.org/diffyqs/html/introtode_section.html) — license CC-BY-SA.

</Exemplo>
