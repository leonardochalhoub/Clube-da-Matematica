### Canonical form and separability

<Definicao titulo="First-order separable ODE">

A 1st-order ODE $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = F(x, y)$ is **separable** if $F$ factors as a product of a function of $x$ only and a function of $y$ only:

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = g(x) \cdot h(y), \quad g : I \to \mathbb{R},\; h : J \to \mathbb{R}$$

**Solution method** (assuming $h(y) \neq 0$):

1. Rewrite: $\dfrac{dy}{h(y)} = g(x)\,dx$.
2. Integrate both sides: $\displaystyle\int \frac{dy}{h(y)} = \int g(x)\,dx + C$.
3. Obtain $H(y) = G(x) + C$ where $H' = 1/h$ and $G' = g$.
4. Solve for $y$ (**explicit** solution) if possible; otherwise, leave in **implicit** form $H(y) = G(x) + C$.

</Definicao>

> "A separable equation is actually the first kind of differential equation that can be solved explicitly." — [Lebl, Notes on Diffy Qs, §1.3](https://www.jirka.org/diffyqs/html/separable_section.html)

### Singular solutions (equilibria)

<Definicao titulo="Constant solutions and equilibria">

Values $y^* \in J$ where $h(y^*) = 0$ generate **constant solutions** $y(x) \equiv y^*$, regardless of $g$. These solutions do not arise from the integration process — they must be checked separately after identifying the zeros of $h$.

<Insight>

Dividing by $h(y)$ in the separation method **eliminates** constant solutions. If $y^* = 0$ is a zero of $h$, the solution $y \equiv 0$ might not be contained in the family $H(y) = G(x) + C$ — even if no value of $C$ reproduces it.

</Insight>

</Definicao>

### Existence and uniqueness theorem (Picard-Lindelöf)

<Teorema titulo="Local existence and uniqueness for separable ODEs">

Let $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = g(x)\,h(y)$ with initial condition $y(x_0) = y_0$. If $g$ is continuous on $(x_0 - \delta, x_0 + \delta)$ and $h$ is **Lipschitz continuous** in $y$ in a neighborhood of $y_0$ — that is, there exists $L > 0$ such that $|h(y_1) - h(y_2)| \leq L|y_1 - y_2|$ —, then there exists $\varepsilon > 0$ and a unique solution $y : (x_0 - \varepsilon, x_0 + \varepsilon) \to \mathbb{R}$ satisfying the IVP.

</Teorema>

> "Theorem 1.2.1. If $f(x,y)$ is continuous and $\partial f/\partial y$ is continuous near some $(x_0, y_0)$, then a solution exists for $x$ near $x_0$, and is unique." — [Lebl, Notes on Diffy Qs, §1.2](https://www.jirka.org/diffyqs/html/picard_section.html)

### Direction field and qualitative analysis

<Definicao titulo="Direction field">

For $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = g(x)\,h(y)$, the **direction field** associates each point $(x, y)$ with the vector $(1,\, g(x)h(y))$, indicating the slope of the solution at that point. Isoclines are curves $g(x)h(y) = k$ (constant slope). **Horizontal isoclines** satisfy $h(y^*) = 0$ — these are the equilibria.

</Definicao>

### Osgood's criterion (global existence)

<Teorema titulo="Osgood's criterion for global existence">

For the autonomous ODE $\dot y = h(y)$, the solution with $y(0) = y_0 > 0$ exists for all $t \geq 0$ if and only if

$$\int_{y_0}^{+\infty} \frac{dy}{h(y)} = +\infty.$$

If the integral converges, **finite-time blow-up** occurs — the solution escapes to $+\infty$ at an instant $T < +\infty$ given by $T = \displaystyle\int_{y_0}^{+\infty} dy/h(y)$.

</Teorema>

**Example:** $\dot y = y^2$, $y(0) = 1$. $\displaystyle\int_1^\infty dy/y^2 = 1 < \infty$ — blow-up at $T = 1$.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

Imagine you have a box full of **colored marbles** — half red (that's $x$) and half blue (that's $y$). They are all mixed up.

We want to **separate** them: put the red ones in one cup and the blue ones in another. Then you count each cup **separately**.

That's what "separation of variables" does: it takes everything that is $x$ and puts it on one side; everything that is $y$ goes on the other side. Then you "count" each side (that's what we call integrating). In the end, you find the function that describes the growth or decay.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

A **differential equation** is like a puzzle: someone tells you **how something changes at every moment**, and you need to figure out **how that something looks over time**.

For example: "A bacterium divides in two every hour. At the beginning, there are 100 bacteria. How many will there be after 5 hours?"

In math: $\dfrac{dP}{dt} = P$ (the growth speed is equal to what is already there). To solve it, we use the **separation trick**:

$$\frac{dP}{P} = dt \;\Rightarrow\; \ln P = t + C \;\Rightarrow\; P(t) = P_0 \, e^t$$

With $P_0 = 100$: $P(5) = 100\,e^5 \approx 14,841$ bacteria.

The trick is called **separation of variables**: put everything with $P$ on one side and everything with $t$ on the other, then integrate. It works whenever you can "separate" the two ingredients.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old">

A **separable ODE** has the form $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = g(x)\,h(y)$ — a product of functions, each depending on only one variable. The recipe is:

1. **Separate**: divide by $h(y)$ and multiply by $dx$: $\dfrac{dy}{h(y)} = g(x)\,dx$.
2. **Integrate** on both sides: $\displaystyle\int \frac{dy}{h(y)} = \int g(x)\,dx + C$.
3. **Solve** for $y$, if possible.
4. **Apply** the initial condition to find $C$.

### Classic example — radioactive decay

The decay rate of carbon-14 is proportional to the remaining amount: $\dfrac{dN}{dt} = -k N$.

Separate: $\dfrac{dN}{N} = -k\,dt$.
Integrate: $\ln |N| = -kt + C$.
Exponentiate: $N(t) = N_0 e^{-kt}$.

With $k = \ln 2 / 5730$ (half-life of 5730 years) and $N_0 = 100\%$, after 10,000 years, $N(10000) = e^{-10000k} \approx 29\%$ remains.

### Don't forget constant solutions

If $h(y^*) = 0$ for some $y^*$, then $y \equiv y^*$ is a solution that **does not appear** when you divide by $h(y)$. Always check.

<Insight>

Separation of variables is the only general method that **works without linearity**. Most non-linear ODEs have no analytical solution — but if it's separable, it does.

</Insight>

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

Separables are **the first closed integrable class** of ODEs. Every subsequent technique — integrating factor, Bernoulli substitution, Ricatti equation — reduces the problem to separability or direct integration.

### Classic engineering models

| Phenomenon | ODE | Solution |
|---|---|---|
| Radioactive decay | $\dot N = -kN$ | $N_0 e^{-kt}$ |
| Capacitor discharge | $\dot V = -V/(RC)$ | $V_0 e^{-t/RC}$ |
| Newton's cooling | $\dot T = -k(T - T_a)$ | $(T_0 - T_a)e^{-kt} + T_a$ |
| Malthusian growth | $\dot P = rP$ | $P_0 e^{rt}$ |
| Logistic growth | $\dot P = rP(1-P/K)$ | $K/(1+Ae^{-rt})$ |
| Mixing tank output | $\dot c = -(Q/V)c$ | $c_0 e^{-Qt/V}$ |

### Logistics via partial fractions

The ODE $\dfrac{dP}{dt} = rP\!\left(1 - \dfrac{P}{K}\right)$ requires partial fractions:

$$\int \frac{dP}{P(1-P/K)} = rt + C.$$

Decompose: $\dfrac{1}{P(1-P/K)} = \dfrac{1}{P} + \dfrac{1/K}{1-P/K}$.

Integrate: $\ln P - \ln(1-P/K) = rt + C$, where $P(t) = \dfrac{K}{1 + A e^{-rt}}$ (sigmoid).

### Finite-time blow-up

$\dot y = y^2$, $y(0) = 1 \Rightarrow y(t) = 1/(1-t)$: solution blows up at $t = 1$. **Local solution always exists; global, not necessarily**.

### Cross-domain

- **Chemistry**: 1st-order kinetics $[\mathrm{A}]' = -k[\mathrm{A}]$.
- **Pharmacokinetics**: single-compartment elimination $C(t) = C_0 e^{-k_e t}$.
- **Quantitative Finance**: continuous interest $\dot S = rS \Rightarrow S = S_0 e^{rt}$ — basis of the Black-Scholes model (Lesson 119).
- **Cosmology**: de Sitter expansion $\dot a = H a \Rightarrow a = a_0 e^{Ht}$.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

Separability is a special case of **exact ODEs** and admits interpretation in terms of **Lie symmetries**: the ODE $dy/dx = g(x)h(y)$ is invariant under the group of dilations generated by the vector field $X = g(x)\partial_x + h(y)\partial_y$.

### Geometric view — product manifold

The ODE defines a vector field $V = (1, g(x)h(y))$ in the $(x,y)$ plane. Separability means $V$ decomposes as a tensor product of one-dimensional fields: $V = \partial_x \otimes g \cdot \partial_y \otimes h$. The change of variables $u = \int dy/h(y)$, $v = \int g(x)dx$ rectifies the flow: in $(u,v)$, the ODE becomes $du/dv = 1$ — uniform translation.

### Global existence and Osgood's criterion

The solution to $\dot y = h(y)$, $y(0) = y_0$ exists on $[0, +\infty)$ if and only if $\int_{y_0}^\infty dy/h(y) = +\infty$ (Osgood's criterion). Otherwise, blow-up at $T = \int_{y_0}^\infty dy/h(y) < \infty$.

### Transport PDEs and characteristics

The hyperbolic PDE $\partial_t u + c(x)\partial_x u = 0$ reduces, along the **characteristic curves** $dx/dt = c(x)$, to a separable ODE. The entire Cauchy-Kovalevskaya theory of characteristics passes through this point.

### Hubble's Law and Nobel Prize

The cosmological ODE $\dot a = Ha$ (de Sitter) — with $H$ as the Hubble constant — is the most famous separable ODE in physical cosmology. The discovery of the **accelerated expansion** of the universe, which experimentally confirms $H > 0$ increasing, earned the [2011 Nobel Prize in Physics](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/2011/summary/) to Saul Perlmutter, Brian Schmidt, and Adam Riess.

### Numerical vs. analytical

The integral $\int dy/h(y)$ may be non-elementary — for example, $h(y) = e^{-y^2}$ leads to the error function. In such cases, numerical integration is used (Euler, Runge-Kutta 4 — Lesson 98). The separable structure is still valuable because it reduces a 2D ODE to two 1D problems.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practice — modeling with real data">

### 6-step recipe

1. Identify $g(x)$ and $h(y)$ in the form $dy/dx = g(x)\,h(y)$.
2. Separate: $dy/h(y) = g(x)\,dx$.
3. Integrate each side.
4. Add $+C$ to **only one** side.
5. Solve for $y$ (explicit form if possible).
6. Use the initial condition to fix $C$.

### Coffee cooling (Newton)

Coffee at 90°C cools in a 20°C room. After 5 min, it reached 70°C. When does it reach 30°C?

Newton's Law: $\dfrac{dT}{dt} = -k(T - 20)$.

Separate and integrate: $T(t) = 20 + 70\,e^{-kt}$. From $T(5) = 70$: $k = \dfrac{1}{5}\ln(70/50) \approx 0.0673\,\mathrm{min}^{-1}$.

For $T = 30$: $10 = 70\,e^{-kt}$, where $t = \dfrac{\ln 7}{k} \approx 29.1$ min.

### Medication concentration (pharmacokinetics)

500 mg IV dose. Elimination rate $k_e = 0.1\,\mathrm{h}^{-1}$. When does less than 10% (less than 50 mg) remain?

$C(t) = 500\,e^{-0.1t}$. Solve: $50 = 500\,e^{-0.1t} \Rightarrow t = 10\ln 10 \approx 23.0\,\mathrm{h}$.

### Continuous investment (finance)

Savings with continuous interest at 6% p.a.: $\dot S = 0.06\,S$, $S(0) = 10,000$.

$S(t) = 10,000\,e^{0.06t}$. It doubles when $e^{0.06t} = 2$, i.e., $t = \ln 2 / 0.06 \approx 11.6$ years.

### Common errors

- **Forgetting $+C$**: solution loses the free parameter — family reduced to a point.
- **Forgetting constant solutions**: $h(y^*) = 0$ generates solutions the method doesn't capture.
- **Writing $\ln y$ without absolute value**: must be $\ln |y|$ — domain matters.
- **Not fixing $C$ with the initial condition**: yields a family of curves, not the specific solution.

</Porta>

## Solved examples

<Exemplo titulo="General solution of dy/dx = ky (application)" numero="1">

**Problem.** Solve $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = 3y$.

**Strategy.** The ODE is separable with $g(x) = 3$ and $h(y) = y$. Separate, integrate, exponentiate.

**Resolution.**

$$\frac{dy}{y} = 3\,dx$$

$$\int \frac{dy}{y} = \int 3\,dx + C \;\Rightarrow\; \ln|y| = 3x + C$$

$$|y| = e^{3x+C} = e^C\,e^{3x}.$$

Writing $A = \pm e^C$ (arbitrary non-zero real constant):

$$y(x) = A e^{3x}, \quad A \in \mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}.$$

Check: $y = 0$ also solves it (constant solution, since $h(0)=0$). Include $A = 0$.

**Verification.** $y' = 3A e^{3x} = 3y$ — checks out.

**Source.** [Lebl, Notes on Diffy Qs, §1.3, ex. 1.3.1](https://www.jirka.org/diffyqs/html/separable_section.html) — CC-BY-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="IVP with initial condition — exponential decay (application)" numero="2">

**Problem.** Solve the IVP $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = -\dfrac{y}{2}$, $y(0) = 4$.

**Strategy.** Separate variables, integrate, apply initial condition to fix $C$.

**Resolution.**

$$\frac{dy}{y} = -\frac{1}{2}\,dx \;\Rightarrow\; \ln|y| = -\frac{x}{2} + C \;\Rightarrow\; y = A\,e^{-x/2}.$$

Initial condition: $4 = A\,e^0 = A$, so $A = 4$.

$$y(x) = 4\,e^{-x/2}.$$

**Verification.** $y' = -2\,e^{-x/2} = -y/2$ and $y(0) = 4$ — correct.

**Source.** [OpenStax Calculus Vol. 2, §4.3, Example 4.15](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-2/pages/4-3-separable-equations) — CC-BY-NC-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="ODE with partial fractions — logistic growth (intermediate)" numero="3">

**Problem.** Solve $\dfrac{dP}{dt} = P(1 - P)$, $P(0) = 0.1$.

**Strategy.** Separate and integrate using partial fractions on $\dfrac{1}{P(1-P)}$.

**Resolution.**

$$\frac{dP}{P(1-P)} = dt.$$

Partial fractions: $\dfrac{1}{P(1-P)} = \dfrac{1}{P} + \dfrac{1}{1-P}$.

$$\int\!\left(\frac{1}{P} + \frac{1}{1-P}\right)dP = \int dt + C$$

$$\ln P - \ln(1-P) = t + C \;\Rightarrow\; \ln\frac{P}{1-P} = t + C.$$

Exponentiating: $\dfrac{P}{1-P} = e^{t+C} = A e^t$.

Solving: $P = \dfrac{A e^t}{1 + A e^t} = \dfrac{1}{1 + B e^{-t}}$.

Initial condition: $P(0) = 1/(1+B) = 0.1 \Rightarrow B = 9$.

$$P(t) = \frac{1}{1 + 9e^{-t}}.$$

**Verification.** $P(0) = 1/10 = 0.1$ and $P \to 1$ as $t \to +\infty$ — correct logistic behavior.

**Source.** [Lebl, Notes on Diffy Qs, §1.3, ex. 1.3.4](https://www.jirka.org/diffyqs/html/separable_section.html) — CC-BY-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Newton's cooling — real data (intermediate/modeling)" numero="4">

**Problem.** An object at 90°C is placed in a 20°C room. After 5 min it is at 70°C. Model and determine when it reaches 30°C.

**Strategy.** Newton's law of cooling: $\dot T = -k(T - T_a)$. Separate and integrate in terms of $u = T - 20$.

**Resolution.**

Let $u = T - 20$. Then $\dot u = \dot T = -ku$, so $u(t) = u_0 e^{-kt}$.

With $u_0 = 90 - 20 = 70$: $u(t) = 70\,e^{-kt}$.

Condition: $u(5) = 50$:

$$70 e^{-5k} = 50 \;\Rightarrow\; k = \frac{1}{5}\ln\!\frac{70}{50} = \frac{\ln(7/5)}{5} \approx 0.0673\,\mathrm{min}^{-1}.$$

For $T = 30 \Rightarrow u = 10$:

$$70\,e^{-kt} = 10 \;\Rightarrow\; t = \frac{\ln 7}{k} = \frac{5\ln 7}{\ln(7/5)} \approx 29.1\,\mathrm{min}.$$

**Verification.** $T(5) = 20 + 70\,e^{-5 \times 0.0673} \approx 70°$C — confirms.

**Source.** [OpenStax Calculus Vol. 2, §4.3, Example 4.17](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-2/pages/4-3-separable-equations) — CC-BY-NC-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Finite-time blow-up — y-dot = y squared (advanced)" numero="5">

**Problem.** Solve $\dfrac{dy}{dt} = y^2$, $y(0) = 1$. Show that the solution blows up in finite time and determine the blow-up instant.

**Strategy.** Separate, integrate (power $-2$), isolate $y$, identify the singularity.

**Resolution.**

$$\frac{dy}{y^2} = dt \;\Rightarrow\; \int y^{-2}\,dy = \int dt + C \;\Rightarrow\; -\frac{1}{y} = t + C.$$

So $y(t) = \dfrac{-1}{t + C}$.

Initial condition: $y(0) = -1/C = 1 \Rightarrow C = -1$.

$$y(t) = \frac{1}{1-t}.$$

The solution is defined on $(-\infty, 1)$ and satisfies $y(t) \to +\infty$ as $t \to 1^-$: **blow-up at $t = 1$**.

**Verification.** $y' = 1/(1-t)^2 = y^2$ and $y(0) = 1$ — correct. Osgood's criterion confirms: $\int_1^\infty dy/y^2 = 1 < \infty$.

**Source.** [Lebl, Notes on Diffy Qs, §1.3, ex. 1.3.6](https://www.jirka.org/diffyqs/html/separable_section.html) — CC-BY-SA.

</Exemplo>
