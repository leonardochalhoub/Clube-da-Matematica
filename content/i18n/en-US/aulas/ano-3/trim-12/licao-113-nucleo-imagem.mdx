### Kernel and image

<Definicao titulo="Kernel and image of a linear transformation">

Let $T: V \to W$ be linear.

- **Kernel**: $\ker T = \{v \in V : T(v) = 0_W\}$ — a subspace of $V$.
- **Image** (range): $\operatorname{Im}(T) = \{T(v) : v \in V\} = T(V)$ — a subspace of $W$.
- **Nullity**: $\operatorname{nul}(T) = \dim \ker T$.
- **Rank**: $\operatorname{rank}(T) = \dim \operatorname{Im}(T)$.

</Definicao>

> "The null space of $T$, denoted $\mathcal{N}(T)$, is the set $\mathcal{N}(T) = \{v \in V : T(v) = 0\}$." — [Beezer, *A First Course in Linear Algebra*, §KER](http://linear.pugetsound.edu/html/section-KER.html)

> "The range of $T$, denoted $\mathcal{R}(T)$, is the set $\mathcal{R}(T) = \{T(v) : v \in V\}$." — [Beezer, *A First Course in Linear Algebra*, §RNG](http://linear.pugetsound.edu/html/section-RNG.html)

### Characterizations of injectivity and surjectivity

<Definicao titulo="Injective and surjective via kernel and image">

- $T$ **injective** $\iff \ker T = \{0\} \iff \operatorname{nul}(T) = 0$.
- $T$ **surjective** $\iff \operatorname{Im}(T) = W \iff \operatorname{rank}(T) = \dim W$.
- $T$ **bijective** (equal finite dimensions) $\iff$ injective $\iff$ surjective.

</Definicao>

<Insight titulo="General solution of Ax = b">

$Ax = b$ has a solution $\iff b \in \operatorname{Im}(A)$. When it exists, the general solution is $x = x_p + h$, with $x_p$ any particular solution and $h \in \ker A$ arbitrary. Geometrically: the solution is not a point, but an **affine subspace** parallel to the kernel.

</Insight>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

Imagine a **mixing machine**. You toss in colored balls and it mixes them and spits out balls.

Two interesting places:

1. **Which balls disappear** — the machine eats them and spits "nothing" (zero). This group is the **kernel**.
2. **Which balls the machine can spit out** — this group is the **image**.

The big magic rule: **how much disappears + how much comes out = the total**. If you had 5 types of ball and 2 types disappeared into the kernel, only 3 types can exit through the image.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

Every linear transformation $T$ has **two questions**:

1. **Who gets mapped to zero?** — that set is the **kernel**.
2. **Where can the transformation reach?** — that set is the **image**.

Example: the projection $T(x, y) = (x, 0)$ "flattens" the $y$-axis to zero. So $\ker T =$ the $y$-axis (all $(0, y)$). And $T$ only hits the $x$-axis, so $\operatorname{Im}(T) =$ the $x$-axis.

The big rule: **dimension of kernel + dimension of image = dimension of where you started**. If you started from the plane (dim 2) and the kernel has dim 1, the image has dim 1. Half was lost, half got through.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old teen">

The rank-nullity theorem is **the fundamental equation of applied linear algebra**. Every analysis of a linear system goes through it.

### Why is it fundamental?

When you solve $Ax = b$ ($A$ is $m \times n$):

- **Existence** depends on $b \in \operatorname{Im}(A)$ — a question in $m$ dimensions.
- **Uniqueness** depends on $\ker A = \{0\}$ — a question in $n$ dimensions.

The relationship between the two:

| Case | $\ker A$ | $\operatorname{Im}(A)$ | System $Ax = b$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| $A$ square invertible | $\{0\}$ | all of $\mathbb{R}^n$ | unique solution always |
| more equations than unknowns ($m > n$) | possibly $\{0\}$ | proper subspace | usually no solution |
| more unknowns than equations ($n > m$) | always nontrivial | possibly all of $\mathbb{R}^m$ | usually infinitely many solutions |

### How to compute in practice

1. Row-reduce $A$ to row-echelon form (Gaussian elimination).
2. Pivots = rank.
3. Free variables = nullity.
4. Image = span generated by pivot columns of $A$ (**original** $A$, not row-reduced).
5. Kernel = solutions of $Ax = 0$ via parameters in free variables.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

Rank and nullity answer central engineering questions: **does the system have a solution? Is it unique? How sensitive is it?**

### Strang's 4 subspaces

For $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ with rank $r$:

| Space | Definition | In | Dimension |
|---|---|---|---|
| Column space $\operatorname{Col}(A)$ | $\operatorname{span}$ of columns | $\mathbb{R}^m$ | $r$ |
| Row space $\operatorname{Row}(A)$ | $\operatorname{span}$ of rows | $\mathbb{R}^n$ | $r$ |
| Kernel $\ker(A)$ | $\{x : Ax = 0\}$ | $\mathbb{R}^n$ | $n - r$ |
| Left kernel $\ker(A^T)$ | $\{y : A^T y = 0\}$ | $\mathbb{R}^m$ | $m - r$ |

And $\operatorname{Row}(A) \perp \ker(A)$, $\operatorname{Col}(A) \perp \ker(A^T)$.

### Least squares

$Ax = b$ has no exact solution? Project $b$ onto $\operatorname{Col}(A)$ and solve $A^T A x = A^T b$ (normal equations). Solution always exists; it is unique if $\ker(A) = \{0\}$.

If $\ker(A) \neq \{0\}$: family of solutions parameterized by $\ker(A)$. In ML, this is regularization: Ridge $A^T A + \lambda I$ makes $\ker = \{0\}$.

### In control theory

- **Controllability**: rank of $[B, AB, A^2 B, \ldots, A^{n-1}B] = n$. Trivial kernel = controllable system.
- **Observability**: rank of $[C^T, A^T C^T, \ldots]^T = n$.

### In ML

- **Multicollinearity**: $\operatorname{rank}(X) < n$ when columns of $X$ are dependent. $X^T X$ singular — cannot invert directly. Solution: Ridge, Lasso, PCA before regression.
- **Underdetermined regression**: $n > m$ — infinitely many solutions, optimal choice via pseudoinverse (SVD, Lesson 117).

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

### Exact sequences and homological algebra

Rank-nullity is a special case of homological algebra. The sequence:

$$0 \to \ker T \xrightarrow{\iota} V \xrightarrow{T} \operatorname{Im}(T) \to 0$$

is **exact** (image of each map = kernel of the next). In finite dimension, the functor $\dim$ is Euler-additive over short exact sequences, hence $\dim V = \dim \ker T + \dim \operatorname{Im}(T)$.

Generalizes to **chain complexes**: $\cdots \to V_{n+1} \to V_n \to V_{n-1} \to \cdots$ — foundation of algebraic topology (singular homology, de Rham cohomology) and modern homological algebra.

### Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse

For any $A$ ($m \times n$, rank $r$):

$$A^+ = V \Sigma^+ U^T$$

where $\Sigma^+$ inverts nonzero singulars and zeros the rest. Properties:

- $A^+ A$ = orthogonal projector onto $\operatorname{Row}(A)$.
- $A A^+$ = orthogonal projector onto $\operatorname{Col}(A)$.
- $x^* = A^+ b$ is the minimum-norm solution of $\min \|Ax - b\|$.

In production: use SVD directly, do not compute $A^+$ explicitly.

### Numerical rank vs algebraic rank

Matrices with small (but nonzero) singulars have **numerical rank** $r_\varepsilon =$ number of singulars $> \varepsilon$. Critical in rank-revealing QR (RRQR) and truncated SVD. Control via condition number $\kappa(A) = \sigma_1 / \sigma_r$.

### Advanced connections

- **Persistent homology**: rank of simplicial complexes — TDA (Topological Data Analysis).
- **Tensor ranks**: extensions of rank to higher-order tensors (applications in ML: Tucker, tensor train).
- **Error-correcting codes**: $\ker H$ is the linear code; minimum distance $\leftrightarrow$ rank of parity-check matrix $H$.
- **Mixed quantum states**: density matrices with rank $r < n$ represent mixed states; rank = dimension of support.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practical application">

### Computation roadmap — by hand

**To find the kernel of $A$ ($m \times n$):**

1. Set up the augmented system $[A \mid 0]$.
2. Row-reduce to reduced row-echelon form (RREF).
3. Identify pivot variables and free variables.
4. Express the pivot variables in terms of free variables.
5. For each free variable: assign 1 to it and 0 to others; form a vector. These vectors span $\ker A$.

**To find the image of $A$:**

1. Row-reduce $A$.
2. The columns of **original** $A$ that correspond to pivot columns form a basis for $\operatorname{Im}(A)$.

### In Python

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.linalg import null_space

A = np.array([[1, 2, 3],
              [2, 4, 6],
              [3, 6, 9]], dtype=float)

r = np.linalg.matrix_rank(A)
print("Rank:", r)          # 1

# Basis of kernel (scipy normalizes)
N = null_space(A)
print("Kernel basis (columns):\n", N)

# Image basis: columns of U corresponding to nonzero singulars
U, S, Vt = np.linalg.svd(A)
Im_base = U[:, :r]
print("Image basis (columns):\n", Im_base)
```

### Quick checks

- $\dim \ker A + \dim \operatorname{Im}(A)$ should give the number of columns of $A$.
- $Av = 0$ for every $v$ in kernel basis (check numerically).
- $Au_i =$ column of $\operatorname{Im}(A)$ for the right standard vectors.

### When reduced rank is a red flag

In ML with 50 features and 5 samples: $X$ is $5 \times 50$, so $\operatorname{rank}(X) \leq 5$. Matrix $X^T X$ ($50 \times 50$) has rank $\leq 5$ — singular, not directly invertible. Solution: Ridge, Lasso, PCA before regression.

## Worked examples

<Exemplo titulo="Kernel and image of a projection (basic)" numero="1">

**Problem.** Let $T: \mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}^3$ be the projection $T(x, y, z) = (x, y, 0)$. Find $\ker T$, $\operatorname{Im}(T)$, rank, and nullity. Verify the rank-nullity theorem.

**Strategy.** Solve $T(x,y,z) = (0,0,0)$ for the kernel. Describe $\{T(v) : v \in \mathbb{R}^3\}$ for the image. Check dimensions.

**Solution.**

$T(x,y,z) = (0,0,0) \iff (x,y,0) = (0,0,0) \iff x = 0$ and $y = 0$. Thus $\ker T = \{(0, 0, z) : z \in \mathbb{R}\}$ — the $z$-axis.

$\operatorname{Im}(T) = \{(x, y, 0) : x, y \in \mathbb{R}\}$ — the $xy$-plane.

$\dim \ker T = 1$; $\dim \operatorname{Im}(T) = 2$.

Rank-nullity check: $1 + 2 = 3 = \dim \mathbb{R}^3$. ✓

**Verification.** $T$ is not injective ($\ker T \neq \{0\}$). $T$ is not surjective ($\operatorname{Im}(T) \neq \mathbb{R}^3$). Geometrically: $T$ "flattens" the $z$-direction.

**Source.** [Beezer, *A First Course in Linear Algebra*, §ILT, Example ILT.STLT](http://linear.pugetsound.edu/html/section-ILT.html) — GNU FDL.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Kernel and image via row reduction (intermediate)" numero="2">

**Problem.** For <Eq>{`A = \\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\\\ 2 & 4 & 6 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>, find $\ker A$, $\operatorname{Im}(A)$, rank, and nullity.

**Strategy.** Row-reduce $[A \mid 0]$ for the kernel. Use pivot columns of original $A$ for the image.

**Solution.**

Row-reducing:

<Eq>{`\\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\\\ 2 & 4 & 6 \\end{pmatrix} \\xrightarrow{R_2 \\leftarrow R_2 - 2R_1} \\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\\\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>

Pivot in column 1. Free variables: $x_2$ and $x_3$.

$x_1 = -2x_2 - 3x_3$. Letting $(x_2, x_3) = (1,0)$: $(-2, 1, 0)$. Letting $(x_2, x_3) = (0,1)$: $(-3, 0, 1)$.

$$\ker A = \operatorname{span}\{(-2, 1, 0), (-3, 0, 1)\}, \quad \dim = 2$$

Pivot column is the 1st column of original $A$: $\operatorname{Im}(A) = \operatorname{span}\{(1, 2)\}$, $\dim = 1$.

Rank-nullity: $2 + 1 = 3$. ✓

**Verification.** $A \cdot (-2, 1, 0)^T = (-2 + 2, -4 + 4)^T = (0,0)^T$. ✓

**Source.** [Beezer, *A First Course in Linear Algebra*, §RNNM, Example RNNM.NSEEF](http://linear.pugetsound.edu/html/section-RNNM.html) — GNU FDL.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Injectivity, surjectivity, and rank-nullity (intermediate)" numero="3">

**Problem.** Let $T: \mathbb{R}^4 \to \mathbb{R}^3$ with matrix <Eq>{`A = \\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & -1 & 2 \\\\ 0 & 1 & 2 & -1 \\\\ 1 & 1 & 1 & 1 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>. Compute rank and nullity. Is $T$ injective? Surjective?

**Strategy.** Row-reduce $A$. Count pivots = rank. Nullity = $4 - \operatorname{rank}$.

**Solution.**

<Eq>{`A \\xrightarrow{R_3 \\leftarrow R_3 - R_1 - R_2} \\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & -1 & 2 \\\\ 0 & 1 & 2 & -1 \\\\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>

Two pivots: $\operatorname{rank}(A) = 2$. Nullity $= 4 - 2 = 2$.

$T$ **is not injective** (nullity $> 0$; $\ker T$ has dim 2).

$T$ **is not surjective** (rank $= 2 < 3 = \dim \mathbb{R}^3$; $\operatorname{Im}(T)$ is a plane in $\mathbb{R}^3$).

**Verification.** $2 + 2 = 4 = \dim \mathbb{R}^4$. ✓

**Source.** [Hefferon, *Linear Algebra*, ch. 3, §II.2, Example 2.16](https://hefferon.net/linearalgebra/) — CC-BY-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Differentiation as a linear transformation (advanced)" numero="4">

**Problem.** Let $D: \mathcal{P}_3 \to \mathcal{P}_3$ be the differentiation operator ($D(p) = p'$). Find $\ker D$, $\operatorname{Im}(D)$, rank, and nullity. Verify rank-nullity.

**Strategy.** Use the basis $\{1, t, t^2, t^3\}$ of $\mathcal{P}_3$. Apply $D$ to each basis element.

**Solution.**

$D(1) = 0$, $D(t) = 1$, $D(t^2) = 2t$, $D(t^3) = 3t^2$.

$\ker D = \{p \in \mathcal{P}_3 : p' = 0\} = \{p : p \text{ is constant}\} = \operatorname{span}\{1\}$.

$\operatorname{Im}(D) = \operatorname{span}\{1, 2t, 3t^2\} = \mathcal{P}_2$.

$\dim \ker D = 1$, $\dim \operatorname{Im}(D) = 3$.

Rank-nullity: $1 + 3 = 4 = \dim \mathcal{P}_3$. ✓

**Verification.** $D$ is not injective (constants vanish). $D$ is not surjective (no degree-3 polynomial in image — $\dim \operatorname{Im} = 3 < 4$).

**Source.** [Beezer, *A First Course in Linear Algebra*, §LT, Example LT.MFLT](http://linear.pugetsound.edu/html/section-LT.html) — GNU FDL.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Proof: injective iff kernel is trivial (proof)" numero="5">

**Problem.** Prove: $T: V \to W$ linear is injective if and only if $\ker T = \{0\}$.

**Strategy.** Prove both directions. Use the definition of injectivity ($T(u) = T(v) \Rightarrow u = v$) and linearity of $T$.

**Solution.**

($\Rightarrow$) Suppose $T$ is injective. If $v \in \ker T$, then $T(v) = 0 = T(0)$. By injectivity, $v = 0$. Thus $\ker T = \{0\}$.

($\Leftarrow$) Suppose $\ker T = \{0\}$. Let $u, v \in V$ with $T(u) = T(v)$. Then $T(u) - T(v) = 0$, and by linearity $T(u - v) = 0$. Thus $u - v \in \ker T = \{0\}$, so $u - v = 0$, i.e., $u = v$. Therefore $T$ is injective.

**Verification.** The proof is complete (both directions). The argument uses only linearity of $T$ and the definition of kernel — no extra hypotheses.

**Source.** [Beezer, *A First Course in Linear Algebra*, §ILT, Theorem KILT](http://linear.pugetsound.edu/html/section-ILT.html) — GNU FDL.

</Exemplo>
