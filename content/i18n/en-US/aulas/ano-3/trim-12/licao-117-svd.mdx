### The SVD Theorem

<Definicao titulo="Singular Value Decomposition (SVD)">

For every <Eq>{`A \\in \\mathbb{R}^{m \\times n}`}</Eq>, there exist:

- <Eq>{`U \\in \\mathbb{R}^{m \\times m}`}</Eq> orthogonal (columns = **left singular vectors**),
- <Eq>{`V \\in \\mathbb{R}^{n \\times n}`}</Eq> orthogonal (columns = **right singular vectors**),
- <Eq>{`\\Sigma \\in \\mathbb{R}^{m \\times n}`}</Eq> with diagonal entries <Eq>{`\\sigma_1 \\geq \\sigma_2 \\geq \\cdots \\geq \\sigma_r > 0 = \\sigma_{r+1} = \\cdots`}</Eq>,

such that <Eq>{`A = U \\Sigma V^T`}</Eq>. The integer <Eq>{`r = \\text{rank}(A)`}</Eq>.

</Definicao>

> "Any matrix A with rank r can be written as a product $A = U \Sigma V^T$ where U is orthogonal ($m \times m$), $\Sigma$ is diagonal ($m \times n$, nonnegative entries decreasing), and V is orthogonal ($n \times n$). The diagonal entries $\sigma_1 \geq \cdots \geq \sigma_r > 0$ are the singular values of A." — [Understanding Linear Algebra, §6.3](https://understandinglinearalgebra.org/chap6.html)

<Definicao titulo="Compact SVD and rank-1 expansion">

The **compact (economic) SVD** retains only the <Eq>{`r`}</Eq> non-trivial columns:

<Equation explicacao="Expansion: A is a sum of r rank-1 matrices, weighted by singular values." latex="A = U_r \Sigma_r V_r^T = \sum_{i=1}^r \sigma_i\, u_i v_i^T" />

Each term <Eq>{`\\sigma_i u_i v_i^T`}</Eq> is a **rank-1 matrix** — outer product of a column vector and a row vector.

</Definicao>

> "Theorem (Existence of SVD). Every real matrix A has a singular value decomposition. The singular values are the positive square roots of the nonzero eigenvalues of $A^T A$." — [A First Course in Linear Algebra, §SVD](http://linear.ups.edu/html/section-SVD.html)

### Connection to eigenvalues

<Definicao titulo="SVD and the matrices A^T A and A A^T">

- <Eq>{`A^T A = V (\\Sigma^T \\Sigma) V^T`}</Eq>: the **eigenvalues** of <Eq>{`A^T A`}</Eq> are <Eq>{`\\sigma_i^2`}</Eq>, eigenvectors = columns of <Eq>{`V`}</Eq>.
- <Eq>{`A A^T = U (\\Sigma \\Sigma^T) U^T`}</Eq>: the **eigenvalues** of <Eq>{`A A^T`}</Eq> are the same <Eq>{`\\sigma_i^2`}</Eq>, eigenvectors = columns of <Eq>{`U`}</Eq>.
- Singular values are **always real and non-negative**, even if <Eq>{`A`}</Eq> has complex eigenvalues.

</Definicao>

### Eckart-Young Theorem

<Teorema titulo="Best rank-k approximation (Eckart-Young, 1936)">

The best rank-<Eq>{`k`}</Eq> approximation to <Eq>{`A`}</Eq> (in Frobenius norm and spectral norm) is:

<Equation explicacao="Truncating the SVD at the k largest singular values gives the best rank-k approximation in the least-squares sense." latex="A_k = \sum_{i=1}^k \sigma_i\, u_i v_i^T" />

Errors: <Eq>{`\\|A - A_k\\|_F^2 = \\sigma_{k+1}^2 + \\cdots + \\sigma_r^2`}</Eq>, <Eq>{`\\|A - A_k\\|_2 = \\sigma_{k+1}`}</Eq>.

</Teorema>

### Moore-Penrose Pseudoinverse

<Definicao titulo="Pseudoinverse via SVD">

<Equation explicacao="Inverts the positive singular values in place of Sigma; zeros the null ones. Solves min ||Ax - b|| with minimum-norm solution." latex="A^+ = V \Sigma^+ U^T" />

where <Eq>{`\\Sigma^+`}</Eq> is obtained by replacing each <Eq>{`\\sigma_i > 0`}</Eq> with <Eq>{`1/\\sigma_i`}</Eq> and transposing. The solution <Eq>{`x^* = A^+ b`}</Eq> minimizes <Eq>{`\\|Ax - b\\|`}</Eq> with smallest norm <Eq>{`\\|x\\|`}</Eq> among all minimizers.

</Definicao>

<Insight>

The **condition number** of <Eq>{`A`}</Eq> is <Eq>{`\\kappa_2(A) = \\sigma_1 / \\sigma_r`}</Eq>. A system <Eq>{`Ax = b`}</Eq> with large <Eq>{`\\kappa_2`}</Eq> is ill-conditioned: small errors in <Eq>{`b`}</Eq> produce large errors in <Eq>{`x`}</Eq>. SVD is the standard diagnostic tool for ill-conditioning.

</Insight>

Imagine you have a **colored photo** of a dog. The photo is full of colored dots — there are so many! SVD is a **magic trick for organizing**: it separates the photo into **layers of importance**.

The first layer captures the most important parts — the outline of the dog. The second captures slightly smaller details. And so on.

If you combine just the first most important layers, you can still recognize the dog — but using **much less memory**. That's how computers store smaller photos without losing too much quality.

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

Every matrix can be taken apart into three pieces: <Eq>{`A = U \\Sigma V^T`}</Eq>.

- <Eq>{`V^T`}</Eq> is a **rotation** of the input space.
- <Eq>{`\\Sigma`}</Eq> is a **stretching** — stretches or shrinks each direction by a number <Eq>{`\\sigma_i \\geq 0`}</Eq>.
- <Eq>{`U`}</Eq> is another **rotation** in the output space.

These numbers <Eq>{`\\sigma_i`}</Eq> — the **singular values** — tell you the importance of each direction. The largest <Eq>{`\\sigma_1`}</Eq> is the most important direction; the smallest is the least important.

**Compression trick**: in a photo of <Eq>{`1000 \\times 1000`}</Eq> pixels, keeping just the 50 largest singular values, you store <Eq>{`50 \\times 1000 + 50 + 50 \\times 1000 = 100050`}</Eq> numbers instead of 1 million — **10 times less**. The image still looks good.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old teen">

SVD is the most important decomposition in applied linear algebra. It combines three properties that no other decomposition has at the same time: **generality** (works for any matrix), **orthogonality** (the bases <Eq>{`U, V`}</Eq> are orthonormal) and **uniqueness** (singular values are unique).

### Geometry

Every linear map <Eq>{`A: \\mathbb{R}^n \\to \\mathbb{R}^m`}</Eq> sends the unit sphere to an **ellipsoid**. The semi-axes of the ellipsoid have lengths <Eq>{`\\sigma_1 \\geq \\cdots \\geq \\sigma_r`}</Eq> and point in directions <Eq>{`u_1, \\ldots, u_r`}</Eq>.

- <Eq>{`V^T`}</Eq> rotates the input space (aligns the vectors <Eq>{`v_i`}</Eq> with the canonical axes).
- <Eq>{`\\Sigma`}</Eq> stretches each axis by <Eq>{`\\sigma_i`}</Eq>.
- <Eq>{`U`}</Eq> rotates the result into the output space.

### Comparison with diagonalization

| Aspect | Diagonalization | SVD |
|---|---|---|
| Applies to | Square diagonalizable matrices | Any matrix |
| Existence | Can fail | Always exists |
| Bases | <Eq>{`P, P^{-1}`}</Eq> (not orthogonal) | <Eq>{`U, V`}</Eq> orthogonal |
| Values | Eigenvalues (<Eq>{`\\mathbb{C}`}</Eq> in general) | Singular (<Eq>{`\\geq 0`}</Eq> real) |

### Immediate application: image compression

A grayscale image is a matrix <Eq>{`M \\in \\mathbb{R}^{m \\times n}`}</Eq>. Truncating the SVD at <Eq>{`k`}</Eq> terms:

- Storing <Eq>{`A_k`}</Eq> requires <Eq>{`k(m + n + 1)`}</Eq> numbers instead of <Eq>{`mn`}</Eq>.
- Relative Frobenius error: <Eq>{`\\|A - A_k\\|_F / \\|A\\|_F = \\sqrt{\\sum_{i>k} \\sigma_i^2} / \\sqrt{\\sum_i \\sigma_i^2}`}</Eq>.

### Netflix Prize case (2009)

Netflix had a ratings matrix: <Eq>{`\\approx 10^7`}</Eq> users <Eq>{`\\times 10^5`}</Eq> movies, sparse. Low-rank approximation (SVD variant) predicted missing ratings. The winning team BellKor's Pragmatic Chaos improved accuracy by 10% and won US$1 million.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

SVD is the Swiss Army knife of computational linear algebra. It solves ill-conditioned systems, discovers latent structure, compresses data with guaranteed optimality.

### Computation algorithm

**For small <Eq>{`A`}</Eq>:**

1. Compute <Eq>{`A^T A`}</Eq> (symmetric PSD).
2. Decompose <Eq>{`A^T A = V \\Lambda V^T`}</Eq> (eigenvectors).
3. <Eq>{`\\sigma_i = \\sqrt{\\lambda_i}`}</Eq>; <Eq>{`u_i = A v_i / \\sigma_i`}</Eq> for <Eq>{`\\sigma_i > 0`}</Eq>.

**For large <Eq>{`A`:</Eq> Golub-Reinsch algorithm, <Eq>{`O(\\min(mn^2, m^2n))`}</Eq>. For top-<Eq>{`k`}</Eq>: Randomized SVD (Halko-Martinsson-Tropp), <Eq>{`O(mnk)`}</Eq> expected.

### Applications by domain

**Computer vision**: Eigenfaces (Turk-Pentland 1991) — SVD of centered face matrix → bases for facial recognition.

**ML**: Word embeddings GloVe — SVD of word co-occurrence matrix; Whitening — <Eq>{`W = V \\Sigma^{-1}`}</Eq> decorrelates features.

**Finance**: PCA of yield curve — 3 principal factors (level, slope, curvature) capture ~99% of variance (Litterman-Scheinkman 1991).

**Numerical**: Numerical rank <Eq>{`r_\\epsilon = \\#\\{\\sigma_i > \\epsilon \\cdot \\sigma_1\\}`}</Eq>; robust least-squares via pseudoinverse.

### Python code

```python
import numpy as np

A = np.random.randn(200, 50)
U, s, Vt = np.linalg.svd(A, full_matrices=False)

# Rank-k approximation
k = 10
A_k = U[:, :k] @ np.diag(s[:k]) @ Vt[:k, :]
print("Relative Frobenius error:", np.linalg.norm(A - A_k, 'fro') / np.linalg.norm(A, 'fro'))

# Pseudoinverse and least squares
b = np.random.randn(200)
x_pinv = np.linalg.lstsq(A, b, rcond=None)[0]  # uses SVD internally
```

### Tikhonov regularization via SVD

Instead of inverting <Eq>{`\\sigma_i`}</Eq> directly (unstable when <Eq>{`\\sigma_i`}</Eq> is small), use:

<Equation explicacao="Tikhonov: shrinks small singular values, avoids amplifying noise." latex="x_\lambda = \sum_{i=1}^r \frac{\sigma_i}{\sigma_i^2 + \lambda}\, (u_i^T b)\, v_i" />

For <Eq>{`\\lambda \\to 0`}</Eq> recovers pseudoinverse; for large <Eq>{`\\lambda`}</Eq> shrinks the solution.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

SVD is not just an algorithm — it is the universal canonical form of a linear transformation. Every matrix has an SVD; not every matrix has a real eigendecomposition.

### Historical context

Eugenio Beltrami (1873) and Camille Jordan (1874) proved SVD independently for square matrices. Erhard Schmidt (1907) generalized it to integral operators (Hilbert-Schmidt kernels). The modern form for rectangular matrices is due to Carl Eckart and Gale Young (1936), who also proved the best approximation theorem.

### Connection to functional analysis

For compact operators <Eq>{`T: H_1 \\to H_2`}</Eq> on Hilbert spaces, the Schmidt decomposition is:

<Equation explicacao="Infinite-dimensional generalization of SVD: compact operators decompose into countable sums of rank-1 operators." latex="T = \sum_{i=1}^\infty \sigma_i\, \langle \cdot, v_i \rangle\, u_i" />

The **singular values** of <Eq>{`T`}</Eq> form a sequence in <Eq>{`\\ell^2`}</Eq>. This is the structure behind signal compression, principal component analysis and spectral kernel methods.

### Randomized SVD (Halko-Martinsson-Tropp 2011)

For matrices with low numerical rank, exact SVD is unnecessary. The algorithm:

1. Random column sampling: <Eq>{`Y = A \\Omega`}</Eq> with <Eq>{`\\Omega \\in \\mathbb{R}^{n \\times (k+p)}`}</Eq> Gaussian.
2. Orthogonalize: <Eq>{`Q, R = \\text{QR}(Y)`}</Eq>.
3. Factor the projection: SVD of <Eq>{`Q^T A`}</Eq> (small — <Eq>{`(k+p) \\times n`}</Eq>).

With oversampling <Eq>{`p \\approx 10`}</Eq>, expected error is near the Eckart-Young optimum. Enables SVD of <Eq>{`10^6 \\times 10^6`}</Eq> matrices on limited memory.

### SVD and modern machine learning

Transformers (Vaswani et al. 2017) decompose attention as a product of three matrices: <Eq>{`Q, K, V`}</Eq>. The geometry of multi-head attention is essentially a learned SVD via gradient descent — the "heads" learn principal subspaces of the training data.

Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA, 2021) — LLM fine-tuning — perturbs pre-trained weights <Eq>{`W`}</Eq> with <Eq>{`\\Delta W = BA`}</Eq> of low rank, exactly what SVD justifies as signal structure in noise.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practical application">

### Image compression step by step

Given a grayscale image with matrix <Eq>{`A \\in \\mathbb{R}^{512 \\times 512}`}</Eq>:

1. Compute full SVD: <Eq>{`A = U \\Sigma V^T`}</Eq>, with <Eq>{`\\sigma_1 \\geq \\cdots \\geq \\sigma_{512} \\geq 0`}</Eq>.
2. Choose <Eq>{`k`}</Eq>: plot <Eq>{`\\sigma_i`}</Eq> vs <Eq>{`i`}</Eq> (scree plot); cut at the "elbow" or where cumulative variance explained <Eq>{`\\geq 95\\%`}</Eq>.
3. Store: <Eq>{`U_{[:, :k]}`}</Eq>, <Eq>{`\\sigma_{1:k}`}</Eq>, <Eq>{`V_{[:, :k]}`}</Eq> — total <Eq>{`k(512 + 1 + 512)`}</Eq> floats.
4. Reconstruct: <Eq>{`A_k = U_k \\, \\text{diag}(\\sigma_{1:k}) \\, V_k^T`}</Eq>.

With <Eq>{`k = 50`}</Eq>: compression ratio <Eq>{`512^2 / (50 \\times 1025) \\approx 5.1\\times`}</Eq>. Visually indistinguishable from original.

### Diagnosis of ill-conditioned regression

In a linear regression <Eq>{`y = X \\beta + \\varepsilon`}</Eq> with ill-conditioned <Eq>{`X`}</Eq>:

1. SVD of <Eq>{`X = U \\Sigma V^T`}</Eq>.
2. <Eq>{`\\kappa_2(X) = \\sigma_1 / \\sigma_r`}</Eq>. If <Eq>{`> 30`}</Eq>, there is multicollinearity.
3. Apply ridge: <Eq>{`\\hat\\beta_\\lambda = V (\\Sigma^T \\Sigma + \\lambda I)^{-1} \\Sigma^T U^T y`}</Eq>.
4. Choose <Eq>{`\\lambda`}</Eq> via cross-validation.

### Practical exercise: ENEM by state

The ENEM microdata has ~200 variables per test-taker. SVD of the state × variable matrix (26 states × 200 items) identifies the 3-5 "inequality axes" that structure regional performance — analysis published by INEP researchers each year.

## Worked examples

<Exemplo titulo="SVD of a simple 2×2 matrix" numero="1">

**Problem.** Compute the SVD of <Eq>{`A = \\begin{pmatrix} 3 & 0 \\\\ 4 & 5 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>.

**Strategy.** Compute <Eq>{`A^T A`}</Eq>, decompose into eigenvectors/eigenvalues, obtain <Eq>{`V`}</Eq> and <Eq>{`\\Sigma`}</Eq>, then <Eq>{`U`}</Eq>.

**Solution.**

<Eq>{`A^T A = \\begin{pmatrix} 3 & 4 \\\\ 0 & 5 \\end{pmatrix}\\begin{pmatrix} 3 & 0 \\\\ 4 & 5 \\end{pmatrix} = \\begin{pmatrix} 25 & 20 \\\\ 20 & 25 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>

Eigenvalues: <Eq>{`\\det(A^T A - \\lambda I) = (25 - \\lambda)^2 - 400 = 0`}</Eq>, so <Eq>{`\\lambda_1 = 45`}</Eq> and <Eq>{`\\lambda_2 = 5`}</Eq>.

Singular values: <Eq>{`\\sigma_1 = \\sqrt{45} = 3\\sqrt{5}`}</Eq>, <Eq>{`\\sigma_2 = \\sqrt{5}`}</Eq>.

Eigenvectors of <Eq>{`A^T A`}</Eq>: for <Eq>{`\\lambda_1 = 45`}</Eq>, solve <Eq>{`(A^T A - 45I)v = 0`}</Eq>: <Eq>{`v_1 = (1/\\sqrt{2})(1, 1)^T`}</Eq>; for <Eq>{`\\lambda_2 = 5`}</Eq>: <Eq>{`v_2 = (1/\\sqrt{2})(1, -1)^T`}</Eq>.

Left vectors: <Eq>{`u_i = Av_i / \\sigma_i`}</Eq>.

<Eq>{`u_1 = A v_1 / (3\\sqrt{5}) = (1/(3\\sqrt{5})) \\begin{pmatrix} 3/\\sqrt{2} \\\\ 9/\\sqrt{2} \\end{pmatrix} = (1/\\sqrt{10})\\begin{pmatrix}1\\\\3\\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>

**Verification.** <Eq>{`U \\Sigma V^T`}</Eq> should reproduce <Eq>{`A`}</Eq> numerically (verify with calculator).

**Source.** [Understanding Linear Algebra, §6.3](https://understandinglinearalgebra.org/chap6.html) — David Austin · CC-BY-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Rank and subspaces of a matrix via SVD" numero="2">

**Problem.** Determine the rank and bases of the 4 subspaces of <Eq>{`A = \\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\\\ 2 & 4 & 6 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq> via SVD.

**Strategy.** <Eq>{`A`}</Eq> is <Eq>{`2 \\times 3`}</Eq>. Since row 2 is twice row 1, the rank is 1.

**Solution.**

<Eq>{`A^T A = \\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\\\ 2 & 4 \\\\ 3 & 6 \\end{pmatrix}\\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\\\ 2 & 4 & 6 \\end{pmatrix} = \\begin{pmatrix} 5 & 10 & 15 \\\\ 10 & 20 & 30 \\\\ 15 & 30 & 45 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>

Non-zero eigenvalue: <Eq>{`\\lambda_1 = \\text{tr}(A^T A) = 70`}</Eq> (sum of squares of all entries, since rank 1). Singular value: <Eq>{`\\sigma_1 = \\sqrt{70}`}</Eq>.

<Eq>{`v_1 = (1/\\sqrt{14})(1, 2, 3)^T`}</Eq> (direction of row of <Eq>{`A`}</Eq>). <Eq>{`u_1 = Av_1/\\sigma_1 = (1/\\sqrt{2})(1, 2)^T`}</Eq>.

**The 4 subspaces:**
- <Eq>{`\\text{Row}(A) = \\text{span}\\{(1,2,3)\\}`}</Eq> — column 1 of <Eq>{`V`}</Eq>.
- <Eq>{`\\ker(A) = \\text{span}\\{(-2,1,0), (-3,0,1)\\}`}</Eq> — columns 2-3 of <Eq>{`V`}</Eq>.
- <Eq>{`\\text{Col}(A) = \\text{span}\\{(1,2)\\}`}</Eq> — column 1 of <Eq>{`U`}</Eq>.
- <Eq>{`\\ker(A^T) = \\text{span}\\{(-2,1)\\}`}</Eq> — column 2 of <Eq>{`U`}</Eq>.

**Verification.** Rank = number of positive singular values = 1. Confirm: dim Row + dim ker = 1 + 2 = 3 = n. ✓

**Source.** [A First Course in Linear Algebra, §SVD and §FS](http://linear.ups.edu/html/section-SVD.html) — Rob Beezer · GNU FDL.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Best rank-1 approximation by Eckart-Young theorem" numero="3">

**Problem.** Given <Eq>{`A = \\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\\\ 1 & 2 \\\\ 1 & 1 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>, find the best rank-1 approximation and compute the Frobenius error.

**Strategy.** Compute the SVD and retain only the first term.

**Solution.**

<Eq>{`A^T A = \\begin{pmatrix} 6 & 5 \\\\ 5 & 6 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>. Eigenvalues: 11 and 1. Singular values: <Eq>{`\\sigma_1 = \\sqrt{11}`}</Eq>, <Eq>{`\\sigma_2 = 1`}</Eq>.

<Eq>{`v_1 = (1/\\sqrt{2})(1,1)^T`}</Eq>, <Eq>{`v_2 = (1/\\sqrt{2})(1,-1)^T`}</Eq>.

<Eq>{`u_1 = Av_1/\\sqrt{11} = (1/\\sqrt{22})(3, 3, 2)^T`}</Eq> (normalized).

Best rank-1 approximation:

<Eq>{`A_1 = \\sigma_1 u_1 v_1^T = \\sqrt{11} \\cdot \\frac{1}{\\sqrt{22}}\\begin{pmatrix}3\\\\3\\\\2\\end{pmatrix} \\cdot \\frac{1}{\\sqrt{2}}\\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\end{pmatrix} = \\frac{1}{2}\\begin{pmatrix}3&3\\\\3&3\\\\2&2\\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>

Error: <Eq>{`\\|A - A_1\\|_F = \\sigma_2 = 1`}</Eq>.

**Verification.** <Eq>{`\\|A\\|_F^2 = 4+1+1+4+1+1 = 12 = \\sigma_1^2 + \\sigma_2^2 = 11 + 1`}</Eq>. ✓

**Source.** [Understanding Linear Algebra, §6.4 Exercise 6.4.3](https://understandinglinearalgebra.org/chap6.html) — David Austin · CC-BY-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Pseudoinverse of a rectangular matrix" numero="4">

**Problem.** Compute <Eq>{`A^+`}</Eq> for <Eq>{`A = \\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\\\ 0 & 1 \\\\ 0 & 0 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq> and solve the least-squares system <Eq>{`\\min \\|Ax - b\\|`}</Eq> with <Eq>{`b = (2, 3, 1)^T`}</Eq>.

**Strategy.** <Eq>{`A`}</Eq> has orthonormal columns, so trivial SVD: <Eq>{`U = I_{3\\times 3}`}</Eq> (columns in order), <Eq>{`\\Sigma = \\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\\\ 0 & 1 \\\\ 0 & 0 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>, <Eq>{`V = I_{2\\times 2}`}</Eq>.

**Solution.**

<Eq>{`\\Sigma^+ = \\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\\\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>, therefore <Eq>{`A^+ = V \\Sigma^+ U^T = \\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\\\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>.

Solution: <Eq>{`x^* = A^+ b = (2, 3)^T`}</Eq>.

Residual: <Eq>{`b - Ax^* = (2,3,1)^T - (2,3,0)^T = (0,0,1)^T`}</Eq>. Minimum error: <Eq>{`\\|b - Ax^*\\| = 1`}</Eq> (the third coordinate of <Eq>{`b`}</Eq> is irreducible).

**Verification.** <Eq>{`A`}</Eq> has rank 2. The solution <Eq>{`x^* = (2,3)^T`}</Eq> is of minimum norm. <Eq>{`\\|A^+ A\\| = I_2`}</Eq>. ✓

<Exemplo titulo="Image compression: compression ratio and error" numero="5">

**Problem.** A grayscale image has <Eq>{`512 \\times 512`}</Eq> pixels. The singular values decay as <Eq>{`\\sigma_i = 10^4 / i`}</Eq>. Compute: (a) cumulative variance explained by the first <Eq>{`k = 20`}</Eq> components; (b) compression ratio with <Eq>{`k = 20`}</Eq>; (c) relative Frobenius error.

**Strategy.** Use formulas from Eckart-Young Theorem and compression definition.

**Solution.**

(a) <Eq>{`\\|A\\|_F^2 = \\sum_{i=1}^{512} \\sigma_i^2 = 10^8 \\sum_{i=1}^{512} 1/i^2 \\approx 10^8 \\cdot 1{,}6449 = 1{,}645 \\times 10^8`}</Eq>.

<Eq>{`\\sum_{i=1}^{20} \\sigma_i^2 = 10^8 \\sum_{i=1}^{20} 1/i^2 \\approx 10^8 \\cdot 1{,}5962`}</Eq>.

Variance explained: <Eq>{`1{,}5962 / 1{,}6449 \\approx 97{,}0\\%`}</Eq>.

(b) Storage of <Eq>{`A_k`}</Eq>: <Eq>{`k(m + n + 1) = 20(512 + 512 + 1) = 20505`}</Eq> vs <Eq>{`512^2 = 262144`}</Eq>. Ratio: <Eq>{`262144 / 20505 \\approx 12{,}8\\times`}</Eq>.

(c) Relative error: <Eq>{`\\sqrt{1 - 0{,}970} = \\sqrt{0{,}030} \\approx 17{,}3\\%`}</Eq> (in Frobenius).

**Verification.** With <Eq>{`k = 50`}</Eq>, variance explained would be even higher and error lower. The choice <Eq>{`k = 20`}</Eq> already captures 97% with 12.8× compression — excellent for visualization.

**Source.** [Understanding Linear Algebra, §6.4](https://understandinglinearalgebra.org/chap6.html) — David Austin · CC-BY-SA. Decay model of <Eq>{`\\sigma_i`}</Eq> analogous to Exercise 6.4.5.

</Exemplo>
