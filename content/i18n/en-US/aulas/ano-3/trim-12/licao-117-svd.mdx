### The SVD theorem

<Definicao titulo="Singular value decomposition (SVD)">

For every <Eq>{`A \\in \\mathbb{R}^{m \\times n}`}</Eq>, there exist:

- <Eq>{`U \\in \\mathbb{R}^{m \\times m}`}</Eq> orthogonal (columns = **left singular vectors**),
- <Eq>{`V \\in \\mathbb{R}^{n \\times n}`}</Eq> orthogonal (columns = **right singular vectors**),
- <Eq>{`\\Sigma \\in \\mathbb{R}^{m \\times n}`}</Eq> with diagonal entries <Eq>{`\\sigma_1 \\geq \\sigma_2 \\geq \\cdots \\geq \\sigma_r > 0 = \\sigma_{r+1} = \\cdots`}</Eq>,

such that <Eq>{`A = U \\Sigma V^T`}</Eq>. The integer <Eq>{`r = \\text{rank}(A)`}</Eq>.

</Definicao>

> "Any matrix A with rank r can be written as a product $A = U \Sigma V^T$ where U is orthogonal ($m \times m$), $\Sigma$ is diagonal ($m \times n$, nonnegative entries decreasing), and V is orthogonal ($n \times n$). The diagonal entries $\sigma_1 \geq \cdots \geq \sigma_r > 0$ are the singular values of A." — [Understanding Linear Algebra, §6.3](https://understandinglinearalgebra.org/chap6.html)

<Definicao titulo="Compact SVD and rank-1 expansion">

The **compact SVD** (economy SVD) keeps only the <Eq>{`r`}</Eq> non-trivial columns:

<Equation explicacao="Expansion: A is a sum of r rank-1 matrices, weighted by the singular values." latex="A = U_r \Sigma_r V_r^T = \sum_{i=1}^r \sigma_i\, u_i v_i^T" />

Each term <Eq>{`\\sigma_i u_i v_i^T`}</Eq> is a **rank-1 matrix** — the outer product of a column vector with a row vector.

</Definicao>

> "Theorem (Existence of SVD). Every real matrix A has a singular value decomposition. The singular values are the positive square roots of the nonzero eigenvalues of $A^T A$." — [A First Course in Linear Algebra, §SVD](http://linear.pugetsound.edu/html/section-SVD.html)

### Connection with eigenvalues

<Definicao titulo="SVD and the matrices A^T A and A A^T">

- <Eq>{`A^T A = V (\\Sigma^T \\Sigma) V^T`}</Eq>: the **eigenvalues** of <Eq>{`A^T A`}</Eq> are <Eq>{`\\sigma_i^2`}</Eq>, eigenvectors = columns of <Eq>{`V`}</Eq>.
- <Eq>{`A A^T = U (\\Sigma \\Sigma^T) U^T`}</Eq>: the **eigenvalues** of <Eq>{`A A^T`}</Eq> are the same <Eq>{`\\sigma_i^2`}</Eq>, eigenvectors = columns of <Eq>{`U`}</Eq>.
- Singular values are **always real and non-negative**, even if <Eq>{`A`}</Eq> has complex eigenvalues.

</Definicao>

### The Eckart-Young theorem

<Teorema titulo="Best rank-k approximation (Eckart-Young, 1936)">

The best rank-<Eq>{`k`}</Eq> approximation of <Eq>{`A`}</Eq> (in both Frobenius norm and spectral norm) is:

<Equation explicacao="Truncating the SVD at the k largest singular values gives the best rank-k approximation in the least-squares sense." latex="A_k = \sum_{i=1}^k \sigma_i\, u_i v_i^T" />

Errors: <Eq>{`\\|A - A_k\\|_F^2 = \\sigma_{k+1}^2 + \\cdots + \\sigma_r^2`}</Eq>, <Eq>{`\\|A - A_k\\|_2 = \\sigma_{k+1}`}</Eq>.

</Teorema>

### Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse

<Definicao titulo="Pseudoinverse via SVD">

<Equation explicacao="Inverts the positive singular values in place of Sigma; zeroes out the null ones. Solves min ||Ax - b|| with the minimum-norm solution." latex="A^+ = V \Sigma^+ U^T" />

where <Eq>{`\\Sigma^+`}</Eq> is obtained by replacing each <Eq>{`\\sigma_i > 0`}</Eq> with <Eq>{`1/\\sigma_i`}</Eq> and transposing. The solution <Eq>{`x^* = A^+ b`}</Eq> minimizes <Eq>{`\\|Ax - b\\|`}</Eq> with the smallest norm <Eq>{`\\|x\\|`}</Eq> among all minimizers.

</Definicao>

<Insight>

The **condition number** of <Eq>{`A`}</Eq> is <Eq>{`\\kappa_2(A) = \\sigma_1 / \\sigma_r`}</Eq>. A system <Eq>{`Ax = b`}</Eq> with large <Eq>{`\\kappa_2`}</Eq> is ill-conditioned: small errors in <Eq>{`b`}</Eq> produce large errors in <Eq>{`x`}</Eq>. SVD is the standard diagnostic tool for ill-conditioning.

</Insight>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

Imagine you have a **color photo** of a dog. The photo is full of tiny dots of color — so many! The SVD is a **bit of organizing magic**: it splits the photo into **layers of importance**.

The first layer captures the most important parts — the dog's outline. The second captures slightly smaller details. And so on.

If you put together only the first, most important layers, you can still recognize the dog — but using **much less memory**. That is how a computer stores smaller photos without losing too much quality.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

Every matrix can be taken apart into three pieces: <Eq>{`A = U \\Sigma V^T`}</Eq>.

- <Eq>{`V^T`}</Eq> is a **rotation** of the input space.
- <Eq>{`\\Sigma`}</Eq> is a **scaling** — it stretches or shrinks each direction by a number <Eq>{`\\sigma_i \\geq 0`}</Eq>.
- <Eq>{`U`}</Eq> is another **rotation** in the output space.

Those numbers <Eq>{`\\sigma_i`}</Eq> — the **singular values** — tell you the importance of each direction. The largest, <Eq>{`\\sigma_1`}</Eq>, is the most important direction; the smallest is the least important.

**Compression trick**: in a photo of <Eq>{`1000 \\times 1000`}</Eq> pixels, keeping only the 50 largest singular values, you store <Eq>{`50 \\times 1000 + 50 + 50 \\times 1000 = 100050`}</Eq> numbers instead of 1 million — **10 times fewer**. The image still looks good.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old teen">

The SVD is the most important decomposition in applied linear algebra. It combines three properties that no other decomposition has all at once: **generality** (it works for any matrix), **orthogonality** (the bases <Eq>{`U, V`}</Eq> are orthonormal), and **uniqueness** (singular values are unique).

### Geometry

Every linear matrix <Eq>{`A: \\mathbb{R}^n \\to \\mathbb{R}^m`}</Eq> maps the unit sphere to an **ellipsoid**. The semi-axes of the ellipsoid have lengths <Eq>{`\\sigma_1 \\geq \\cdots \\geq \\sigma_r`}</Eq> and point in the directions <Eq>{`u_1, \\ldots, u_r`}</Eq>.

- <Eq>{`V^T`}</Eq> rotates the input space (aligns the vectors <Eq>{`v_i`}</Eq> with the canonical axes).
- <Eq>{`\\Sigma`}</Eq> stretches each axis by <Eq>{`\\sigma_i`}</Eq>.
- <Eq>{`U`}</Eq> rotates the result into the output space.

### Comparison with diagonalization

| Aspect | Diagonalization | SVD |
|---|---|---|
| Applies to | Diagonalizable square matrices | Any matrix |
| Existence | Can fail | Always exists |
| Bases | <Eq>{`P, P^{-1}`}</Eq> (not orthogonal) | <Eq>{`U, V`}</Eq> orthogonal |
| Values | Eigenvalues (<Eq>{`\\mathbb{C}`}</Eq> in general) | Singular (<Eq>{`\\geq 0`}</Eq> real) |

### Immediate application: image compression

A grayscale image is a matrix <Eq>{`M \\in \\mathbb{R}^{m \\times n}`}</Eq>. Truncating the SVD at <Eq>{`k`}</Eq> terms:

- Storing <Eq>{`A_k`}</Eq> requires <Eq>{`k(m + n + 1)`}</Eq> numbers instead of <Eq>{`mn`}</Eq>.
- Relative Frobenius error: <Eq>{`\\|A - A_k\\|_F / \\|A\\|_F = \\sqrt{\\sum_{i>k} \\sigma_i^2} / \\sqrt{\\sum_i \\sigma_i^2}`}</Eq>.

### The Netflix Prize case (2009)

Netflix had a ratings matrix: roughly <Eq>{`10^7`}</Eq> users by <Eq>{`10^5`}</Eq> movies, extremely sparse (most user-movie pairs had no rating). Low-rank approximation (a variant of the SVD) predicted the missing ratings. The winning team, BellKor's Pragmatic Chaos, improved accuracy by 10% and won a prize of **1 million dollars**. The core technique was **matrix factorization**: representing each user and each movie by a short vector of <Eq>{`k = 20`}</Eq> to <Eq>{`100`}</Eq> "latent factors" (taste for action, comedy, romance...) and predicting the rating as a dot product. Truncated SVD is the canonical method for this factorization when the matrix is complete; for sparse matrices, iterative variants are used (Alternating Least Squares, gradient descent).

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

In an engineering course, the SVD is often the **first tool** you reach for when you need to handle real data (noisy, ill-conditioned, high-dimensional). Get to know it before any other dimensionality-reduction technique.

### Computation algorithm (small case, by hand)

For <Eq>{`A \\in \\mathbb{R}^{m \\times n}`}</Eq> with <Eq>{`m \\geq n`}</Eq>:

1. Compute <Eq>{`A^T A`}</Eq> (a symmetric PSD <Eq>{`n \\times n`}</Eq> matrix).
2. Find eigenvalues <Eq>{`\\lambda_1 \\geq \\cdots \\geq \\lambda_n \\geq 0`}</Eq> and orthonormal eigenvectors <Eq>{`v_1, \\ldots, v_n`}</Eq>.
3. Define <Eq>{`\\sigma_i = \\sqrt{\\lambda_i}`}</Eq>.
4. For each <Eq>{`\\sigma_i > 0`}</Eq>, define <Eq>{`u_i = A v_i / \\sigma_i \\in \\mathbb{R}^m`}</Eq>. These <Eq>{`u_i`}</Eq> are automatically orthonormal.
5. Complete <Eq>{`u_{r+1}, \\ldots, u_m`}</Eq> with an orthonormal basis of <Eq>{`\\ker(A^T)`}</Eq> (use Gram-Schmidt).
6. Assemble <Eq>{`U = [u_1 | \\cdots | u_m]`}</Eq>, <Eq>{`V = [v_1 | \\cdots | v_n]`}</Eq>, <Eq>{`\\Sigma`}</Eq> diagonal with <Eq>{`\\sigma_i`}</Eq>.

Numerically, this algorithm is unstable (it loses precision when forming <Eq>{`A^T A`}</Eq>, which has condition number <Eq>{`\\kappa(A)^2`}</Eq>). Professional packages (LAPACK, NumPy) use the Golub-Kahan-Reinsch algorithm (1965): bidiagonalization via Householder reflections followed by implicit QR. Cost: <Eq>{`O(\\min(mn^2, m^2n))`}</Eq>.

### Why the SVD is numerically stable

When you solve <Eq>{`Ax = b`}</Eq> by Gaussian elimination, the error in <Eq>{`x`}</Eq> scales with <Eq>{`\\kappa_2(A) = \\sigma_1 / \\sigma_r`}</Eq>. When <Eq>{`\\kappa_2`}</Eq> is large (say, <Eq>{`10^{12}`}</Eq>) and <Eq>{`b`}</Eq> has a relative error of <Eq>{`10^{-15}`}</Eq>, the error in <Eq>{`x`}</Eq> can reach <Eq>{`10^{-3}`}</Eq> — three significant digits lost. SVD lets you **discard small singular values** (truncated SVD, TSVD) and solve in a regularized way: <Eq>{`x = V \\Sigma_k^+ U^T b`}</Eq>. It is the linear version of Tikhonov regularization.

### Cross-domain applications

- **Latent Semantic Analysis (LSA)** — Deerwester et al. 1990. A document-term matrix decomposed by SVD; "latent" meaning emerges from the singular vectors.
- **Recommendation systems** — Netflix Prize (2009). SVD on a sparse user-item matrix via ALS (Alternating Least Squares).
- **PCA** — next lesson. PCA is the SVD of the centered data matrix, divided by <Eq>{`\\sqrt{N-1}`}</Eq>.
- **Image compression** — JPEG uses the DCT (related), but truncated SVD gives an optimal theoretical benchmark (Eckart-Young).
- **Total Least Squares** — line fitting when both variables have error. Solved via the SVD of the augmented matrix <Eq>{`[A | b]`}</Eq>.

### Short numerical example

Compute the SVD of <Eq>{`A = \\begin{pmatrix} 3 & 0 \\\\ 4 & 5 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>.

**Step 1.** <Eq>{`A^T A = \\begin{pmatrix} 25 & 20 \\\\ 20 & 25 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>.

**Step 2.** Eigenvalues: <Eq>{`\\det(A^TA - \\lambda I) = (25-\\lambda)^2 - 400 = 0 \\Rightarrow \\lambda = 45, 5`}</Eq>.

**Step 3.** Singular values: <Eq>{`\\sigma_1 = \\sqrt{45} = 3\\sqrt{5}`}</Eq>, <Eq>{`\\sigma_2 = \\sqrt{5}`}</Eq>.

**Step 4.** Eigenvectors of <Eq>{`A^TA`}</Eq>: <Eq>{`v_1 = (1,1)/\\sqrt{2}`}</Eq>, <Eq>{`v_2 = (1,-1)/\\sqrt{2}`}</Eq>.

**Step 5.** <Eq>{`u_1 = Av_1/\\sigma_1 = (3,9)/(3\\sqrt{5} \\cdot \\sqrt{2}) = (1,3)/\\sqrt{10}`}</Eq>, <Eq>{`u_2 = (3,-1)/\\sqrt{10}`}</Eq>.

So <Eq>{`A = U\\Sigma V^T`}</Eq> with <Eq>{`U = \\frac{1}{\\sqrt{10}}\\begin{pmatrix}1 & 3 \\\\ 3 & -1\\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>, <Eq>{`\\Sigma = \\begin{pmatrix}3\\sqrt{5} & 0 \\\\ 0 & \\sqrt{5}\\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>, <Eq>{`V = \\frac{1}{\\sqrt{2}}\\begin{pmatrix}1 & 1 \\\\ 1 & -1\\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>.

Condition number: <Eq>{`\\kappa_2(A) = 3\\sqrt{5}/\\sqrt{5} = 3`}</Eq> — well-conditioned.

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

The SVD ties together many seemingly unrelated themes. The senior professional should see those connections.

### Unification with other decompositions

| Decomposition | When it applies | SVD generalizes |
|---|---|---|
| <Eq>{`A = QDQ^T`}</Eq> (spectral) | <Eq>{`A`}</Eq> symmetric | SVD = spectral if <Eq>{`A \\succeq 0`}</Eq> |
| <Eq>{`A = QR`}</Eq> (QR) | Any <Eq>{`m \\times n`}</Eq> | SVD is a "double QR" |
| <Eq>{`A = LL^T`}</Eq> (Cholesky) | <Eq>{`A`}</Eq> SPD | SVD applied to a Cholesky factor gives the original SVD |
| <Eq>{`A = PLU`}</Eq> (LU) | Any square matrix | SVD is the general canonical form |

The SVD is the only decomposition with **three simultaneous properties**: orthogonality, generality, and canonical ordering of the diagonal factors.

### Variational characterizations (Courant-Fischer)

The <Eq>{`k`}</Eq>-th singular value admits a min-max characterization:

<Equation explicacao="Min-max over subspaces of dimension k: the k-th singular value is the sup of the norm of A restricted to maximal subspaces." latex="\sigma_k = \max_{\dim S = k} \min_{x \in S, \|x\|=1} \|Ax\|" />

Consequence (Weyl, 1912): for arbitrary <Eq>{`A, B`}</Eq>, <Eq>{`|\\sigma_i(A+B) - \\sigma_i(A)| \\leq \\|B\\|_2`}</Eq>. Singular values are **Lipschitz-1** with respect to the spectral norm. This explains why the SVD is robust to noise.

### Eckart-Young and the optimal approximation theorem

The Eckart-Young theorem (1936) is one of the gems of 20th-century applied mathematics. It states that the truncated SVD **simultaneously** solves two problems:

<Equation explicacao="In both the spectral and the Frobenius norm, A_k is the best rank-k approximation among all matrices of the same size." latex="A_k = \arg\min_{\text{rank}(B) \leq k} \|A - B\|_F = \arg\min_{\text{rank}(B) \leq k} \|A - B\|_2" />

Sketch of proof (Frobenius norm): expanding <Eq>{`A = \\sum_i \\sigma_i u_i v_i^T`}</Eq> in an orthonormal basis of <Eq>{`m \\times n`}</Eq>, the best projection onto a subspace of dimension <Eq>{`k`}</Eq> is to take the <Eq>{`k`}</Eq> largest coefficients — exactly <Eq>{`A_k`}</Eq>.

### Pseudoinverse and least squares

For any <Eq>{`A \\in \\mathbb{R}^{m \\times n}`}</Eq>, the problem <Eq>{`\\min_x \\|Ax - b\\|`}</Eq> always has a solution. When there are infinitely many (underdetermined system or rank-deficient <Eq>{`A`}</Eq>), the pseudoinverse selects the one of smallest norm:

<Equation explicacao="Minimum-norm solution of the least-squares problem via the pseudoinverse." latex="x^* = A^+ b = V \Sigma^+ U^T b" />

When <Eq>{`A`}</Eq> has full column rank: <Eq>{`A^+ = (A^T A)^{-1} A^T`}</Eq>, recovering the normal equations. When it has full row rank: <Eq>{`A^+ = A^T (A A^T)^{-1}`}</Eq>. In general, <Eq>{`A^+`}</Eq> is defined by the SVD.

### Generalization: SVD of operators on Hilbert spaces

The SVD extends to compact operators on Hilbert spaces (Schmidt, 1907): <Eq>{`T = \\sum_i \\sigma_i \\langle \\cdot, v_i \\rangle u_i`}</Eq>. The singular values <Eq>{`\\sigma_i \\to 0`}</Eq> encode the operator's "degree of compactness." In PDE and spectral theory, it is the **Schmidt-Mirsky decomposition**.

### Connection with quantum physics

In quantum mechanics, the **entanglement** of a bipartite state <Eq>{`|\\psi\\rangle \\in \\mathcal{H}_A \\otimes \\mathcal{H}_B`}</Eq> is measured by the **Schmidt decomposition** (1907 again) — exactly the SVD of the matrix of state coefficients. The squared singular values <Eq>{`\\sigma_i^2`}</Eq> are the probabilities of the Schmidt states, and the von Neumann entropy is <Eq>{`S = -\\sum_i \\sigma_i^2 \\log \\sigma_i^2`}</Eq>.

### Why it matters in quantitative finance

In building risk factors (a generalized Fama-French model), one applies the SVD to the returns matrix <Eq>{`R \\in \\mathbb{R}^{T \\times N}`}</Eq> (periods by assets). The first singular values capture market factors (common movement), the next capture sector factors, and the smallest are idiosyncratic noise. Financial PCA (next lesson) does this canonically. Ribeiro & Veronesi (2002) use the SVD on yield curves to extract Nelson-Siegel factors; every fixed-income desk uses this technique.

### Practical numerical tricks

1. **Never compute <Eq>{`A^T A`}</Eq> explicitly** if <Eq>{`\\kappa(A) > 10^4`}</Eq>. Use Golub-Kahan-Reinsch directly on <Eq>{`A`}</Eq>.
2. **Use truncated SVD** when you only need the <Eq>{`k`}</Eq> largest values. Lanczos bidiagonalization (ARPACK) computes them in <Eq>{`O(mnk)`}</Eq> instead of <Eq>{`O(mn^2)`}</Eq>.
3. **For large sparse matrices**: use iterative methods (randomized SVD of Halko-Martinsson-Tropp, 2011) — goes from <Eq>{`O(mn^2)`}</Eq> to <Eq>{`O(mn \\log k)`}</Eq> with a small loss of precision.
4. **Tolerance for "zero singular value"**: <Eq>{`\\sigma_i < m \\cdot \\varepsilon_{\\text{machine}} \\cdot \\sigma_1`}</Eq>. In float64, <Eq>{`\\varepsilon \\approx 10^{-16}`}</Eq>.

### Example 1: Grayscale image compression

A portrait photograph at <Eq>{`512 \\times 512`}</Eq> pixels is a matrix <Eq>{`M \\in \\mathbb{R}^{512 \\times 512}`}</Eq> with 262,144 entries. After the SVD, the first 30 singular values typically capture <Eq>{`> 95\\%`}</Eq> of the Frobenius norm. Keeping <Eq>{`k = 30`}</Eq>:

- Storage: <Eq>{`30 \\times (512 + 1 + 512) = 30\\,750`}</Eq> numbers (vs. 262,144).
- Compression ratio: <Eq>{`\\approx 8.5\\times`}</Eq>.
- Error: depending on the image, <Eq>{`\\|A - A_{30}\\|_F / \\|A\\|_F \\approx 5\\%`}</Eq>.

Comparison: JPEG at the "high quality" setting uses a similar compression ratio (8–10×) with a similar percentage error — only using the DCT on <Eq>{`8 \\times 8`}</Eq> blocks instead of a global SVD. Eckart-Young says the SVD is the optimal theoretical limit for low rank.

### Example 2: Simplified recommendation system

Four users (Ana, Beto, Carla, Diego) rate three movies (Matrix, Titanic, Star Wars) on a 1–5 scale:

| | Matrix | Titanic | Star Wars |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ana | 5 | 1 | 5 |
| Beto | 4 | 1 | 4 |
| Carla | 1 | 5 | 1 |
| Diego | 1 | 4 | 2 |

The SVD reveals 2 main latent factors: "preference for science fiction" (the Ana/Beto vs. Carla/Diego axis) and "rating intensity." The rank-2 reconstruction predicts missing ratings. At real scale (Netflix), one applies <Eq>{`k = 20`}</Eq> to <Eq>{`100`}</Eq> factors on a sparse <Eq>{`10^7 \\times 10^5`}</Eq> matrix.

### Example 3: Latent Semantic Analysis (LSA)

A document-term matrix of 1000 documents by 5000 words. Each entry <Eq>{`a_{ij}`}</Eq> = frequency of word <Eq>{`j`}</Eq> in document <Eq>{`i`}</Eq> (TF-IDF). SVD with <Eq>{`k = 100`}</Eq> groups together words with close meaning (synonyms) and documents with close topic, even without sharing literal words. It was the foundation of **semantic search** before neural embeddings (Deerwester et al., 1990; Hofmann, 1999).

### Example 4: Face recognition — Eigenfaces

Turk & Pentland (1991) treated each face as a vector of pixels (<Eq>{`64 \\times 64 = 4096`}</Eq> dimensions). Applying the SVD to a matrix of 200 mean-centered faces, the first 20–40 singular vectors form a "face basis" — the **eigenfaces**. A new face is classified by its projection onto this basis. It was the first viable automatic face-recognition system.

### Example 5: Industrial quality control

In a chip factory, 30 sensors monitor each production line in real time (vibration, temperature, current, etc.). This forms a matrix <Eq>{`A \\in \\mathbb{R}^{T \\times 30}`}</Eq> (<Eq>{`T`}</Eq> time instants). The first singular values capture the "normal mode" of operation; defects appear as components along the directions of small singular values. This technique is called **monitoring PCA** (Jackson & Mudholkar, 1979) and detects faults before they manifest as yield loss.

## Worked examples

<Exemplo titulo="SVD of a 2x2 square matrix (application)" numero="1">

**Problem.** Find the SVD of <Eq>{`A = \\begin{pmatrix} 4 & 0 \\\\ 3 & -5 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>.

**Strategy.** Compute <Eq>{`A^T A`}</Eq>, find orthonormal eigenvalues and eigenvectors to obtain <Eq>{`V`}</Eq> and the <Eq>{`\\sigma_i`}</Eq>; then <Eq>{`u_i = A v_i / \\sigma_i`}</Eq>.

**Solution.**

**Step 1.** <Eq>{`A^T A = \\begin{pmatrix} 4 & 3 \\\\ 0 & -5 \\end{pmatrix} \\begin{pmatrix} 4 & 0 \\\\ 3 & -5 \\end{pmatrix} = \\begin{pmatrix} 25 & -15 \\\\ -15 & 25 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>.

**Step 2.** Eigenvalues: <Eq>{`\\det(A^T A - \\lambda I) = (25 - \\lambda)^2 - 225 = 0 \\Rightarrow \\lambda = 25 \\pm 15 = 40, 10`}</Eq>.

**Step 3.** Singular values: <Eq>{`\\sigma_1 = \\sqrt{40} = 2\\sqrt{10}`}</Eq>, <Eq>{`\\sigma_2 = \\sqrt{10}`}</Eq>.

**Step 4.** Eigenvectors of <Eq>{`A^T A`}</Eq>:
- For <Eq>{`\\lambda_1 = 40`}</Eq>: <Eq>{`(A^TA - 40I)v = 0 \\Rightarrow -15 v_1 - 15 v_2 = 0 \\Rightarrow v_1 = -v_2`}</Eq>. So <Eq>{`v_1 = (1, -1)/\\sqrt{2}`}</Eq>.
- For <Eq>{`\\lambda_2 = 10`}</Eq>: <Eq>{`v_2 = (1, 1)/\\sqrt{2}`}</Eq>.

**Step 5.** Left singular vectors:

<Eq>{`u_1 = \\frac{A v_1}{\\sigma_1} = \\frac{1}{2\\sqrt{10}} \\cdot \\frac{1}{\\sqrt{2}}\\begin{pmatrix} 4 \\\\ 8 \\end{pmatrix} = \\frac{1}{\\sqrt{20}}\\begin{pmatrix} 2 \\\\ 4 \\end{pmatrix}/\\sqrt{2} = \\frac{1}{\\sqrt{5}}\\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\\\ 2 \\end{pmatrix}/\\sqrt{?}`}</Eq>

Simplified computation: <Eq>{`A v_1 = \\frac{1}{\\sqrt{2}}(4, 8)^T`}</Eq>; <Eq>{`\\|A v_1\\| = \\frac{\\sqrt{80}}{\\sqrt{2}} = \\sqrt{40} = \\sigma_1`}</Eq>. So <Eq>{`u_1 = (1, 2)/\\sqrt{5}`}</Eq>.

Analogously <Eq>{`A v_2 = \\frac{1}{\\sqrt{2}}(4, -2)^T`}</Eq>; <Eq>{`u_2 = (2, -1)/\\sqrt{5}`}</Eq>.

**Answer.**

<Eq>{`U = \\frac{1}{\\sqrt{5}}\\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\\\ 2 & -1 \\end{pmatrix}, \\quad \\Sigma = \\begin{pmatrix} 2\\sqrt{10} & 0 \\\\ 0 & \\sqrt{10} \\end{pmatrix}, \\quad V = \\frac{1}{\\sqrt{2}}\\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\\\ -1 & 1 \\end{pmatrix}.`}</Eq>

**Verification.** <Eq>{`U \\Sigma V^T = \\frac{1}{\\sqrt{5}}\\begin{pmatrix}1 & 2 \\\\ 2 & -1\\end{pmatrix}\\begin{pmatrix}2\\sqrt{10} & 0 \\\\ 0 & \\sqrt{10}\\end{pmatrix}\\frac{1}{\\sqrt{2}}\\begin{pmatrix}1 & -1 \\\\ 1 & 1\\end{pmatrix} = \\begin{pmatrix}4 & 0 \\\\ 3 & -5\\end{pmatrix} = A`}</Eq>. Correct.

**Source.** [Beezer, A First Course in Linear Algebra, §SVD](http://linear.pugetsound.edu/html/section-SVD.html) — GNU FDL.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="SVD of a 3x2 rectangular matrix (intermediate)" numero="2">

**Problem.** Compute the compact SVD of <Eq>{`A = \\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\\\ 1 & 0 \\\\ 0 & 1 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>.

**Strategy.** For a <Eq>{`3 \\times 2`}</Eq> <Eq>{`A`}</Eq>, form the <Eq>{`2 \\times 2`}</Eq> matrix <Eq>{`A^T A`}</Eq> (easier than <Eq>{`A A^T`}</Eq>, which would be <Eq>{`3 \\times 3`}</Eq>).

**Solution.**

<Eq>{`A^T A = \\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 & 0 \\\\ 1 & 0 & 1 \\end{pmatrix}\\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\\\ 1 & 0 \\\\ 0 & 1 \\end{pmatrix} = \\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\\\ 1 & 2 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>.

Eigenvalues: <Eq>{`(2-\\lambda)^2 - 1 = 0 \\Rightarrow \\lambda = 3, 1`}</Eq>.

Singular values: <Eq>{`\\sigma_1 = \\sqrt{3}`}</Eq>, <Eq>{`\\sigma_2 = 1`}</Eq>.

Eigenvectors: for <Eq>{`\\lambda = 3`}</Eq>: <Eq>{`v_1 = (1, 1)/\\sqrt{2}`}</Eq>. For <Eq>{`\\lambda = 1`}</Eq>: <Eq>{`v_2 = (1, -1)/\\sqrt{2}`}</Eq>.

Left vectors:

<Eq>{`u_1 = \\frac{A v_1}{\\sigma_1} = \\frac{1}{\\sqrt{3}} \\cdot \\frac{1}{\\sqrt{2}}\\begin{pmatrix}2\\\\1\\\\1\\end{pmatrix} = \\frac{1}{\\sqrt{6}}\\begin{pmatrix}2\\\\1\\\\1\\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>.

<Eq>{`u_2 = \\frac{A v_2}{\\sigma_2} = \\frac{1}{\\sqrt{2}}\\begin{pmatrix}0\\\\1\\\\-1\\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>.

**Answer (compact form, discarding the null column).**

<Eq>{`U_2 = \\begin{pmatrix} 2/\\sqrt{6} & 0 \\\\ 1/\\sqrt{6} & 1/\\sqrt{2} \\\\ 1/\\sqrt{6} & -1/\\sqrt{2} \\end{pmatrix}, \\quad \\Sigma_2 = \\begin{pmatrix} \\sqrt{3} & 0 \\\\ 0 & 1 \\end{pmatrix}, \\quad V = \\frac{1}{\\sqrt{2}}\\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\\\ 1 & -1 \\end{pmatrix}.`}</Eq>

**Verification.** The rank of <Eq>{`A`}</Eq> is 2 (the rows <Eq>{`(1,1), (1,0), (0,1)`}</Eq> have 2 linearly independent). So the compact SVD has exactly 2 positive singular values. Verified.

**Source.** [Beezer, A First Course in Linear Algebra, §SVD Example SVD3by2](http://linear.pugetsound.edu/html/section-SVD.html) — GNU FDL.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Rank-1 approximation and Eckart-Young (intermediate)" numero="3">

**Problem.** For <Eq>{`A = \\begin{pmatrix} 3 & 0 \\\\ 4 & 5 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>, find the best rank-1 approximation and compute the Frobenius error.

**Strategy.** Apply the SVD, take the first term of the expansion <Eq>{`A_1 = \\sigma_1 u_1 v_1^T`}</Eq>, and compute <Eq>{`\\|A - A_1\\|_F = \\sigma_2`}</Eq>.

**Solution.**

From Door 25: <Eq>{`\\sigma_1 = 3\\sqrt{5}`}</Eq>, <Eq>{`\\sigma_2 = \\sqrt{5}`}</Eq>, <Eq>{`u_1 = (1, 3)/\\sqrt{10}`}</Eq>, <Eq>{`v_1 = (1, 1)/\\sqrt{2}`}</Eq>.

<Eq>{`A_1 = \\sigma_1 u_1 v_1^T = 3\\sqrt{5} \\cdot \\frac{1}{\\sqrt{10}}\\begin{pmatrix}1\\\\3\\end{pmatrix} \\cdot \\frac{1}{\\sqrt{2}}(1, 1) = \\frac{3\\sqrt{5}}{\\sqrt{20}}\\begin{pmatrix}1 & 1 \\\\ 3 & 3\\end{pmatrix} = \\frac{3}{2}\\begin{pmatrix}1 & 1 \\\\ 3 & 3\\end{pmatrix} = \\begin{pmatrix}1{,}5 & 1{,}5 \\\\ 4{,}5 & 4{,}5\\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>.

Error: <Eq>{`A - A_1 = \\begin{pmatrix}1{,}5 & -1{,}5 \\\\ -0{,}5 & 0{,}5\\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>.

<Eq>{`\\|A - A_1\\|_F = \\sqrt{1{,}5^2 + 1{,}5^2 + 0{,}5^2 + 0{,}5^2} = \\sqrt{5} = \\sigma_2`}</Eq>.

**Verification.** By Eckart-Young, <Eq>{`\\|A - A_k\\|_F = \\sqrt{\\sum_{i>k} \\sigma_i^2}`}</Eq>. Here <Eq>{`k = 1`}</Eq>, so <Eq>{`\\|A - A_1\\|_F = \\sigma_2 = \\sqrt{5} \\approx 2{,}236`}</Eq>. Consistent with our direct computation.

**Captured energy.** <Eq>{`\\sigma_1^2 / (\\sigma_1^2 + \\sigma_2^2) = 45/50 = 90\\%`}</Eq>. The rank-1 approximation captures 90% of the energy of <Eq>{`A`}</Eq>.

**Source.** [Austin, Understanding Linear Algebra, §6.4](https://understandinglinearalgebra.org/chap6.html) — CC-BY-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Pseudoinverse and least squares (advanced)" numero="4">

**Problem.** Use the SVD to find the least-squares solution of <Eq>{`Ax = b`}</Eq>, where <Eq>{`A = \\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\\\ 1 & 1 \\\\ 0 & 1 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq> and <Eq>{`b = (2, 0, 3)^T`}</Eq>.

**Strategy.** Compute the compact SVD of <Eq>{`A`}</Eq>, form the pseudoinverse <Eq>{`A^+ = V \\Sigma^+ U^T`}</Eq>, compute <Eq>{`x^* = A^+ b`}</Eq>.

**Solution.**

<Eq>{`A^T A = \\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 2 \\\\ 2 & 3 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>. Eigenvalues: <Eq>{`\\det = (2-\\lambda)(3-\\lambda) - 4 = \\lambda^2 - 5\\lambda + 2 = 0`}</Eq>, so <Eq>{`\\lambda = (5 \\pm \\sqrt{17})/2`}</Eq>.

Numerically: <Eq>{`\\lambda_1 \\approx 4{,}561`}</Eq>, <Eq>{`\\lambda_2 \\approx 0{,}439`}</Eq>. Singular values: <Eq>{`\\sigma_1 \\approx 2{,}136`}</Eq>, <Eq>{`\\sigma_2 \\approx 0{,}662`}</Eq>.

Eigenvectors (after normalization): <Eq>{`v_1 \\approx (0{,}615, 0{,}789)^T`}</Eq>, <Eq>{`v_2 \\approx (0{,}789, -0{,}615)^T`}</Eq>.

Left singular vectors: <Eq>{`u_i = A v_i / \\sigma_i`}</Eq>. Direct computation gives <Eq>{`u_1 \\approx (0{,}658, 0{,}658, 0{,}369)^T`}</Eq> and <Eq>{`u_2 \\approx (0{,}198, 0{,}198, -0{,}929)^T`}</Eq>.

Pseudoinverse: <Eq>{`A^+ = V \\text{diag}(1/\\sigma_1, 1/\\sigma_2) U^T`}</Eq>.

Solution: <Eq>{`x^* = A^+ b`}</Eq>. Equivalently, since <Eq>{`A`}</Eq> has full column rank, <Eq>{`x^* = (A^T A)^{-1} A^T b`}</Eq>.

<Eq>{`A^T b = (2, 5)^T`}</Eq>, <Eq>{`(A^T A)^{-1} = \\frac{1}{2}\\begin{pmatrix}3 & -2 \\\\ -2 & 2\\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>.

<Eq>{`x^* = \\frac{1}{2}\\begin{pmatrix}3 & -2 \\\\ -2 & 2\\end{pmatrix}\\begin{pmatrix}2\\\\5\\end{pmatrix} = \\frac{1}{2}\\begin{pmatrix}-4 \\\\ 6\\end{pmatrix} = \\begin{pmatrix}-2 \\\\ 3\\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>.

**Verification.** <Eq>{`A x^* = (1, 1, 3)^T`}</Eq>; residual <Eq>{`b - Ax^* = (1, -1, 0)^T`}</Eq>; norm <Eq>{`\\sqrt{2}`}</Eq>. The residual is orthogonal to <Eq>{`\\text{col}(A)`}</Eq>: <Eq>{`A^T(b - Ax^*) = (1 \\cdot 1 + 1 \\cdot (-1), 1 \\cdot 1 + 1 \\cdot (-1) + 1 \\cdot 0)^T = (0, 0)^T`}</Eq>. Correct.

**Remark.** Since <Eq>{`A`}</Eq> has full rank, the SVD and the normal equations give the same result. But if <Eq>{`A`}</Eq> were rank-deficient, **only** the SVD would work (the normal equations would have infinitely many solutions).

**Source.** [Hefferon, Linear Algebra, ch. 5, §IV](https://hefferon.net/linearalgebra/) — CC-BY-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Prove the existence of the SVD via the spectral theorem (proof)" numero="5">

**Problem.** Show that every matrix <Eq>{`A \\in \\mathbb{R}^{m \\times n}`}</Eq> admits an SVD, using the spectral theorem for real symmetric matrices.

**Strategy.** Apply the spectral theorem to the matrix <Eq>{`A^T A`}</Eq> (symmetric PSD), construct <Eq>{`V`}</Eq> and <Eq>{`\\Sigma`}</Eq>, then define <Eq>{`U`}</Eq> via <Eq>{`u_i = A v_i / \\sigma_i`}</Eq> and complete the basis.

**Solution.**

**Step 1.** <Eq>{`A^T A \\in \\mathbb{R}^{n \\times n}`}</Eq> is symmetric and PSD (shown in L116, Example 5).

**Step 2.** By the spectral theorem (L116), there exist eigenvalues <Eq>{`\\lambda_1 \\geq \\cdots \\geq \\lambda_n \\geq 0`}</Eq> and orthonormal eigenvectors <Eq>{`v_1, \\ldots, v_n`}</Eq> such that <Eq>{`A^T A v_i = \\lambda_i v_i`}</Eq>.

**Step 3.** Let <Eq>{`r`}</Eq> be the number of <Eq>{`\\lambda_i > 0`}</Eq>. Define <Eq>{`\\sigma_i = \\sqrt{\\lambda_i}`}</Eq> for <Eq>{`i \\leq r`}</Eq>.

**Step 4.** For <Eq>{`i \\leq r`}</Eq>, define <Eq>{`u_i = A v_i / \\sigma_i \\in \\mathbb{R}^m`}</Eq>. Verify:

<Eq>{`\\langle u_i, u_j \\rangle = \\frac{(Av_i)^T(Av_j)}{\\sigma_i \\sigma_j} = \\frac{v_i^T A^T A v_j}{\\sigma_i \\sigma_j} = \\frac{\\lambda_j v_i^T v_j}{\\sigma_i \\sigma_j} = \\frac{\\lambda_j \\delta_{ij}}{\\sigma_i \\sigma_j} = \\delta_{ij}`}</Eq>.

So <Eq>{`u_1, \\ldots, u_r`}</Eq> are orthonormal.

**Step 5.** Complete <Eq>{`u_{r+1}, \\ldots, u_m`}</Eq> with an orthonormal basis of <Eq>{`\\ker(A^T) = (\\text{col}(A))^\\perp`}</Eq>. (It exists by Gram-Schmidt.) Then <Eq>{`U = [u_1 | \\cdots | u_m] \\in \\mathbb{R}^{m \\times m}`}</Eq> is orthogonal.

**Step 6.** Verify <Eq>{`A = U \\Sigma V^T`}</Eq>. Equivalently, <Eq>{`A V = U \\Sigma`}</Eq>. For <Eq>{`i \\leq r`}</Eq>: <Eq>{`(AV)_{:,i} = A v_i = \\sigma_i u_i = (U\\Sigma)_{:,i}`}</Eq>. For <Eq>{`i > r`}</Eq>: <Eq>{`A v_i = 0`}</Eq> (since <Eq>{`\\|A v_i\\|^2 = v_i^T A^T A v_i = \\lambda_i = 0`}</Eq>) and <Eq>{`(U\\Sigma)_{:,i} = 0`}</Eq>. So <Eq>{`AV = U\\Sigma`}</Eq>, that is <Eq>{`A = U \\Sigma V^T`}</Eq>. <Eq>{`\\square`}</Eq>

**Remark.** The uniqueness of the <Eq>{`\\sigma_i`}</Eq> follows from the uniqueness of the eigenvalues of <Eq>{`A^T A`}</Eq>. The <Eq>{`u_i, v_i`}</Eq> are unique when the <Eq>{`\\sigma_i`}</Eq> are distinct and positive; in the case of multiplicity, there is freedom of rotation within the eigenspace.

**Source.** [Beezer, A First Course in Linear Algebra, §SVD Theorem EXIST-SVD](http://linear.pugetsound.edu/html/section-SVD.html) — GNU FDL.

</Exemplo>
