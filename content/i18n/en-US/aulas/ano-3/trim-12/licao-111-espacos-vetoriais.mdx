<Porta nivel="formal" titulo="Axiomatic definition">

### The 8 vector space axioms

<Definicao titulo="K-vector space">

Let $K$ be a field (typically $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$). A **$K$-vector space** is a nonempty set $V$ equipped with two operations:

- **addition**: $+ : V \times V \to V$
- **scalar multiplication**: $\cdot : K \times V \to V$

satisfying, for all $u, v, w \in V$ and $a, b \in K$:

| # | Axiom | Formula |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Associativity | $(u + v) + w = u + (v + w)$ |
| 2 | Commutativity | $u + v = v + u$ |
| 3 | Additive identity | $\exists\, \mathbf{0} \in V : v + \mathbf{0} = v$ |
| 4 | Additive inverse | $\exists\, (-v) \in V : v + (-v) = \mathbf{0}$ |
| 5 | Vector distributivity | $a(u + v) = au + av$ |
| 6 | Scalar distributivity | $(a + b)v = av + bv$ |
| 7 | Compatibility | $a(bv) = (ab)v$ |
| 8 | Scalar identity | $1 \cdot v = v$ |

</Definicao>

> "We will use the term *field* to refer to either $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$. Elements of a field are called *scalars*. A *vector space* is a set $V$ along with an addition on $V$ and a scalar multiplication on $V$ such that the following properties hold..." — [Axler — *Linear Algebra Done Right*, §1A](https://linear.axler.net/)

<Definicao titulo="Vector subspace">

$W \subseteq V$ is a **subspace** of $V$ if:
1. $\mathbf{0} \in W$
2. $u, v \in W \Rightarrow u + v \in W$ (closed under addition)
3. $a \in K,\ v \in W \Rightarrow av \in W$ (closed under scalars)

**Shortcut**: it suffices to verify $\mathbf{0} \in W$ and closure under **linear combination**: $au + bv \in W$ for all $a, b \in K$, $u, v \in W$.

</Definicao>

> "A subset $W$ of $V$ is called a *subspace* of $V$ if $W$ is a vector space under the addition and scalar multiplication defined on $V$." — [Beezer — *A First Course in Linear Algebra*, §VS](http://linear.ups.edu/fcla/section-VS.html)

<Definicao titulo="Linear combination, span, linear independence, basis">

Given $S = \{v_1, \ldots, v_k\} \subseteq V$:

- **Linear combination**: $c_1 v_1 + \cdots + c_k v_k$ with $c_i \in K$.
- **Span** (generated space): $\text{span}(S) = \{c_1 v_1 + \cdots + c_k v_k : c_i \in K\}$ — the smallest subspace containing $S$.
- **Linearly independent (LI)**: $c_1 v_1 + \cdots + c_k v_k = \mathbf{0} \Rightarrow c_1 = \cdots = c_k = 0$.
- **Basis**: $\mathcal{B} \subseteq V$ that is LI and $\text{span}(\mathcal{B}) = V$.
- **Dimension**: $\dim V = |\mathcal{B}|$ — well-defined by the theorem below.

</Definicao>

<Teorema titulo="Invariance of dimension (Steinitz)">

If $V$ has a basis with $n$ elements, then **every** basis of $V$ has exactly $n$ elements.

**Proof (sketch for finite dim):** Suppose $\mathcal{B} = \{b_1, \ldots, b_n\}$ and $\mathcal{C} = \{c_1, \ldots, c_m\}$ are both bases. Since $\mathcal{B}$ generates $V$, each $c_j$ is a linear combination of $\mathcal{B}$. If $m > n$, the system of $m$ equations in $n$ scalars would have a nontrivial solution, contradicting LI of $\mathcal{C}$. Thus $m \leq n$. Symmetry gives $n \leq m$, hence $m = n$. $\blacksquare$

</Teorema>

<Teorema titulo="Classification in finite dimension">

Two $K$-vector spaces of finite dimension are isomorphic if and only if they have the same dimension.

$$V \cong W \iff \dim V = \dim W$$

In particular, $\dim V = n \Rightarrow V \cong K^n$.

</Teorema>

<Insight>

**Why 8 axioms and not 7 or 9?** Each axiom covers a pathology. Without axiom 3 (identity), there is no origin. Without axiom 8 ($1 \cdot v = v$), a "space" could have $1 \cdot v = \mathbf{0}$ for all $v$ — technically consistent with the other 7, but useless. The 8 form the **minimum set** that guarantees useful behavior: every LI set extends to a basis, projections exist, dimension is well-defined.

</Insight>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

Imagine **boxes of Lego**. You have pieces you can **put together** (add) and you can **make copies** (multiply by number).

A **vector space** is like a **well-organized toy box**: everything you build with those pieces fits in the box. If you put two creations together, it still fits. If you make the same creation three times, it still fits.

The **basis** is the **smallest set of pieces** with which you can build everything. The **dimension** is **how many pieces are in that smallest set**.

A box of blocks that goes right and up has dimension 2 — you need two types of piece to go anywhere. A box that also goes forward has dimension 3.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

You've already seen **vectors in the plane** — arrows with direction and size. In the plane, any arrow is the sum of an arrow going right and one going up. The **basis** of the plane has 2 vectors; the **dimension** is 2.

In 3D space? The dimension is 3. You need 3 basis arrows.

The giant discovery was: **many things that don't look like arrows behave like arrows**. For example:

- Polynomials $ax^2 + bx + c$ add like vectors: $(x^2 + 2x + 1) + (3x^2 - x + 5) = 4x^2 + x + 6$.
- You can multiply by number: $2(x^2 + 1) = 2x^2 + 2$.

The space of polynomials of degree $\leq 2$ has dimension 3, with basis $\{1, x, x^2\}$. Each polynomial $ax^2 + bx + c$ is like the vector $(c, b, a)$ in 3D. Exactly the same.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old teen">

Vector space is the **most reusable concept in mathematics**. It started with geometric arrows and became an **abstract structure**: any set where you add consistently and scale consistently.

### Examples that are NOT "geometric vectors"

| Space | Operations | Dimension |
|---|---|---|
| $\mathbb{R}^n$ | $n$-tuples with usual addition and scaling | $n$ |
| $\mathcal{P}_n$ | polynomials degree $\leq n$, usual sum/scale | $n+1$ |
| $\mathcal{M}_{m \times n}$ | $m \times n$ matrices | $mn$ |
| Solutions of linear ODE of order $k$ | functions | $k$ |
| $C[a, b]$ | continuous functions | $\infty$ |

### How to check if something is a subspace

Given $V$ and $W \subseteq V$, three questions:
1. $\mathbf{0} \in W$? If not, it's not a subspace.
2. $u, v \in W \Rightarrow u + v \in W$?
3. $a \in \mathbb{R},\ v \in W \Rightarrow av \in W$?

**Example:** $W = \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2 : x + y = 0\}$ — is it a subspace? Yes: $(0,0) \in W$; if $x_1 + y_1 = 0$ and $x_2 + y_2 = 0$, then $(x_1+x_2) + (y_1+y_2) = 0$; and $a(x_1 + y_1) = 0$. Everything checks.

**Counterexample:** $W = \{(x, y) : x + y = 1\}$ — not a subspace because $(0,0) \notin W$.

<Insight>

**Recognizing vector space structure is key.** Once you know something is a vector space, you **get for free** all the theory: linear independence, basis, dimension, coordinates, projections. You don't need to reinvent for each application.

</Insight>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

Linear algebra is the **operational language** of modern engineering. Vector spaces appear in control, signals, ML, mechanics, and quantitative finance.

### Why axioms instead of "vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$"?

Because the **same apparatus works** in drastically different contexts:

- **Discrete signals**: $\ell^2(\mathbb{Z})$ — square-summable sequences. Discrete Fourier basis.
- **Continuous signals**: $L^2[0, T]$ — square-integrable functions. Orthonormal Fourier basis.
- **Quantum states**: $\mathbb{C}^n$ or separable Hilbert space.
- **Images**: $\mathbb{R}^{H \times W}$ — viewed as a vector in $\mathbb{R}^{HW}$.
- **ML embeddings**: $\mathbb{R}^d$ with $d \in \{128, 384, 1536, \ldots\}$ — every LLM operates in this space.

### Relevant subspaces in engineering

| Subspace | Meaning | Dimension |
|---|---|---|
| $\ker(A)$ | homogeneous solutions $Ax = 0$ | $n - \text{rank}(A)$ |
| $\text{im}(A)$ | reachable outputs | $\text{rank}(A)$ |
| $E_\lambda = \ker(A - \lambda I)$ | eigenspace for $\lambda$ | $\geq 1$ |
| Solutions of ODE of order $k$ | system modes | $k$ |

### Linear independence as redundancy test

In **regression**, linearly dependent columns in the design matrix $X$ generate multicollinearity, $X^T X$ singular, and least squares without a unique solution. Detection: rank of $X$, singular values near zero, VIF $> 10$.

### Dimension theorem (Grassmann formula)

For subspaces $U, W \subseteq V$:

$$\dim(U + W) = \dim U + \dim W - \dim(U \cap W)$$

Analogous to inclusion-exclusion principle for finite sets.

### Orthonormal basis: the computational ideal

If $\mathcal{B} = \{e_1, \ldots, e_n\}$ is orthonormal ($\langle e_i, e_j\rangle = \delta_{ij}$), the projection of $v$ is trivial: $v = \sum_i \langle v, e_i\rangle e_i$. That's why PCA, Fourier, and wavelets seek orthonormal bases.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

### Vector spaces as a category

The category $\mathbf{Vect}_K$ has vector spaces as objects and linear transformations as morphisms. It is **abelian** (kernels and cokernels exist and behave well), **monoidal** (tensor product $\otimes$) and, in finite dimension, **self-dual** ($V \cong V^{**}$ canonically).

### Infinite dimension: beware of Zorn

The existence of a basis (Hamel basis) via Zorn's Lemma is **nonconstructive** in general. In $\ell^\infty$, the Hamel basis exists but is uncomputable. In practice:

- **Schauder bases** (series convergence): in separable Banach spaces.
- **Orthonormal bases** ($L^2$ convergence): in separable Hilbert spaces — Fourier, Hermite, Legendre.
- **Frames**: overcomplete relaxation — useful in *compressed sensing* (Candès–Donoho).

### Common additional structure

- **Normed** $\Rightarrow$ Banach if complete. Completeness matters: in $C[0,1]$ with $L^2$ norm, Cauchy sequences don't always converge to continuous functions.
- **Inner product** $\Rightarrow$ Hilbert if complete. Riesz theorem: $V \cong V^*$ canonically.
- **Algebra**: bilinear product $V \times V \to V$ (e.g., matrices, polynomials).
- **Lie space**: antisymmetric product $[\cdot, \cdot]$ satisfying Jacobi.

### Advanced applications

- **RKHS** (*Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Spaces*): theoretical basis of SVM, *gaussian processes*, kernel methods.
- **Sobolev $H^k$**: natural space for PDEs (FEM converges in $H^1$).
- **Schwartz $\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})$**: tempered distributions — Fourier, PDEs, signals.
- **Bergman spaces**: holomorphic $L^2$ — quantitative complex analysis.

### Connection to Black-Scholes (Lesson 119)

The space of **admissible portfolios** in mathematical finance is a subspace of $L^2$-adapted processes — an infinite vector space. Dynamic replication (Black-Scholes) is projection onto the subspace generated by available hedges. Market completeness = the subspace generated by assets *is* the entire space.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practice — verification, bases, and Brazilian data">

### Practical algorithm: verify subspace

For any $W \subseteq V$, follow:

1. Find the **zero** of $V$. Check that $\mathbf{0} \in W$.
2. Take $u, v \in W$ generic and check $u + v \in W$.
3. Take $a \in K$ and $v \in W$ and check $av \in W$.

If any step fails, $W$ is not a subspace.

### Practical algorithm: find basis of a span

Given $v_1, \ldots, v_k \in \mathbb{R}^n$:

1. Form matrix $A$ with vectors as **rows**.
2. Row-reduce (Gaussian elimination).
3. The **nonzero rows** of the row-reduced form are a basis of the span.
4. The number of nonzero rows = $\dim(\text{span}\{v_1, \ldots, v_k\})$.

**Brazilian example:** portfolio of 3 Bovespa assets (PETR4, VALE3, BBAS3) with weekly returns generates vectors in $\mathbb{R}^{52}$ (52 weeks). The effective dimension of the span is the number of independent risk factors — typically 2 to 3 for large correlated corporations.

### When dimension surprises

| Object | Expected dimension | Actual dimension |
|---|---|---|
| $2 \times 2$ matrices | 4 | 4 |
| Symmetric $2 \times 2$ matrices | — | 3 |
| Antisymmetric $2 \times 2$ matrices | — | 1 |
| Solutions of $y'' + y = 0$ | — | 2 |
| Polynomials of degree $\leq 5$ | — | 6 |

## Worked examples

<Exemplo titulo="Verification of 8 axioms for polynomials (application)" numero="1">

**Problem:** Show that $\mathcal{P}_2 = \{a_0 + a_1 x + a_2 x^2 : a_i \in \mathbb{R}\}$ with usual polynomial addition and real scalar multiplication is a vector space over $\mathbb{R}$.

**Strategy:** Verify the 8 axioms directly for generic polynomials. Most inherit properties from the reals (field).

**Solution:**

Take $p = a_0 + a_1 x + a_2 x^2$ and $q = b_0 + b_1 x + b_2 x^2$ in $\mathcal{P}_2$, and $c, d \in \mathbb{R}$.

**Closure:** $p + q = (a_0+b_0) + (a_1+b_1)x + (a_2+b_2)x^2 \in \mathcal{P}_2$. $cp = ca_0 + ca_1 x + ca_2 x^2 \in \mathcal{P}_2$.

**Axiom 1 (assoc.):** $(p+q)+r = p+(q+r)$ — coefficients are real, associativity inherited.

**Axiom 2 (comut.):** $p+q = q+p$ — coefficients are real, commutativity inherited.

**Axiom 3 (identity):** $\mathbf{0} = 0 + 0x + 0x^2$. Clearly $p + \mathbf{0} = p$.

**Axiom 4 (inverse):** $-p = -a_0 - a_1 x - a_2 x^2 \in \mathcal{P}_2$ and $p + (-p) = \mathbf{0}$.

**Axiom 5:** $c(p+q) = c(a_0+b_0) + \ldots = cp + cq$. Distributivity of reals.

**Axiom 6:** $(c+d)p = (c+d)a_0 + \ldots = cp + dp$. Same.

**Axiom 7:** $c(dp) = c(da_i x^i) = (cd)a_i x^i$. Associativity of real multiplication.

**Axiom 8:** $1 \cdot p = 1 \cdot a_0 + 1 \cdot a_1 x + 1 \cdot a_2 x^2 = p$.

All 8 axioms verified. Thus $\mathcal{P}_2$ is an $\mathbb{R}$-vector space.

**Verification:** The canonical basis $\{1, x, x^2\}$ is LI (none is a linear combination of the others) and generates $\mathcal{P}_2$. Thus $\dim \mathcal{P}_2 = 3$, consistent with 3 free coefficients.

**Source.** [Beezer — *A First Course in Linear Algebra*, §VS Example VS.EXS](http://linear.ups.edu/fcla/section-VS.html) — GNU FDL.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Verify subspace: symmetric matrices (intermediate)" numero="2">

**Problem:** Show that $W = \{A \in \mathcal{M}_{2 \times 2} : A^T = A\}$ ($2\times2$ symmetric matrices) is a subspace of $\mathcal{M}_{2\times2}$.

**Strategy:** Check the 3 subspace criteria: zero, closure under addition, and closure under scalars.

**Solution:**

**Zero:** The null matrix satisfies $\mathbf{0}^T = \mathbf{0}$, hence $\mathbf{0} \in W$.

**Closure under addition:** Let $A, B \in W$, i.e., $A^T = A$ and $B^T = B$. Then:
$$(A + B)^T = A^T + B^T = A + B.$$
Thus $A + B \in W$.

**Closure under scalar:** Let $c \in \mathbb{R}$ and $A \in W$. Then:
$$(cA)^T = c A^T = c A.$$
Thus $cA \in W$.

All 3 criteria verified: $W$ is a subspace.

**Verification:** A basis of $W$ is $\left\{\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\right\}$ — three LI matrices, hence $\dim W = 3$. Makes sense: symmetry imposes $a_{12} = a_{21}$, leaving 3 free entries ($a_{11}, a_{12}, a_{22}$).

**Source.** [Hefferon — *Linear Algebra*, Chapter 2 §2.I Exercise 2.I.1.17](https://hefferon.net/linearalgebra/) — CC-BY-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Find basis and dimension of a span (intermediate)" numero="3">

**Problem:** Find a basis and dimension of the subspace $W = \text{span}\{v_1, v_2, v_3\}$ where $v_1 = (1, 2, 3)$, $v_2 = (2, 4, 6)$, $v_3 = (1, 0, 1)$.

**Strategy:** Form matrix with vectors as rows and row-reduce. Nonzero rows of the reduced form are a basis of the span.

**Solution:**

$$\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 2 & 4 & 6 \\ 1 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \xrightarrow{L_2 - 2L_1} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \xrightarrow{L_3 - L_1} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & -2 & -2 \end{pmatrix} \xrightarrow{\text{swap } L_2, L_3} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 0 & -2 & -2 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$$

Two nonzero rows. A basis of $W$:

$$\mathcal{B} = \{(1, 2, 3),\ (0, -2, -2)\} \quad \text{or equivalently} \quad \{(1, 2, 3),\ (0, 1, 1)\}$$

Thus $\dim W = 2$.

**Verification:** $v_2 = 2v_1$ (dependent — confirms it doesn't contribute). $v_3 = v_1 - (0,2,2)/2 \cdot 1 + \ldots$ — can be written as a combination of $(1,2,3)$ and $(0,1,1)$. The span is a plane in $\mathbb{R}^3$.

**Source.** [Beezer — *A First Course in Linear Algebra*, §LI Example LI.LIHS](http://linear.ups.edu/fcla/section-LI.html) — GNU FDL.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Coordinates in a noncanonical basis (advanced)" numero="4">

**Problem:** Let $\mathcal{B} = \{b_1, b_2\} = \{(1, 1), (1, -1)\}$ be a basis of $\mathbb{R}^2$. Find the coordinates of vector $v = (5, 1)$ in this basis.

**Strategy:** Solve the system $c_1 b_1 + c_2 b_2 = v$ for the scalars $c_1, c_2$ — the coordinates of $v$ in basis $\mathcal{B}$.

**Solution:**

$$c_1 \begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix} + c_2 \begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}5\\1\end{pmatrix}$$

System:
$$\begin{cases} c_1 + c_2 = 5 \\ c_1 - c_2 = 1 \end{cases}$$

Adding: $2c_1 = 6 \Rightarrow c_1 = 3$. Subtracting: $2c_2 = 4 \Rightarrow c_2 = 2$.

The coordinates of $v$ in basis $\mathcal{B}$ are $[v]_\mathcal{B} = (3, 2)$.

**Verification:** $3(1,1) + 2(1,-1) = (3,3) + (2,-2) = (5, 1)$. Correct.

**Note:** The vector $v = (5,1)$ is the same geometric object; what changes are its **numeric representations** depending on the chosen basis. Change of basis only changes the coordinates, not the vector.

**Source.** [Axler — *Linear Algebra Done Right*, §2B](https://linear.axler.net/) — CC-BY-NC.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Proof: 0 cdot v = 0 using the axioms (proof)" numero="5">

**Problem:** Using only the 8 vector space axioms, prove that $0 \cdot v = \mathbf{0}$ for all $v \in V$ (where $0$ is the scalar zero and $\mathbf{0}$ is the additive identity).

**Strategy:** The scalar $0$ does not appear directly in the axioms. The strategy is to use $0 = 0 + 0$ (zero property in the scalars) and axiom 6 (scalar distributivity), then cancel using the inverse.

**Solution:**

Let $w = 0 \cdot v$. We want to show $w = \mathbf{0}$.

**Step 1.** In field $K$, we have $0 = 0 + 0$ (zero is additive identity in $K$).

**Step 2.** By axiom 6 (scalar distributivity):
$$0 \cdot v = (0 + 0) \cdot v = 0 \cdot v + 0 \cdot v = w + w.$$

**Step 3.** Thus $w = w + w$.

**Step 4.** Adding $(-w)$ (inverse of $w$, axiom 4) to both sides:
$$w + (-w) = (w + w) + (-w) = w + (w + (-w)) \quad \text{(axiom 1)}$$
$$\mathbf{0} = w + \mathbf{0} \quad \text{(axiom 4 and 3)}$$
$$\mathbf{0} = w.$$

Therefore $0 \cdot v = \mathbf{0}$. $\blacksquare$

**Verification:** The result is a theorem, not an axiom — it follows from the 8. The proof uses: $K$ is a ring, axioms 1 (assoc.), 3 (identity), 4 (inverse), 6 (scalar distrib.). No axiom was used unnecessarily.

**Source.** [Axler — *Linear Algebra Done Right*, §1A, Proposition 1.2](https://linear.axler.net/) — CC-BY-NC.

</Exemplo>
