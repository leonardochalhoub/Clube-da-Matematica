<Porta nivel="formal" titulo="Axiomatic definition">

### The 8 axioms of vector space

<Definicao titulo="K-vector space">

Let $K$ be a field (typically $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$). A **$K$-vector space** is a nonempty set $V$ equipped with two operations:

- **addition**: $+ : V \times V \to V$
- **scalar multiplication**: $\cdot : K \times V \to V$

satisfying, for all $u, v, w \in V$ and $a, b \in K$:

| # | Axiom | Formula |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Associativity | $(u + v) + w = u + (v + w)$ |
| 2 | Commutativity | $u + v = v + u$ |
| 3 | Additive identity | $\exists\, \mathbf{0} \in V : v + \mathbf{0} = v$ |
| 4 | Additive inverse | $\exists\, (-v) \in V : v + (-v) = \mathbf{0}$ |
| 5 | Distributivity (vectors) | $a(u + v) = au + av$ |
| 6 | Distributivity (scalars) | $(a + b)v = av + bv$ |
| 7 | Compatibility | $a(bv) = (ab)v$ |
| 8 | Scalar identity | $1 \cdot v = v$ |

</Definicao>

> "We will use the term *field* to refer to either $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$. Elements of a field are called *scalars*. A *vector space* is a set $V$ along with an addition on $V$ and a scalar multiplication on $V$ such that the following properties hold..." — [Axler — *Linear Algebra Done Right*, §1A](https://linear.axler.net/)

<Definicao titulo="Vector subspace">

$W \subseteq V$ is a **subspace** of $V$ if:
1. $\mathbf{0} \in W$
2. $u, v \in W \Rightarrow u + v \in W$ (closed under addition)
3. $a \in K,\ v \in W \Rightarrow av \in W$ (closed under scalar multiplication)

**Shortcut**: it suffices to verify $\mathbf{0} \in W$ and closure under **linear combination**: $au + bv \in W$ for all $a, b \in K$, $u, v \in W$.

</Definicao>

> "A subset $W$ of $V$ is called a *subspace* of $V$ if $W$ is a vector space under the addition and scalar multiplication defined on $V$." — [Beezer — *A First Course in Linear Algebra*, §VS](http://linear.pugetsound.edu/fcla/section-VS.html)

<Definicao titulo="Linear combination, span, linear independence, basis">

Given $S = \{v_1, \ldots, v_k\} \subseteq V$:

- **Linear combination**: $c_1 v_1 + \cdots + c_k v_k$ with $c_i \in K$.
- **Span** (generated space): $\text{span}(S) = \{c_1 v_1 + \cdots + c_k v_k : c_i \in K\}$ — the smallest subspace containing $S$.
- **Linearly independent (LI)**: $c_1 v_1 + \cdots + c_k v_k = \mathbf{0} \Rightarrow c_1 = \cdots = c_k = 0$.
- **Basis**: $\mathcal{B} \subseteq V$ that is LI and $\text{span}(\mathcal{B}) = V$.
- **Dimension**: $\dim V = |\mathcal{B}|$ — well-defined by the theorem below.

</Definicao>

<Teorema titulo="Invariance of dimension (Steinitz)">

If $V$ has a basis with $n$ elements, then **every** basis of $V$ has exactly $n$ elements.

**Proof sketch (for finite dim):** Suppose $\mathcal{B} = \{b_1, \ldots, b_n\}$ and $\mathcal{C} = \{c_1, \ldots, c_m\}$ are both bases. Since $\mathcal{B}$ spans $V$, each $c_j$ is a linear combination of $\mathcal{B}$. If $m > n$, the system of $m$ equations in $n$ scalars would have a nontrivial solution, contradicting LI of $\mathcal{C}$. Thus $m \leq n$. Symmetry gives $n \leq m$, therefore $m = n$. $\blacksquare$

</Teorema>

<Teorema titulo="Classification in finite dimension">

Two $K$-vector spaces of finite dimension are isomorphic if and only if they have the same dimension.

$$V \cong W \iff \dim V = \dim W$$

In particular, $\dim V = n \Rightarrow V \cong K^n$.

</Teorema>

<Insight>

**Why exactly 8 axioms and not 7 or 9?** Each axiom covers a pathology. Without axiom 3 (additive identity), there is no origin. Without axiom 8 ($1 \cdot v = v$), a "space" could have $1 \cdot v = \mathbf{0}$ for all $v$ — technically consistent with the other 7, but useless. The 8 form the **minimal set** that ensures useful behavior: every LI set extends to a basis, projections exist, dimension is well-defined.

</Insight>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

Imagine **Lego building blocks**. You have pieces that you can **put together** (add) and **make copies** (multiply by number).

A **vector space** is like a **well-organized toy box**: everything you build with those pieces fits in the box. If you combine two creations, it still fits. If you make the same creation three times, it still fits.

The **basis** is the **smallest set of pieces** with which you can build everything. The **dimension** is **how many pieces are in that smallest set**.

A box of blocks that goes right and up has dimension 2 — you need two types of piece to go anywhere. A box that also goes forward has dimension 3.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

You have already seen **vectors in a plane** — little arrows with direction and size. In a plane, any arrow is the sum of one arrow going right and another going up. The **basis** of a plane has 2 vectors; the **dimension** is 2.

In 3D space? The dimension is 3. You need 3 basis arrows.

The giant discovery was: **many things that don't look like arrows behave like arrows**. For example:

- Polynomials $ax^2 + bx + c$ add like vectors: $(x^2 + 2x + 1) + (3x^2 - x + 5) = 4x^2 + x + 6$.
- You can multiply by number: $2(x^2 + 1) = 2x^2 + 2$.

The space of polynomials of degree $\leq 2$ has dimension 3, with basis $\{1, x, x^2\}$. Each polynomial $ax^2 + bx + c$ is like the vector $(c, b, a)$ in 3D. Exactly the same.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old">

Vector space is the **most reusable concept in mathematics**. It came from geometric arrows and became an **abstract structure**: any set where you add consistently and scale consistently.

### Examples that are NOT "geometric vectors"

| Space | Operations | Dimension |
|---|---|---|
| $\mathbb{R}^n$ | $n$-tuples with usual addition and scaling | $n$ |
| $\mathcal{P}_n$ | polynomials degree $\leq n$, usual addition/scaling | $n+1$ |
| $\mathcal{M}_{m \times n}$ | $m \times n$ matrices | $mn$ |
| Solutions of linear ODE order $k$ | functions | $k$ |
| $C[a, b]$ | continuous functions | $\infty$ |

### How to check if something is a subspace

Given $V$ and $W \subseteq V$, three questions:
1. $\mathbf{0} \in W$? If not, not a subspace.
2. $u, v \in W \Rightarrow u + v \in W$?
3. $a \in \mathbb{R},\ v \in W \Rightarrow av \in W$?

**Example:** $W = \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2 : x + y = 0\}$ — is it a subspace? Yes: $(0,0) \in W$; if $x_1 + y_1 = 0$ and $x_2 + y_2 = 0$, then $(x_1+x_2) + (y_1+y_2) = 0$; and $a(x_1 + y_1) = 0$. Everything checks out.

**Counterexample:** $W = \{(x, y) : x + y = 1\}$ — not a subspace because $(0,0) \notin W$.

<Insight>

**Recognizing vector space structure is the key.** Once you know something is a vector space, you **get for free** all the theory: linear independence, basis, dimension, coordinates, projections. No need to reinvent for each application.

</Insight>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

Linear algebra is the **operational language** of modern engineering. Vector spaces appear in control, signals, ML, mechanics, and quantitative finance.

### Why axioms instead of "vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$"?

Because the **same machinery works** in drastically different contexts:

- **Discrete signals**: $\ell^2(\mathbb{Z})$ — square-summable sequences. Discrete Fourier basis.
- **Continuous signals**: $L^2[0, T]$ — square-integrable functions. Orthonormal Fourier basis.
- **Quantum states**: $\mathbb{C}^n$ or separable Hilbert space.
- **Images**: $\mathbb{R}^{H \times W}$ — viewed as a vector in $\mathbb{R}^{HW}$.
- **ML embeddings**: $\mathbb{R}^d$ with $d \in \{128, 384, 1536, \ldots\}$ — every LLM operates in this space.

### Relevant subspaces in engineering

| Subspace | Meaning | Dimension |
|---|---|---|
| $\ker(A)$ | solutions to $Ax = 0$ | $n - \text{rank}(A)$ |
| $\text{im}(A)$ | achievable outputs | $\text{rank}(A)$ |
| $E_\lambda = \ker(A - \lambda I)$ | eigenspace for $\lambda$ | $\geq 1$ |
| Solutions of ODE order $k$ | system modes | $k$ |

### Linear independence as redundancy test

In **regression**, linearly dependent columns in design matrix $X$ create multicollinearity, singular $X^T X$, and nonunique least-squares solution. Detection: rank of $X$, singular values close to zero, VIF $> 10$.

### Dimension theorem (Grassmann formula)

For subspaces $U, W \subseteq V$:

$$\dim(U + W) = \dim U + \dim W - \dim(U \cap W)$$

Analogous to inclusion-exclusion principle for finite sets.

### Orthonormal basis: the computational ideal

If $\mathcal{B} = \{e_1, \ldots, e_n\}$ is orthonormal ($\langle e_i, e_j\rangle = \delta_{ij}$), projection of $v$ is trivial: $v = \sum_i \langle v, e_i\rangle e_i$. That is why PCA, Fourier, and wavelets seek orthonormal bases.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

### Vector spaces as a category

The category $\mathbf{Vect}_K$ has vector spaces as objects and linear transformations as morphisms. It is **abelian** (kernels and cokernels exist and behave well), **monoidal** (tensor product $\otimes$), and in finite dimension, **self-dual** ($V \cong V^{**}$ canonically).

### Infinite dimension: beware of Zorn

Existence of a basis (Hamel basis) via Zorn's Lemma is **non-constructive** in general. In $\ell^\infty$, the Hamel basis exists but is uncomputable. In practice:

- **Schauder bases** (series convergence): in separable Banach spaces.
- **Orthonormal bases** ($L^2$ convergence): in separable Hilbert spaces — Fourier, Hermite, Legendre.
- **Frames**: overcomplete relaxation — useful in *compressed sensing* (Candès–Donoho).

### Common additional structure

- **Normed** $\Rightarrow$ Banach if complete. Completeness matters: in $C[0,1]$ with $L^2$ norm, Cauchy sequences need not converge to a continuous function.
- **Inner product** $\Rightarrow$ Hilbert if complete. Riesz theorem: $V \cong V^*$ canonically.
- **Algebra**: bilinear product $V \times V \to V$ (e.g. matrices, polynomials).
- **Lie space**: antisymmetric product $[\cdot, \cdot]$ satisfying Jacobi.

### Advanced applications

- **RKHS** (*Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Spaces*): theoretical foundation of SVM, *gaussian processes*, kernel methods.
- **Sobolev $H^k$**: natural space for PDEs (FEM converges in $H^1$).
- **Schwartz $\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})$**: tempered distributions — Fourier, PDEs, signals.
- **Bergman spaces**: holomorphic $L^2$ — quantitative complex analysis.

### Connection to Black-Scholes (Lesson 119)

The space of **admissible portfolios** in mathematical finance is a subspace of $L^2$-adapted processes — infinite-dimensional vector space. Dynamic replication (Black-Scholes) is projection onto the subspace spanned by available hedges. Market completeness = the subspace spanned by assets *is* the whole space.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practice — verification, bases, and Brazilian data">

### Practical algorithm: verify a subspace

For any $W \subseteq V$, follow:

1. Find the **zero** of $V$. Verify $\mathbf{0} \in W$.
2. Take generic $u, v \in W$ and verify $u + v \in W$.
3. Take $a \in K$ and $v \in W$ and verify $av \in W$.

If any step fails, $W$ is not a subspace.

### Practical algorithm: find a basis of a span

Given $v_1, \ldots, v_k \in \mathbb{R}^n$:

1. Form matrix $A$ with vectors as **rows**.
2. Row-reduce (Gaussian elimination).
3. The **nonzero rows** in reduced form are a basis of the span.
4. Number of nonzero rows = $\dim(\text{span}\{v_1, \ldots, v_k\})$.

**Brazilian example:** portfolio of 3 Bovespa assets (PETR4, VALE3, BBAS3) with weekly returns generates vectors in $\mathbb{R}^{52}$ (52 weeks). The effective dimension of the span is the number of independent risk factors — typically 2–3 for large correlated corporations.

### When dimension surprises

| Object | Expected dimension | Actual dimension |
|---|---|---|
| $2 \times 2$ matrices | 4 | 4 |
| $2 \times 2$ symmetric matrices | — | 3 |
| $2 \times 2$ antisymmetric matrices | — | 1 |
| Solutions of $y'' + y = 0$ | — | 2 |
| Polynomials degree $\leq 5$ | — | 6 |

## Worked examples

<Exemplo titulo="Verify the 8 axioms for polynomials (application)" numero="1">

**Problem:** Show that $\mathcal{P}_2 = \{a_0 + a_1 x + a_2 x^2 : a_i \in \mathbb{R}\}$ with usual polynomial addition and real scalar multiplication is a vector space over $\mathbb{R}$.

**Strategy:** Verify the 8 axioms directly for generic polynomials. Most are inherited from properties of the reals (a field).

**Solution:**

Take $p = a_0 + a_1 x + a_2 x^2$ and $q = b_0 + b_1 x + b_2 x^2$ in $\mathcal{P}_2$, and $c, d \in \mathbb{R}$.

**Closure:** $p + q = (a_0+b_0) + (a_1+b_1)x + (a_2+b_2)x^2 \in \mathcal{P}_2$. $cp = ca_0 + ca_1 x + ca_2 x^2 \in \mathcal{P}_2$.

**Axiom 1 (assoc.):** $(p+q)+r = p+(q+r)$ — coefficients are real, associativity inherited.

**Axiom 2 (comm.):** $p+q = q+p$ — coefficients are real, commutativity inherited.

**Axiom 3 (identity):** $\mathbf{0} = 0 + 0x + 0x^2$. Clearly $p + \mathbf{0} = p$.

**Axiom 4 (inverse):** $-p = -a_0 - a_1 x - a_2 x^2 \in \mathcal{P}_2$ and $p + (-p) = \mathbf{0}$.

**Axiom 5:** $c(p+q) = c(a_0+b_0) + \ldots = cp + cq$. Distributivity of the reals.

**Axiom 6:** $(c+d)p = (c+d)a_0 + \ldots = cp + dp$. Same.

**Axiom 7:** $c(dp) = c(da_i x^i) = (cd)a_i x^i$. Associativity of real multiplication.

**Axiom 8:** $1 \cdot p = 1 \cdot a_0 + 1 \cdot a_1 x + 1 \cdot a_2 x^2 = p$.

All 8 axioms verified. Therefore $\mathcal{P}_2$ is an $\mathbb{R}$-vector space.

**Verification:** The canonical basis $\{1, x, x^2\}$ is LI (none is a linear combination of the others) and spans $\mathcal{P}_2$. Therefore $\dim \mathcal{P}_2 = 3$, consistent with 3 free coefficients.

**Source.** [Beezer — *A First Course in Linear Algebra*, §VS Example VS.EXS](http://linear.pugetsound.edu/fcla/section-VS.html) — GNU FDL.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Verify a subspace: symmetric matrices (intermediate)" numero="2">

**Problem:** Show that $W = \{A \in \mathcal{M}_{2 \times 2} : A^T = A\}$ (symmetric $2 \times 2$ matrices) is a subspace of $\mathcal{M}_{2 \times 2}$.

**Strategy:** Verify 3 subspace criteria: zero, closure under addition, and closure under scaling.

**Solution:**

**Zero:** The zero matrix satisfies $\mathbf{0}^T = \mathbf{0}$, so $\mathbf{0} \in W$.

**Closure under addition:** Let $A, B \in W$, i.e., $A^T = A$ and $B^T = B$. Then:
$$(A + B)^T = A^T + B^T = A + B.$$
Thus $A + B \in W$.

**Closure under scaling:** Let $c \in \mathbb{R}$ and $A \in W$. Then:
$$(cA)^T = c A^T = c A.$$
Thus $cA \in W$.

All 3 criteria verified: $W$ is a subspace.

**Verification:** A basis of $W$ is <Eq>{`\\left\\{\\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\\\0&0\\end{pmatrix}, \\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\\\1&0\\end{pmatrix}, \\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\\\0&1\\end{pmatrix}\\right\\}`}</Eq> — three LI matrices, so $\dim W = 3$. Makes sense: symmetry imposes $a_{12} = a_{21}$, leaving 3 free entries ($a_{11}, a_{12}, a_{22}$).

**Source.** [Hefferon — *Linear Algebra*, Chapter 2 §2.I Exercise 2.I.1.17](https://hefferon.net/linearalgebra/) — CC-BY-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Find basis and dimension of a span (intermediate)" numero="3">

**Problem:** Find a basis and the dimension of the subspace $W = \text{span}\{v_1, v_2, v_3\}$ where $v_1 = (1, 2, 3)$, $v_2 = (2, 4, 6)$, $v_3 = (1, 0, 1)$.

**Strategy:** Form a matrix with vectors as rows and reduce. Nonzero rows in reduced form are a basis of the span.

**Solution:**

$<Eq>{`\\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\\\ 2 & 4 & 6 \\\\ 1 & 0 & 1 \\end{pmatrix} \\xrightarrow{L_2 - 2L_1} \\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\\\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\\\ 1 & 0 & 1 \\end{pmatrix} \\xrightarrow{L_3 - L_1} \\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\\\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\\\ 0 & -2 & -2 \\end{pmatrix} \\xrightarrow{\\text{swap } L_2, L_3} \\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\\\ 0 & -2 & -2 \\\\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>$

Two nonzero rows. A basis of $W$:

$$\mathcal{B} = \{(1, 2, 3),\ (0, -2, -2)\} \quad \text{or equivalently} \quad \{(1, 2, 3),\ (0, 1, 1)\}$$

Therefore $\dim W = 2$.

**Verification:** $v_2 = 2v_1$ (dependent — confirms it doesn't contribute). $v_3 = v_1 - (0,2,2)/2 \cdot 1 + \ldots$ — can be written as a linear combination of $(1,2,3)$ and $(0,1,1)$. The span is a plane in $\mathbb{R}^3$.

**Source.** [Beezer — *A First Course in Linear Algebra*, §LI Example LI.LIHS](http://linear.pugetsound.edu/fcla/section-LI.html) — GNU FDL.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Coordinates in a noncanonical basis (advanced)" numero="4">

**Problem:** Let $\mathcal{B} = \{b_1, b_2\} = \{(1, 1), (1, -1)\}$ be a basis of $\mathbb{R}^2$. Find the coordinates of the vector $v = (5, 1)$ in this basis.

**Strategy:** Solve the system $c_1 b_1 + c_2 b_2 = v$ for the scalars $c_1, c_2$ — the coordinates of $v$ in basis $\mathcal{B}$.

**Solution:**

$<Eq>{`c_1 \\begin{pmatrix}1\\\\1\\end{pmatrix} + c_2 \\begin{pmatrix}1\\\\-1\\end{pmatrix} = \\begin{pmatrix}5\\\\1\\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>$

System:
$<Eq>{`c_1 + c_2 = 5`}</Eq>, <Eq>{`c_1 - c_2 = 1`}</Eq>$

Adding: $2c_1 = 6 \Rightarrow c_1 = 3$. Subtracting: $2c_2 = 4 \Rightarrow c_2 = 2$.

The coordinates of $v$ in basis $\mathcal{B}$ are $[v]_\mathcal{B} = (3, 2)$.

**Verification:** $3(1,1) + 2(1,-1) = (3,3) + (2,-2) = (5, 1)$. Correct.

**Note:** the vector $v = (5,1)$ is the same geometric object; what changes are its **numerical representations** depending on the basis chosen. Change of basis only changes coordinates, not the vector.

**Source.** [Axler — *Linear Algebra Done Right*, §2B](https://linear.axler.net/) — CC-BY-NC.

<Exemplo titulo="Proof: 0 times v equals zero using axioms (proof)" numero="5">

**Problem:** Using only the 8 axioms of a vector space, show that $0 \cdot v = \mathbf{0}$ for all $v \in V$ (where $0$ is the scalar zero and $\mathbf{0}$ is the additive identity vector).

**Strategy:** The scalar $0$ does not appear directly in the axioms. The strategy is to use $0 = 0 + 0$ (a property of scalars) and axiom 6 (scalar distributivity), then cancel using the inverse.

**Solution:**

Let $w = 0 \cdot v$. We want to show $w = \mathbf{0}$.

**Step 1.** In the field $K$, we have $0 = 0 + 0$ (zero is the additive identity in $K$).

**Step 2.** By axiom 6 (scalar distributivity):
$$0 \cdot v = (0 + 0) \cdot v = 0 \cdot v + 0 \cdot v = w + w.$$

**Step 3.** Therefore $w = w + w$.

**Step 4.** Adding $(-w)$ (the inverse of $w$, axiom 4) to both sides:
$$w + (-w) = (w + w) + (-w) = w + (w + (-w)) \quad \text{(axiom 1)}$$
$$\mathbf{0} = w + \mathbf{0} \quad \text{(axiom 4 and 3)}$$
$$\mathbf{0} = w.$$

Therefore $0 \cdot v = \mathbf{0}$. $\blacksquare$

**Verification:** The result is a theorem, not an axiom — it follows from the 8. The proof uses: $K$ is a field, axioms 1 (assoc.), 3 (identity), 4 (inverse), 6 (scalar distrib.). No axiom was used unnecessarily.

**Source.** [Axler — *Linear Algebra Done Right*, §1A, Proposition 1.2](https://linear.axler.net/) — CC-BY-NC.

</Exemplo>
