### Eigenvalues and eigenvectors

<Definicao titulo="Eigenvector and eigenvalue">

Let $A$ be an $n \times n$ matrix (or linear operator $T: V \to V$). A nonzero vector $\vec{v} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is an **eigenvector** of $A$ if there exists a scalar $\lambda$ such that

$$A\vec{v} = \lambda\,\vec{v}.$$

The scalar $\lambda$ is the **eigenvalue** corresponding to $\vec{v}$.

> "A scalar $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue of a linear transformation $T: V \to V$ if there exists a nonzero vector $\vec{v}$ such that $T(\vec{v}) = \lambda\vec{v}$. The vector $\vec{v}$ is then called an eigenvector corresponding to the eigenvalue $\lambda$." — [Beezer, A First Course in Linear Algebra, §EE, Definition EE](http://linear.ups.edu/download/fcla-electric-2.00.pdf)

</Definicao>

### Characteristic equation

<Definicao titulo="Characteristic polynomial">

The condition $A\vec{v} = \lambda\vec{v}$ with $\vec{v} \neq \vec{0}$ is equivalent to

$$(A - \lambda I)\vec{v} = \vec{0}$$

having a nontrivial solution, which occurs if and only if $A - \lambda I$ is singular:

<Equation explicacao="Characteristic equation: the roots of p_A(λ) are exactly the eigenvalues of A." latex="p_A(\lambda) = \det(A - \lambda I) = 0." />

$p_A$ is the **characteristic polynomial** of $A$, of degree $n$ in $\lambda$. Over $\mathbb{C}$, it has exactly $n$ roots (with multiplicity).

> "The characteristic polynomial of $A$ is $p_A(x) = \det(A - xI_n)$. The eigenvalues of $A$ are exactly the roots of $p_A$." — [Beezer, A First Course in Linear Algebra, §EE, Theorem EMRCP](http://linear.ups.edu/download/fcla-electric-2.00.pdf)

</Definicao>

### Eigenspace and multiplicities

<Definicao titulo="Eigenspace and multiplicities">

For an eigenvalue $\lambda$ of $A$:

- **Eigenspace**: $E_\lambda = \ker(A - \lambda I)$ — subspace of all eigenvectors associated with $\lambda$ (plus the zero vector).
- **Geometric multiplicity**: $m_g(\lambda) = \dim E_\lambda \geq 1$.
- **Algebraic multiplicity**: $m_a(\lambda)$ = order of the root $\lambda$ in $p_A(\lambda)$.

We always have $1 \leq m_g(\lambda) \leq m_a(\lambda)$.

</Definicao>

### Fundamental properties

<Teorema titulo="Properties of the spectrum">

Let $A$ be an $n \times n$ matrix with eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_n$ (with algebraic multiplicity):

1. $\det A = \lambda_1 \cdot \lambda_2 \cdots \lambda_n$
2. $\operatorname{tr}(A) = \lambda_1 + \lambda_2 + \cdots + \lambda_n$
3. $A$ invertible $\iff 0 \notin \sigma(A)$
4. Eigenvectors of **distinct** eigenvalues are linearly independent
5. $A$ similar to $B$ $\Rightarrow$ $p_A = p_B$ (same spectrum)
6. $\sigma(A^k) = \{\lambda^k : \lambda \in \sigma(A)\}$

</Teorema>

<Insight>

The condition $\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$ is equivalent to saying that the system $(A - \lambda I)\vec{v} = \vec{0}$ has infinitely many solutions — that is, the kernel of $A - \lambda I$ is nontrivial. This is why eigenvectors are never unique: every nonzero scalar multiple of an eigenvector is also an eigenvector.

</Insight>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

Imagine a **magic mirror** that distorts everything. When you look from the side, your image becomes twisted and tilted.

But there are **certain special directions** where the mirror does not tilt anything — it only makes you bigger or smaller, but in the same direction. These special directions are the **eigenvectors**. How much bigger or smaller you become is the **eigenvalue**.

If the eigenvalue is 2, you become twice as big, but still pointing in the same direction. If it is negative, you flip to the opposite side.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

Every matrix (every linear transformation) has **favorite directions**. If you pull a vector in that direction, it **only gets bigger or smaller**, but does not rotate.

These directions are the **eigenvectors**. The factor that multiplies is the **eigenvalue**.

Simple example: the matrix that stretches $x$ by 3 and $y$ by 2 has two obvious eigenvectors — the $x$-axis (with eigenvalue 3) and the $y$-axis (with eigenvalue 2). You can verify: if $\vec{v} = (1, 0)$, then $A\vec{v} = (3, 0) = 3 \cdot (1, 0)$.

But a pure rotation (that rotates everything) has **no real eigenvectors** — every direction changes. But it has complex eigenvectors, which is how mathematics handles it.

To find eigenvalues: set up the polynomial $\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$ and solve.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old teen">

Eigenvalues are **the most revealing property** of a matrix. They tell you what the transformation "does" repeatedly — much more than the individual numbers in the matrix.

### Computation recipe

1. Set up $A - \lambda I$ (subtract $\lambda$ from the diagonal).
2. Compute $\det(A - \lambda I)$ — you obtain a polynomial in $\lambda$.
3. Set it equal to zero. The roots are the **eigenvalues**.
4. For each $\lambda$, solve $(A - \lambda I)\vec{v} = \vec{0}$ — the nonzero solutions are the **eigenvectors**.

### Direct example

<Eq>{`A = \\begin{pmatrix} 4 & 1 \\\\ 2 & 3 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>

$\det(A - \lambda I) = (4 - \lambda)(3 - \lambda) - 2 = \lambda^2 - 7\lambda + 10 = (\lambda - 5)(\lambda - 2)$

Eigenvalues: $\lambda_1 = 5$, $\lambda_2 = 2$.

For $\lambda = 5$: <Eq>{`(A - 5I)\\vec{v} = \\begin{pmatrix} -1 & 1 \\\\ 2 & -2 \\end{pmatrix}\\vec{v} = \\vec{0}`}</Eq> gives $\vec{v} = (1, 1)$.

For $\lambda = 2$: similarly, $\vec{v} = (1, -2)$.

### Why does it matter?

- $A^k$ on the eigenvector $\vec{v}$: $A^k\vec{v} = \lambda^k\vec{v}$. Exponential growth by the eigenvalue.
- Matrix power: the eigenvalue of largest magnitude dominates the long term (basis of PageRank).
- **Stability** of the system $\dot{x} = Ax$: depends on $\operatorname{Re}(\lambda) < 0$ for all eigenvalues.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

Eigenvalues are the **computational DNA** of a matrix. In engineering, every linear system is characterized by its eigenvalues.

### Canonical applications

| Domain | Appearance |
|---|---|
| Structural mechanics | Natural vibration modes — eigenvalues of $K M^{-1}$ (stiffness/mass) |
| Automatic control | System poles — eigenvalues of $A$ in $\dot{x} = Ax$; stability $\iff \operatorname{Re}(\lambda) < 0$ |
| Quantum mechanics | Energies = eigenvalues of the Hamiltonian $\hat{H}$ |
| PCA / Statistics | Principal components = eigenvectors of the covariance |
| Graphs and networks | Spectrum of the Laplacian $L = D - W$ reveals connectivity |
| PageRank | Stationary distribution = eigenvector of $\lambda = 1$ |
| Finance (Lesson 119) | Risk factors = principal eigenvectors of $\Sigma$ |

### Numerical computation

In production, you **never** use $\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$ directly (numerically unstable for large $n$). Standard methods:

- **Power iteration**: $\vec{v}_{k+1} = A\vec{v}_k / \|A\vec{v}_k\|$ — converges to the dominant eigenvector. Basis of original PageRank.
- **QR algorithm**: $A_k = Q_k R_k$, $A_{k+1} = R_k Q_k$ — converges to triangular Schur form. LAPACK standard.
- **Lanczos**: for large sparse symmetric matrices (Krylov subspaces).

### Condition number

For $A$ positive definite, the ratio $\kappa(A) = \lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}$ is the **condition number**. If $\kappa(A) \gg 1$, the system $A\vec{x} = \vec{b}$ is ill-conditioned — small perturbations in $\vec{b}$ cause large variations in $\vec{x}$.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

### Spectrum in infinite dimension

In Hilbert spaces, $\sigma(T)$ decomposes into:

- **Point spectrum** $\sigma_p$: classical eigenvalues ($T - \lambda I$ not injective).
- **Continuous spectrum** $\sigma_c$: $T - \lambda I$ injective but image not closed.
- **Residual spectrum** $\sigma_r$: $T - \lambda I$ injective, image closed but not dense.

Compact operators on Hilbert spaces have enumerable spectrum with unique accumulation point at $0$ — Fredholm alternative. This founds the spectral analysis of PDEs (eigenfunctions of the Laplacian).

### Matrix functions via spectrum

If $A = PDP^{-1}$ diagonalizable: $f(A) = Pf(D)P^{-1}$ with $f(D) = \operatorname{diag}(f(\lambda_i))$. Thus:

$$e^{At} = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{t^k A^k}{k!}$$

solution of $\dot{x} = Ax$. For the general case (not diagonalizable), functional calculus via Cauchy integral:

$$f(A) = \frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_\Gamma f(z)(zI - A)^{-1}\,dz$$

where $\Gamma$ encloses $\sigma(A)$.

### Jordan and Schur form

When $A$ is not diagonalizable ($m_g(\lambda) < m_a(\lambda)$ for some $\lambda$), the Jordan form $J = P^{-1}AP$ is block-diagonal with blocks $J_k(\lambda) = \lambda I + N$ ($N$ nilpotent). In numerical computation the Schur form $A = QTQ^*$ ($T$ triangular, $Q$ unitary) is preferred, as it is always stable.

### Perron-Frobenius theorem

Matrix $A \geq 0$ irreducible has spectral radius $\rho(A) = \lambda^* > 0$ with unique strictly positive eigenvector (up to scale). **Basis of all theory of irreducible Markov chains, Leontief input-output models, and PageRank.** The stationary distribution of a Markov chain is the normalized eigenvector of $\lambda = 1$.

### Connection to Black-Scholes multi-asset (Lesson 119)

In multi-asset portfolios, the covariance matrix $\Sigma$ is symmetric positive semidefinite. Its eigenvalues measure variance in principal directions; the eigenvectors are the **orthogonal risk factors**. Reducing to the $k$ largest eigenvalues is exactly what PCA does — and it is what allows approximating $\Sigma$ as low-rank for option pricing in baskets.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practical application">

### Basic recipe by hand

1. Compute $p_A(\lambda) = \det(A - \lambda I) = 0$.
2. Solve the polynomial (quadratic formula, factoring, sum/product of roots).
3. For each eigenvalue $\lambda$, solve $(A - \lambda I)\vec{v} = \vec{0}$ by row reduction.

### Practical shortcuts

- **Triangular** (upper or lower): eigenvalues on the diagonal — determinant of triangular is product of diagonal.
- $A^T$: same eigenvalues as $A$ (since $\det(A^T - \lambda I) = \det(A - \lambda I)$).
- $A + cI$: eigenvalues of $A$ shifted by $c$.
- $A^k$: eigenvalues are $\lambda^k$.
- $A^{-1}$ (if it exists): eigenvalues are $1/\lambda$.

### Quick verification

Always check: $\sum \lambda_i = \operatorname{tr}(A)$ and $\prod \lambda_i = \det A$. If they don't match, there is an error in the polynomial.

### Interpretation for $2 \times 2$ matrix

For <Eq>{`A = \\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\\\ c & d \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>, the polynomial is

$$\lambda^2 - (a+d)\lambda + (ad - bc) = 0$$

that is, $\lambda^2 - \operatorname{tr}(A)\,\lambda + \det(A) = 0$. Direct quadratic formula.

### Numerical alert

- **Complex conjugate eigenvalues**: a real matrix can have them (e.g., rotation). Expect to see $e^{\pm i\theta}$ in system analysis.
- **Algebraic multiplicity > 1**: check whether $m_g = m_a$ (diagonalizable) or $m_g < m_a$ (not diagonalizable — Jordan form).
- Eigenvalues **very close together**: numerical instability — avoid computing by hand for $n \geq 4$.

</Porta>

## Solved examples

<Exemplo numero="1" titulo="Eigenvalues of 2x2 matrix via characteristic polynomial">

**Problem.** Compute the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of <Eq>{`A = \\begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \\\\ 0 & 2 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>.

**Strategy.** $A$ is upper triangular — eigenvalues are on the diagonal. But we compute via the polynomial to practice the general method.

**Solution.**

<Eq>{`p_A(\\lambda) = \\det(A - \\lambda I) = \\det\\begin{pmatrix} 3-\\lambda & 1 \\\\ 0 & 2-\\lambda \\end{pmatrix} = (3-\\lambda)(2-\\lambda) = \\lambda^2 - 5\\lambda + 6.`}</Eq>

Roots: $\lambda_1 = 3$, $\lambda_2 = 2$.

For $\lambda_1 = 3$: <Eq>{`(A - 3I)\\vec{v} = \\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\\\ 0 & -1 \\end{pmatrix}\\vec{v} = \\vec{0}`}</Eq> $\Rightarrow$ $v_2 = 0$, $v_1$ free. Eigenvector: $(1, 0)$.

For $\lambda_2 = 2$: <Eq>{`(A - 2I)\\vec{v} = \\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\\\ 0 & 0 \\end{pmatrix}\\vec{v} = \\vec{0}`}</Eq> $\Rightarrow$ $v_1 = -v_2$. Eigenvector: $(-1, 1)$.

**Verification.** $A(1,0) = (3,0) = 3(1,0)$. $A(-1,1) = (-3+1, 2) = (-2, 2) = 2(-1, 1)$. Correct.

**Source.** [Beezer, A First Course in Linear Algebra, §EE, Example ESMS2](http://linear.ups.edu/) — GNU FDL.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="2" titulo="Eigenvalues of symmetric 2x2 matrix (real eigenvalues)">

**Problem.** Compute the eigenvalues of <Eq>{`A = \\begin{pmatrix} 5 & 4 \\\\ 4 & 5 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq> and verify the properties $\operatorname{tr}(A) = \sum\lambda_i$ and $\det A = \prod\lambda_i$.

**Strategy.** Degree-2 polynomial with quadratic formula. Then verification.

**Solution.**

$p_A(\lambda) = (5-\lambda)^2 - 16 = \lambda^2 - 10\lambda + 9 = (\lambda - 9)(\lambda - 1).$

Eigenvalues: $\lambda_1 = 9$, $\lambda_2 = 1$.

Verification: $\operatorname{tr}(A) = 5 + 5 = 10 = 9 + 1$. $\det A = 25 - 16 = 9 = 9 \cdot 1$. Both check out.

For $\lambda = 9$: <Eq>{`(A - 9I)\\vec{v} = \\begin{pmatrix} -4 & 4 \\\\ 4 & -4 \\end{pmatrix}\\vec{v} = \\vec{0}`}</Eq> $\Rightarrow$ $v_1 = v_2$. Eigenvector: $(1, 1)$.

For $\lambda = 1$: eigenvector $(1, -1)$.

**Verification.** The two eigenvectors are orthogonal: $(1,1) \cdot (1,-1) = 0$. Expected — $A$ is symmetric.

**Source.** [Beezer, A First Course in Linear Algebra, §EE, Example ESMS4](http://linear.ups.edu/) — GNU FDL.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="3" titulo="Algebraic vs geometric multiplicity (not diagonalizable)">

**Problem.** For <Eq>{`A = \\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\\\ 0 & 2 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>, compute the characteristic polynomial, identify multiplicities, and decide whether $A$ is diagonalizable.

**Strategy.** Compute $p_A$, find roots and dimension of eigenspace.

**Solution.**

$p_A(\lambda) = (2-\lambda)^2 \Rightarrow \lambda = 2$ with $m_a(2) = 2$.

Eigenspace: <Eq>{`(A - 2I)\\vec{v} = \\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\\\ 0 & 0 \\end{pmatrix}\\vec{v} = \\vec{0}`}</Eq> $\Rightarrow$ $v_2 = 0$, $v_1$ free. Thus $E_2 = \operatorname{span}\{(1,0)\}$, with $m_g(2) = 1$.

Since $m_g(2) = 1 < m_a(2) = 2$, the matrix is **not diagonalizable**. Its Jordan form is <Eq>{`J = \\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\\\ 0 & 2 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq> (the same as $A$ itself).

**Verification.** If it were diagonalizable, there would exist two linearly independent eigenvectors — but there is only one. Hence there is no eigenbasis for $\mathbb{R}^2$.

**Source.** [Beezer, A First Course in Linear Algebra, §EE, Example EMMS](http://linear.ups.edu/) — GNU FDL.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="4" titulo="Matrix power via eigenvalues">

**Problem.** Compute $A^{10}$ for <Eq>{`A = \\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\\\ 1 & 0 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq> (Fibonacci matrix).

**Strategy.** Diagonalize $A = PDP^{-1}$, then $A^{10} = PD^{10}P^{-1}$.

**Solution.**

$p_A(\lambda) = \lambda^2 - \lambda - 1 = 0 \Rightarrow \lambda = \frac{1 \pm \sqrt{5}}{2}.$

Let $\phi = (1 + \sqrt{5})/2 \approx 1.618$ (golden ratio) and $\psi = (1 - \sqrt{5})/2 \approx -0.618$.

Eigenvectors: for $\phi$ we have $(\phi, 1)$; for $\psi$ we have $(\psi, 1)$.

$A^{10} = PD^{10}P^{-1}$ where $D = \operatorname{diag}(\phi^{10}, \psi^{10})$.

In particular, the element $(1,1)$ of $A^n$ is $F_{n+1}$ (the $(n+1)$-th Fibonacci number), given by Binet's formula:

$$F_n = \frac{\phi^n - \psi^n}{\sqrt{5}}.$$

Numerically: $\phi^{10} \approx 122.99$, $\psi^{10} \approx 0.009$, $F_{11} = 89$.

**Verification.** <Eq>{`A^{10} = \\begin{pmatrix} F_{11} & F_{10} \\\\ F_{10} & F_9 \\end{pmatrix} = \\begin{pmatrix} 89 & 55 \\\\ 55 & 34 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq> — confirms the Fibonacci sequence.

**Source.** [Austin, Understanding Linear Algebra, §4.1](https://understandinglinearalgebra.org/html/section-eigen-intro.html) — CC-BY-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="5" titulo="Stationary distribution of Markov chain via eigenvector">

**Problem.** A Markov chain models weather in São Paulo: states Sunny (S) and Rainy (R). The transition matrix is

$<Eq>{`P = \\begin{pmatrix} 0.8 & 0.2 \\\\ 0.4 & 0.6 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>$

where $P_{ij}$ is the probability of transitioning from $j$ to $i$. Find the stationary distribution $\pi$ (eigenvector of $\lambda = 1$).

**Strategy.** Stochastic matrices always have $\lambda = 1$ as an eigenvalue. Solve $(P - I)\pi = \vec{0}$ with normalization $\pi_1 + \pi_2 = 1$.

**Solution.**

<Eq>{`(P - I)\\pi = \\begin{pmatrix} -0.2 & 0.2 \\\\ 0.4 & -0.4 \\end{pmatrix}\\pi = \\vec{0}`}</Eq> $\Rightarrow$ $-0.2\pi_S + 0.2\pi_R = 0$ $\Rightarrow$ $\pi_S = \pi_R$.

Normalizing: $\pi_S + \pi_R = 1 \Rightarrow \pi_S = \pi_R = 0.5$.

In the long run, São Paulo is sunny 50% of the time and rainy 50%.

**Verification.** <Eq>{`P\\pi = \\begin{pmatrix} 0.8 \\cdot 0.5 + 0.2 \\cdot 0.5 \\\\ 0.4 \\cdot 0.5 + 0.6 \\cdot 0.5 \\end{pmatrix} = \\begin{pmatrix} 0.5 \\\\ 0.5 \\end{pmatrix} = \\pi`}</Eq>. Correct.

**Source.** [Austin, Understanding Linear Algebra, §4.3](https://understandinglinearalgebra.org/html/section-stochastic.html) — CC-BY-SA.

</Exemplo>
