<Porta nivel="formal" titulo="Rigorous definition">

### Symmetric matrices

<Definicao titulo="Real symmetric matrix">

$A \in \mathcal{M}_n(\mathbb{R})$ is **symmetric** if $A^T = A$, that is, $a_{ij} = a_{ji}$ for all $i, j$.

Equivalently, $A$ is symmetric if and only if $A$ equals its own transpose.

</Definicao>

> "A matrix is called *symmetric* if it equals its own transpose: $A = A^T$. [...] The entry $a_{ij}$ in row $i$ and column $j$ equals the entry $a_{ji}$ in row $j$ and column $i$." — [Beezer, A First Course in Linear Algebra, §OD](http://linear.ups.edu/fcla/fcla.html)

<Teorema titulo="Spectral theorem (real form)">

Let $A \in \mathcal{M}_n(\mathbb{R})$ be symmetric. Then:

1. All eigenvalues of $A$ are **real**.
2. Eigenvectors corresponding to **distinct** eigenvalues are **orthogonal**.
3. There exists an orthonormal basis of $\mathbb{R}^n$ formed by eigenvectors of $A$.
4. There exist $Q$ orthogonal and $D$ diagonal such that $A = QDQ^T$.

</Teorema>

<Equation explicacao="Orthogonal diagonalization of symmetric A: Q is the matrix whose columns are orthonormal eigenvectors; D contains the corresponding eigenvalues on the diagonal." latex="A = QDQ^T = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \lambda_i \, q_i q_i^T" />

The last equality shows $A$ as a sum of rank-1 projections weighted by eigenvalues — the spectral decomposition.

### Orthogonal matrices

<Definicao titulo="Orthogonal matrix">

$Q \in \mathcal{M}_n(\mathbb{R})$ is **orthogonal** if $Q^T Q = Q Q^T = I_n$, that is, $Q^{-1} = Q^T$.

Equivalently: the columns (and rows) of $Q$ form an **orthonormal basis** of $\mathbb{R}^n$.

</Definicao>

> "An $n \times n$ matrix $Q$ is called an *orthogonal matrix* if $Q^T Q = I$." — [Austin, Understanding Linear Algebra, §7.1](https://understandinglinearalgebra.org/sec-symmetric-matrices.html)

**Fundamental properties:**

- Preserves norm: $\lVert Qv \rVert = \lVert v \rVert$ for all $v$.
- Preserves inner product: $\langle Qu, Qv \rangle = \langle u, v \rangle$.
- $\det Q = \pm 1$. If $\det Q = 1$: rotation. If $\det Q = -1$: rotation followed by reflection.
- Complex eigenvalues have modulus 1.

The complex analog is the **unitary matrix**: $U^* U = I$, where $U^*$ is the adjoint (conjugate transpose).

### Hermitian matrices

<Definicao titulo="Hermitian matrix">

$A \in \mathcal{M}_n(\mathbb{C})$ is **Hermitian** if $A^* = A$, where $A^* = \overline{A}^T$ is the adjoint (transpose of conjugate). For real entries, Hermitian coincides with symmetric.

</Definicao>

> "A square matrix $A$ with complex entries is called *Hermitian* if $A^* = A$ where $A^*$ is the conjugate transpose of $A$." — [Beezer, A First Course in Linear Algebra, §HMD](http://linear.ups.edu/fcla/fcla.html)

**Complex spectral theorem.** Every Hermitian matrix has real eigenvalues and admits unitary diagonalization: $A = U D U^*$.

### Positive definite matrices

<Definicao titulo="Symmetric positive definite (SPD) matrix">

$A$ symmetric is **positive definite** (SPD) if $x^T A x > 0$ for all $x \neq 0$.

It is **positive semidefinite** (PSD) if $x^T A x \geq 0$ for all $x$.

</Definicao>

**Equivalent criteria for SPD:**

- All eigenvalues $\lambda_i > 0$.
- All leading principal minors (determinants of the $k \times k$ submatrices from the upper left corner) are positive — **Sylvester's criterion**.
- There exists $L$ lower triangular invertible with $A = LL^T$ — **Cholesky factorization**.

### Quadratic form

<Definicao titulo="Quadratic form">

Given $A$ symmetric, the **quadratic form** associated is $Q_A(x) = x^T A x$.

By diagonalization $A = QDQ^T$ and change of variables $y = Q^T x$:

<Equation explicacao="Canonical form of the quadratic form: sum of pure squares weighted by eigenvalues. The signs of eigenvalues determine the classification." latex="Q_A(x) = y^T D y = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \lambda_i y_i^2" />

</Definicao>

**Classification:** if all $\lambda_i > 0$: positive definite. If all $\lambda_i < 0$: negative definite. If there are positive and negative eigenvalues: indefinite.

<Insight>

The spectral theorem is the most important theorem in all applied linear algebra. Nearly every technique in multivariate statistics (PCA, discriminant analysis, canonical correlation), quadratic optimization (Newton, conjugate gradient), and quantum physics (stationary states) rests on it. Every covariance matrix is symmetric PSD — and the spectral theorem says there exists a basis in which it "looks diagonal," with no cross-correlation.

</Insight>

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

Imagine you have a magic mirror in the middle of a grid of squares.

A **symmetric matrix** is like a picture where, if you fold the paper along the diagonal, the numbers on both sides fit perfectly. What is in position (row 2, column 3) equals what is in (row 3, column 2).

An **orthogonal matrix** is like a frame that you can rotate without ever stretching or shrinking the picture inside. Everything stays the same size, just turns at a different angle.

A **Hermitian matrix** is like a symmetric one, but for numbers that have an imaginary part (like complex numbers). It guarantees that everything it measures — like energy — is always a real number, not imaginary.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

In linear algebra there are three types of matrix that appear everywhere:

**Symmetric** ($A = A^T$): the element in row 2, column 3 equals the one in row 3, column 2. The matrix is a reflection of itself along the diagonal. Example:

<Eq>{`A = \\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 3 \\\\ 3 & 5 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>

The $3$ appears in both off-diagonal positions. Every matrix of distances, correlations, or covariances between variables is symmetric.

**Orthogonal** ($Q^T Q = I$): the columns of $Q$ are perpendicular to each other and have length 1. Multiplying by $Q$ is like rotating — lengths and angles are preserved.

**Positive definite**: symmetric with the special property that $x^T A x > 0$ for any nonzero vector $x$. Appears in energies, variances, and distances — things that can never be negative.

The big result is the **spectral theorem**: every symmetric matrix can be written as $A = Q D Q^T$, where $Q$ is orthogonal and $D$ is diagonal with the real eigenvalues of $A$.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old teen">

These three classes are not rare special cases — they are **the majority** of matrices that appear in real problems.

### Symmetric ($A = A^T$)

Appears in: covariance matrices ($\text{Cov}(X_i, X_j) = \text{Cov}(X_j, X_i)$), Hessians of twice-differentiable functions (Schwarz's theorem), adjacency matrices of undirected graphs, Hamiltonians in quantum physics.

**The spectral theorem** guarantees that every symmetric matrix has real eigenvalues and orthogonal eigenvectors — therefore it is diagonalizable in the most natural basis possible.

### Orthogonal ($Q^T Q = I$)

Rotations in $\mathbb{R}^2$ and $\mathbb{R}^3$ are orthogonal with determinant 1. Reflections have determinant $-1$. In QR and SVD decompositions, $Q$ is always orthogonal. The discrete Fourier transform matrix is unitary (complex version).

### Positive definite

The Hessian at a local minimum of a smooth function is SPD — this is exactly what guarantees the point is a minimum and not a maximum or saddle point. In optimization, SPD is synonymous with "well-posed and convex problem."

### Quadratic form and conics

$Q(x, y) = ax^2 + 2bxy + cy^2 = x^T A x$ with <Eq>{`A = \\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\\\ b & c \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>. The equation $Q(x, y) = 1$ is a **conic**. The spectral theorem rotates the axes to eliminate the cross term $2bxy$: after rotation, the conic becomes canonical form $\lambda_1 u^2 + \lambda_2 v^2 = 1$ — an ellipse if $\lambda_1, \lambda_2 > 0$, a hyperbola if they have opposite signs.

<Insight>

When you see the expression $5x^2 + 6xy + 5y^2 = 8$, the "trick" is to write the matrix form, diagonalize, change to the eigenvector basis — and boom, you eliminated the $6xy$ without any complicated algebra.

</Insight>

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

These three classes are the **currency** of all computational engineering. Recognizing them completely changes the numerical strategy.

### Computational costs

| Class | Decomposition | Cost | Algorithm |
|---|---|---|---|
| General $n \times n$ | LU | $O(n^3)$ | Gaussian elimination with pivoting |
| Symmetric | $LDL^T$ | $O(n^3 / 2)$ | Bunch-Kaufman factorization |
| SPD | Cholesky $LL^T$ | $O(n^3 / 6)$ | More stable, no pivoting needed |
| Symmetric tridiagonal | Implicit QR | $O(n^2)$ | Lapack `dsytrd` |
| Orthogonal | Inversion = transposition | $O(n^2)$ | `Q^{-1} = Q^T` |

### Cholesky in practice

To solve $Ax = b$ with $A$ SPD: factor $A = LL^T$ (once, cost $n^3/6$), then solve $Ly = b$ (lower triangular) and $L^T x = y$ (upper triangular). Stable without pivoting.

Uses: correlated Monte Carlo simulation (generate $L z$ with $z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ to obtain $x \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \Sigma)$ with $\Sigma = LL^T$), Markowitz (minimize portfolio variance $w^T \Sigma w$), finite elements.

### SPD in optimization

Newton's method: $x_{k+1} = x_k - H^{-1} \nabla f$. $H$ SPD guarantees descent direction. When $H$ is not SPD (plateau or local maximum), modify: Levenberg-Marquardt adds $\mu I$ until SPD.

Ridge regression: $\hat\beta = (X^TX + \lambda I)^{-1} X^T y$. $X^TX + \lambda I$ is always SPD for $\lambda > 0$, even if $X^TX$ is only PSD (linearly dependent columns).

### Orthogonal matrices and QR decomposition

$A = QR$ via Gram-Schmidt (unstable) or Householder reflections (stable). Foundation of numerical least squares. Normalized DFT is unitary — FFT computes $Fv$ in $O(N \log N)$.

### Quantum mechanics and Hermitian matrices

The Hamiltonian $\hat H$ is Hermitian: real eigenvalues (observable energies), orthogonal eigenstates. All of perturbation theory in quantum mechanics uses the Hermitian spectral theorem.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

### Cone of PSD matrices

$\mathbb{S}^n_+ = \{A \in \mathcal{M}_n(\mathbb{R}) : A \succeq 0\}$ is a closed convex cone. Optimization over this cone is **semidefinite programming (SDP)** — generalization of linear programming. Uses: convex relaxations (Goemans-Williamson for Max-Cut with guarantee $0{.}878$ of optimum), robust control ($H_\infty$), sum-of-squares hierarchies.

### Random matrix theory

For symmetric matrices with i.i.d. entries (Wigner), the empirical distribution of eigenvalues converges to the **Wigner semicircle law** as $n \to \infty$. Foundation of random matrix theory in finance (Marchenko-Pastur model for sample covariances in high dimension).

### Positive operators in Hilbert spaces

$T \in B(H)$ is positive if $\langle Tv, v \rangle \geq 0$ for all $v$. Square root $T^{1/2}$ exists by functional calculus (Borel spectral integration). In quantum mechanics, **mixed state** = density operator $\rho$: positive, trace 1, convex subset of positive operator space.

### Pseudospectrum and non-normal matrices

Symmetric matrix is **normal** ($AA^T = A^TA$) and well-conditioned: the pseudospectrum $\epsilon$ collapses near the spectrum. Non-normal matrices have expanded pseudospectrum — transient phenomena (Trefethen-Embree) and non-modal amplification in fluid dynamics.

### Spectral Laplacian of graphs

$L = D - W$ (degree minus adjacency) is symmetric PSD. Graph connectivity $\leftrightarrow$ multiplicity of eigenvalue 0 (constant eigenvector). **Spectral cut** (spectral clustering): use the $k$ smallest eigenvectors of $L$ to partition the graph — foundation of spectral k-means.

### Connection to Black-Scholes (Lesson 119)

The covariance matrix $\Sigma$ of multi-asset returns is PSD. In Monte Carlo simulation for pricing, generate $\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \Sigma)$ via Cholesky ($\Sigma = LL^T$, then $L z$ with $z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$). Any breakdown of $\Sigma$ to non-PSD (by rounding or sparse data) can be corrected by projecting onto $\mathbb{S}^n_+$ via spectral truncation: $\Sigma_+ = Q \max(D, 0) Q^T$.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practice — applications with real data">

### Application 1: covariance of Brazilian stocks (B3)

Suppose monthly returns of 3 stocks (PETR4, VALE3, ITUB4). The sample covariance matrix is always symmetric and, if the stocks are linearly independent, SPD:

<Eq>{`\\Sigma = \\begin{pmatrix} 0{.}04 & 0{.}02 & 0{.}01 \\\\ 0{.}02 & 0{.}09 & 0{.}03 \\\\ 0{.}01 & 0{.}03 & 0{.}06 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>

Verify SPD: all eigenvalues of $\Sigma$ positive? Sylvester criterion: $0{.}04 > 0$; $0{.}04 \cdot 0{.}09 - 0{.}02^2 = 0{.}0032 > 0$; $\det \Sigma > 0$ (compute). To generate correlated scenarios, factor $\Sigma = LL^T$ via Cholesky and compute $r = L z$ with $z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_3)$.

### Application 2: rotation in computer graphics (3D games)

A rotation of $\theta = 45°$ around the $z$ axis is represented by the orthogonal matrix:

<Eq>{`R_z(45°) = \\begin{pmatrix} \\cos 45° & -\\sin 45° & 0 \\\\ \\sin 45° & \\cos 45° & 0 \\\\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\end{pmatrix} = \\frac{1}{\\sqrt{2}} \\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 & 0 \\\\ 1 & 1 & 0 \\\\ 0 & 0 & \\sqrt 2 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>

Verification: $R^T R = I$, $\det R = 1$. Composing two rotations: $R_1 R_2$ (both orthogonal) is orthogonal — the product preserves structure. Inversion costs $O(n^2)$ via transposition.

### Application 3: identify conic via quadratic form

Classify $5x^2 + 6xy + 5y^2 = 8$. Associated matrix: <Eq>{`A = \\begin{pmatrix} 5 & 3 \\\\ 3 & 5 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>. Eigenvalues: $\lambda_{1,2} = 5 \pm 3$, so $\lambda_1 = 8$ and $\lambda_2 = 2$. Both positive: conic is an **ellipse**. Canonical form: $8u^2 + 2v^2 = 8$, or $u^2 + v^2/4 = 1$. Semi-axes: $a = 1$, $b = 2$.

</Porta>

## Worked examples

<Exemplo titulo="Verify symmetry and classify (basic level)" numero="1">

**Problem.** Given <Eq>{`A = \\begin{pmatrix} 3 & -1 \\\\ -1 & 3 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>, verify that $A$ is symmetric and determine if it is SPD. If it is SPD, find the Cholesky factorization.

**Strategy.** Verify $A = A^T$ by inspection. Check SPD via Sylvester criterion. For Cholesky, solve $A = LL^T$ with $L$ lower triangular.

**Solution.**

Symmetry: <Eq>{`A^T = \\begin{pmatrix} 3 & -1 \\\\ -1 & 3 \\end{pmatrix} = A`}</Eq>. Confirmed.

Sylvester: $a_{11} = 3 > 0$; $\det A = 9 - 1 = 8 > 0$. So $A$ is SPD.

Cholesky: write <Eq>{`L = \\begin{pmatrix} \\ell_{11} & 0 \\\\ \\ell_{21} & \\ell_{22} \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>. Then <Eq>{`LL^T = \\begin{pmatrix} \\ell_{11}^2 & \\ell_{11}\\ell_{21} \\\\ \\ell_{11}\\ell_{21} & \\ell_{21}^2 + \\ell_{22}^2 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>.

Comparing with $A$: $\ell_{11} = \sqrt{3}$; $\ell_{21} = -1/\sqrt{3}$; $\ell_{22} = \sqrt{3 - 1/3} = \sqrt{8/3}$.

<Eq>{`L = \\begin{pmatrix} \\sqrt{3} & 0 \\\\ -1/\\sqrt{3} & \\sqrt{8/3} \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>

**Verification.** <Eq>{`LL^T = \\begin{pmatrix} 3 & -1 \\\\ -1 & 1/3 + 8/3 \\end{pmatrix} = \\begin{pmatrix} 3 & -1 \\\\ -1 & 3 \\end{pmatrix} = A`}</Eq>. Correct.

**Source.** [Beezer, A First Course in Linear Algebra, §PSD, p. 447](http://linear.ups.edu/fcla/fcla.html) — GNU FDL.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Orthogonally diagonalize a 2x2 matrix (intermediate)" numero="2">

**Problem.** Orthogonally diagonalize <Eq>{`A = \\begin{pmatrix} 5 & 4 \\\\ 4 & 5 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>.

**Strategy.** Calculate eigenvalues, then orthonormal eigenvectors, assemble $Q$ and verify $QDQ^T = A$.

**Solution.**

Characteristic polynomial: $\det(A - \lambda I) = (\lambda - 5)^2 - 16 = \lambda^2 - 10\lambda + 9 = (\lambda - 1)(\lambda - 9) = 0$.

Eigenvalues: $\lambda_1 = 1$, $\lambda_2 = 9$.

For $\lambda_1 = 1$: $(A - I)v = 0$ gives $4v_1 + 4v_2 = 0$, so $v = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(1, -1)^T$.

For $\lambda_2 = 9$: $(A - 9I)v = 0$ gives $-4v_1 + 4v_2 = 0$, so $v = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(1, 1)^T$.

Since $\lambda_1 \neq \lambda_2$, the eigenvectors are orthogonal (spectral theorem).

<Eq>{`Q = \\frac{1}{\\sqrt{2}} \\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\\\ -1 & 1 \\end{pmatrix}, \\quad D = \\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\\\ 0 & 9 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>

**Verification.** <Eq>{`QDQ^T = \\frac{1}{2}\\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\\\ -1 & 1 \\end{pmatrix} \\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\\\ 0 & 9 \\end{pmatrix} \\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\\\ 1 & 1 \\end{pmatrix} = \\frac{1}{2}\\begin{pmatrix} 10 & 8 \\\\ 8 & 10 \\end{pmatrix} = \\begin{pmatrix} 5 & 4 \\\\ 4 & 5 \\end{pmatrix} = A`}</Eq>.

**Source.** [Austin, Understanding Linear Algebra, §7.2, Example 7.2.3](https://understandinglinearalgebra.org/sec-symmetric-matrices.html) — CC-BY-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Verify and use orthogonal matrix (intermediate)" numero="3">

**Problem.** Let <Eq>{`Q = \\frac{1}{\\sqrt{2}} \\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\\\ 1 & 1 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>. Verify that $Q$ is orthogonal, compute $\det Q$, and interpret geometrically.

**Strategy.** Verify $Q^T Q = I$, compute determinant, identify the transformation.

**Solution.**

<Eq>{`Q^T = \\frac{1}{\\sqrt{2}} \\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\\\ -1 & 1 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>.

<Eq>{`Q^T Q = \\frac{1}{2} \\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\\\ -1 & 1 \\end{pmatrix} \\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\\\ 1 & 1 \\end{pmatrix} = \\frac{1}{2} \\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\\\ 0 & 2 \\end{pmatrix} = I_2`}</Eq>.

So $Q$ is orthogonal. Also: $QQ^T = I$ (the reciprocal verification follows similarly).

$\det Q = \frac{1}{2}(1 \cdot 1 - (-1) \cdot 1) = \frac{1}{2} \cdot 2 = 1$.

Since $\det Q = 1$, the transformation is a **rotation** of $45°$ counterclockwise ($\cos 45° = \sin 45° = 1/\sqrt{2}$).

Lengths preserved: $\lVert Q v \rVert^2 = v^T Q^T Q v = v^T I v = \lVert v \rVert^2$.

**Verification.** Apply $Q$ to the vector $e_1 = (1, 0)^T$: $Qe_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(1, 1)^T$, which has norm 1 and angle $45°$. Consistent with a $45°$ rotation.

**Source.** [Hefferon, Linear Algebra, chapter 5, §2, Exercise 2.15](https://hefferon.net/linearalgebra/) — CC-BY-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Classify quadratic form and identify conic (advanced)" numero="4">

**Problem.** Classify the quadratic form $Q(x, y) = 5x^2 + 6xy + 5y^2$ and identify the curve $Q(x, y) = 8$.

**Strategy.** Write matrix form, orthogonally diagonalize, obtain canonical form, identify the conic by eigenvalues.

**Solution.**

Associated matrix: <Eq>{`A = \\begin{pmatrix} 5 & 3 \\\\ 3 & 5 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq> (note: the coefficient of the cross term $6xy$ is divided by 2 for off-diagonal elements, since $x^T A x = 5x^2 + 2 \cdot 3 \cdot xy + 5y^2$).

Eigenvalues: $\det(A - \lambda I) = (5-\lambda)^2 - 9 = 0 \Rightarrow \lambda_{1,2} = 5 \mp 3$, so $\lambda_1 = 2$, $\lambda_2 = 8$.

Both positive $\Rightarrow$ $A$ is **SPD** $\Rightarrow$ $Q(x, y)$ is positive definite.

In the eigenvector basis (45° rotation, $u = (x+y)/\sqrt{2}$, $v = (y-x)/\sqrt{2}$):

$$Q = 2u^2 + 8v^2$$

The curve $2u^2 + 8v^2 = 8$ is equivalent to $u^2/4 + v^2/1 = 1$. This is an **ellipse** with semi-major axis $a = 2$ (along $u$) and semi-minor axis $b = 1$ (along $v$).

**Verification.** The ellipse axes are the eigenvector directions: $u$-direction is $(1/\sqrt{2}, 1/\sqrt{2})$ (eigenvector of $\lambda_1 = 2$) and $v$-direction is $(1/\sqrt{2}, -1/\sqrt{2})$ (eigenvector of $\lambda_2 = 8$). At the extreme of the major axis with $u = 2, v = 0$: the corresponding $(x, y)$ is $(\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{2})$. Then $Q(\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{2}) = 2 \cdot 4 + 0 = 8$. Verified.

**Source.** [Austin, Understanding Linear Algebra, §7.3, Example 7.3.4](https://understandinglinearalgebra.org/sec-quadratic-forms.html) — CC-BY-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Demonstrate that A^T A is always PSD (proof level)" numero="5">

**Problem.** Show that $A^T A$ is always symmetric and positive semidefinite for any $A \in \mathcal{M}_{m \times n}(\mathbb{R})$.

**Strategy.** Check symmetry by direct transposition. Check PSD by computing $x^T (A^T A) x$ and recognizing it as a squared norm.

**Solution.**

**Symmetry.** $(A^T A)^T = A^T (A^T)^T = A^T A$. So $A^T A$ is symmetric.

**PSD.** For any $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$:

$$x^T (A^T A) x = (Ax)^T (Ax) = \lVert Ax \rVert^2 \geq 0$$

So $A^T A$ is positive semidefinite.

**When is it SPD?** When $\lVert Ax \rVert = 0 \Rightarrow x = 0$, that is, when $A$ has full rank ($n$ linearly independent columns).

Consequence: the Gram matrix $G_{ij} = v_i \cdot v_j = V^T V$ (where $V$ has the $v_i$ as columns) is always PSD, and SPD if and only if the vectors $v_1, \ldots, v_n$ are linearly independent.

**Verification.** For <Eq>{`A = \\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\\\ 0 & 1 \\\\ 1 & 1 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>: <Eq>{`A^T A = \\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\\\ 1 & 2 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>, which has eigenvalues $1$ and $3$, both positive. SPD (full rank).

**Source.** [Beezer, A First Course in Linear Algebra, §PSD, Theorem PSMT](http://linear.ups.edu/fcla/fcla.html) — GNU FDL.

</Exemplo>
