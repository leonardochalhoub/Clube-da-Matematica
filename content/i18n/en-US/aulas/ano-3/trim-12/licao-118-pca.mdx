<Porta nivel="formal" titulo="Mathematical definition">

### Setup and sample covariance

<Definicao titulo="Centered data and covariance matrix">

Data <Eq>{`X \\in \\mathbb{R}^{N \\times d}`}</Eq> (<Eq>{`N`}</Eq> samples, <Eq>{`d`}</Eq> features). Center:

<Equation explicacao="Subtracting the mean from each feature removes the constant component and centers the data." latex="\tilde{X} = X - \mathbf{1}\bar{x}^T, \quad \bar{x} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N x_i" />

**Sample covariance matrix:**

<Equation explicacao="Sigma is symmetric positive semidefinite (PSD): all eigenvalues are nonnegative." latex="\Sigma = \frac{1}{N-1}\tilde{X}^T \tilde{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}" />

</Definicao>

> "The covariance matrix $\Sigma$ is always symmetric and positive semidefinite. Its eigenvalues are nonnegative and the eigenvectors form an orthonormal basis of $\mathbb{R}^d$." — [Introduction to Applied Linear Algebra (VMLS), §10.1](https://web.stanford.edu/~boyd/vmls/)

### Principal components

<Definicao titulo="PCA: spectral decomposition of covariance">

<Equation explicacao="Sigma is diagonalizable (symmetric PSD): V orthogonal, Lambda diagonal with eigenvalues in descending order." latex="\Sigma = V \Lambda V^T, \quad \Lambda = \text{diag}(\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_d), \quad \lambda_1 \geq \cdots \geq \lambda_d \geq 0" />

- **Principal vector** <Eq>{`k`}</Eq>: <Eq>{`v_k`}</Eq> — <Eq>{`k`}</Eq>-th column of <Eq>{`V`}</Eq>.
- **Score** of sample <Eq>{`x_i`}</Eq> in PC <Eq>{`k`}</Eq>: <Eq>{`z_{ik} = v_k^T(x_i - \\bar{x})`}</Eq>.
- **Explained variance** by PC <Eq>{`k`}</Eq>: <Eq>{`\\lambda_k`}</Eq>.
- **Proportion of explained variance**: <Eq>{`\\lambda_k / \\sum_j \\lambda_j`}</Eq>.
- **Total variance**: <Eq>{`\\sum_j \\lambda_j = \\text{tr}(\\Sigma)`}</Eq>.

</Definicao>

### Optimality

<Teorema titulo="PCA maximizes projected variance (Hotelling 1933)">

The first principal component <Eq>{`v_1`}</Eq> is the direction that maximizes the variance of projection:

<Equation explicacao="Among all unit directions, v1 is the one for which the projected data has the greatest variance." latex="v_1 = \arg\max_{\|v\|=1} v^T \Sigma v" />

The <Eq>{`k`}</Eq>-th PC maximizes residual variance subject to being orthogonal to <Eq>{`v_1, \\ldots, v_{k-1}`}</Eq>. This is a Rayleigh quotient problem — the solution is the <Eq>{`k`}</Eq>-th eigenvector of <Eq>{`\\Sigma`}</Eq>.

</Teorema>

> "The principal components are the eigenvectors of the data covariance matrix, ordered by decreasing eigenvalue. The first principal component captures the maximum variance; successive components capture maximum residual variance subject to orthogonality." — [Understanding Linear Algebra, §7.1](https://understandinglinearalgebra.org/chap7.html)

### Connection with SVD

<Definicao titulo="PCA via SVD of centered data matrix">

If <Eq>{`\\tilde{X} = U \\Sigma_{SVD} V^T`}</Eq> (SVD of centered matrix):

- **Principal components**: columns of <Eq>{`V`}</Eq> (same as those of <Eq>{`\\Sigma_{cov}`}</Eq>).
- **Scores**: <Eq>{`Z = \\tilde{X} V = U \\Sigma_{SVD}`}</Eq>.
- **Eigenvalues of covariance**: <Eq>{`\\lambda_k = \\sigma_k^2 / (N-1)`}</Eq>.

SVD of <Eq>{`\\tilde{X}`}</Eq> is **numerically more stable** than decomposing <Eq>{`\\Sigma_{cov}`}</Eq> explicitly (avoids the square-up that amplifies rounding errors).

</Definicao>

### Reconstruction and approximation error

<Equation explicacao="Keeping K components minimizes the mean squared reconstruction error among all rank-K projections (Eckart-Young applied to PCA)." latex="\hat{x}_i = \bar{x} + \sum_{k=1}^K z_{ik}\, v_k" />

Reconstruction error: <Eq>{`\\frac{1}{N}\\sum_i \\|x_i - \\hat{x}_i\\|^2 = \\sum_{k > K} \\lambda_k`}</Eq>.

<Insight>

The **scree plot** plots <Eq>{`\\lambda_k`}</Eq> vs <Eq>{`k`}</Eq>. The "elbow" of the curve indicates where additional variance per component becomes negligible. Rule of thumb: choose <Eq>{`K`}</Eq> such that cumulative variance <Eq>{`\\geq 95\\%`}</Eq> for quantitative analysis, <Eq>{`\\geq 80\\%`}</Eq> for visualization.

</Insight>

Imagine you have a **box of marbles** scattered on the floor in a **cigar shape** — long in one direction, thin in the other.

**Principal component** is just the **"long way" of the cigar** — the main direction where the marbles are spread out most.

If I ask you to explain where each marble is with **just one number**, the best number is "how far along the cigar". The other directions matter much less.

PCA finds this "cigar axis" automatically, even in 100 or 1000 dimensions where you can't see it.

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

You have data on **height and weight** of 1,000 people. Each person is a point on a 2-axis graph. But height and weight are **highly correlated**: tall person tends to be heavier.

The points form an **elongated cloud** — like a tilted cigar. PCA finds:

- **Main direction**: the cigar direction (mix of height+weight). Captures 90%+ of variation.
- **Secondary direction**: perpendicular. Captures individual deviations.

If you project each person onto just the **main direction**, you lose little — most variation is there. You've reduced from 2D to 1D.

In real problems with 100 features, PCA finds the **5-10 main directions** that capture almost all variation. **Dramatically reduces** data size without losing much information.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old teenager">

PCA is the most used exploratory analysis tool in data science. **Every time you hear "dimensionality reduction"**, it's probably PCA or a variant.

### Recipe in 6 steps

1. **Collect data**: matrix <Eq>{`X \\in \\mathbb{R}^{N \\times d}`}</Eq>.
2. **Center**: subtract the mean from each column: <Eq>{`\\tilde{X} = X - \\bar{X}`}</Eq>.
3. **Compute SVD**: <Eq>{`\\tilde{X} = U \\Sigma V^T`}</Eq> (or eigendecomposition of <Eq>{`\\Sigma_{cov}`}</Eq>).
4. **Scree plot**: plot <Eq>{`\\sigma_k^2`}</Eq> as a function of <Eq>{`k`}</Eq>.
5. **Choose <Eq>{`K`}</Eq>**: where the curve "elbows" or cumulative variance <Eq>{`\\geq 95\\%`}</Eq>.
6. **Project**: <Eq>{`Z = \\tilde{X} V_K`}</Eq> are the scores in the <Eq>{`K`}</Eq> PCs.

### Explained variance

<Eq>{`\\text{ExpVar}(k) = \\lambda_k / \\sum_j \\lambda_j`}</Eq>. Cumulative: <Eq>{`\\sum_{k=1}^K \\text{ExpVar}(k)`}</Eq>.

### When PCA works well vs poorly

| Works | Doesn't work |
|---|---|
| Correlated variables | Non-linear structure |
| Similar scales | Strong outliers (use Robust PCA) |
| Want 2D visualization | Want classification (use LDA) |

### Classic case: Iris Dataset (Fisher, 1936)

4 features (petal/sepal length/width). PCA reduces to 2D retaining 95%+ variance. Visualization reveals 3 clearly separated clusters of species — Fisher already showed this in 1936.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

PCA is the first tool in any data pipeline. Understanding it deeply (not just the recipe) is essential.

### Optimality: proof of the Rayleigh quotient

We want <Eq>{`v^* = \\arg\\max_{\\|v\\|=1} v^T \\Sigma v`}</Eq>. Using Lagrange multipliers:

<Eq>{`\\mathcal{L}(v, \\mu) = v^T \\Sigma v - \\mu(\\|v\\|^2 - 1)`}</Eq>

Taking derivative with respect to <Eq>{`v`}</Eq> and setting to zero: <Eq>{`2\\Sigma v = 2\\mu v`}</Eq>, i.e. <Eq>{`\\Sigma v = \\mu v`}</Eq>. Therefore <Eq>{`v^*`}</Eq> is an eigenvector of <Eq>{`\\Sigma`}</Eq> and the maximum is <Eq>{`\\mu = v^{*T} \\Sigma v^* = \\lambda_1`}</Eq> (largest eigenvalue).

### Typical ML pipeline

```
raw data → standardize → PCA(K components) → model(kNN, SVM, NN, ...)
```

Reduces dimension → reduces overfitting + speeds training.

### Canonical applications

**Genomics**: microarray with 30,000 genes × 100 patients. PCA reduces to ~10 dimensions → cancer subtype clusters (Golub et al. 1999).

**Finance**: yield curve — 3 PCs (level, slope, curvature) capture ~99% variance (Litterman-Scheinkman 1991). Basis of all fixed-income trading.

**Computer vision**: Eigenfaces (Turk-Pentland 1991) — PCA of centered faces → bases for facial recognition.

**Bioinformatics**: 1000 Genomes Project — PCA of alleles reveals geographic structure (clusters by continent).

### Standardization: classic pitfall

If features have different scales (e.g., salary in R$ vs age in years), **without standardizing** salary dominates the variance. Always z-score before PCA unless scales are deliberately comparable.

### Python code

```python
import numpy as np
from sklearn.decomposition import PCA

# Standardize (ALWAYS when scales differ)
X_std = (X - X.mean(axis=0)) / X.std(axis=0, ddof=1)

# PCA
pca = PCA(n_components=10)
Z = pca.fit_transform(X_std)

print("Explained variance:", pca.explained_variance_ratio_)
print("Cumulative:", np.cumsum(pca.explained_variance_ratio_))

# Loadings (eigenvectors)
print("Components (loadings):", pca.components_.shape)
```

### Orthogonality of scores

PCs are orthogonal by construction: <Eq>{`\\text{Cov}(z_j, z_k) = 0`}</Eq> for <Eq>{`j \\neq k`}</Eq>. This eliminates multicollinearity — scores can be used directly as features in linear models.

### Important variants

| Variant | When to use |
|---|---|
| Kernel PCA | Non-linear structure in data |
| Sparse PCA | Want interpretable components |
| Robust PCA | Data with outliers (Candès-Li-Ma-Wright) |
| Incremental PCA | Streaming data |
| Probabilistic PCA | Missing data, Bayesian inference |

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

PCA is not "just" dimensionality reduction — it is the optimal representation theorem for data with second-moment structure.

### Historical context

Karl Pearson (1901) introduced the idea of "lines of best fit" in multivariate data — what we now call PC1. Harold Hotelling (1933) formalized the complete method for multiple component analysis in the context of psychometric variables. The connection to SVD is due to Eckart-Young (1936). The name "principal component analysis" is Hotelling's.

For 50 years, PCA was primarily multivariate statistics (Pearson, Fisher, Hotelling, Rao). In the 1980s-90s, application in computer vision (Eigenfaces, Turk-Pentland 1991) and signal processing transformed PCA into the standard preprocessing tool in ML.

### Connection to information theory

PCA minimizes information lost in compression when "loss" is measured by mean squared error. For Gaussian distributions, PCA is **optimal** in Shannon's sense: it maximizes entropy of scores while preserving total variance. For non-Gaussian distributions, Independent Component Analysis (ICA) may be better.

### PCA and statistical inference

How many components to retain? Formal criteria:

- **Kaiser criterion**: retain PCs with <Eq>{`\\lambda_k > 1`}</Eq> (variance above unit, if data standardized).
- **Scree test**: cut at visible "elbow".
- **Marchenko-Pastur distribution**: for pure noise (<Eq>{`X`}</Eq> iid Gaussian), eigenvalues of <Eq>{`\\Sigma_{cov}`}</Eq> follow the MP distribution. Retain PCs whose <Eq>{`\\lambda_k`}</Eq> exceeds this distribution's upper limit.
- **Bootstrap**: resample data and verify stability of PCs.

### PCA and Transformers

Multi-head attention in Transformers (Vaswani 2017) can be interpreted as gradient-learned PCA: the product <Eq>{`Q K^T`}</Eq> computes token correlations; softmax normalizes to distribution; product with <Eq>{`V`}</Eq> is weighted projection. Essentially, each head learns a subspace projection that resembles PCA with context-dependent weights.

Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA, Hu 2022) — LLM fine-tuning — justifies the low-rank perturbation <Eq>{`\\Delta W = BA`}</Eq> precisely because pre-trained weights live on a manifold of intrinsic low dimensionality, analogous to Eckart-Young rank-<Eq>{`K`}</Eq> approximation.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practical application">

### PCA in ENEM microdata

The ENEM has ~200 variables per candidate (grades, socioeconomic questionnaire, school). PCA of candidate × variable matrix:

1. **Center and standardize** (z-score per column).
2. **Compute SVD** of <Eq>{`\\tilde X`}</Eq>.
3. **Scree plot**: identify how many PCs explain 80%, 90%, 95%.
4. **Interpret PC1**: usually the "general performance" factor.
5. **Interpret PC2**: usually "school type" (public vs private).
6. **Visualize** in 2D (PC1 × PC2): reveals regional and socioeconomic structure.

INEP researchers do exactly this for equity reports.

### PCA in portfolio management

Given <Eq>{`n`}</Eq> stocks with daily returns:

1. Calculate correlation matrix <Eq>{`R`}</Eq> (covariance of standardized returns).
2. PCA of <Eq>{`R`}</Eq>: PC1 is usually the "market" (all correlated).
3. PC2 may be "sector" (financial vs tech vs commodities).
4. **Unsystematic risk**: variance explained by last PCs.
5. **Hedge**: short on PC1 eliminates market risk (beta hedge).

Institutional managers (banks, funds) use PCA for **risk attribution** — decomposing portfolio risk into systematic (PCs) and idiosyncratic components.

</Porta>

## Worked examples

<Exemplo titulo="PCA of a simple 3D dataset" numero="1">

**Problem.** Given <Eq>{`\\tilde{X} = \\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 & 0 \\\\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\\\ 0 & -1 & -1 \\\\ -2 & 0 & 0 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq> (4 samples, 3 features, already centered), compute the covariance matrix, eigenvalues, and explained variance.

**Strategy.** Calculate <Eq>{`\\Sigma = \\tilde{X}^T \\tilde{X} / (N-1)`}</Eq> and decompose.

**Solution.**

<Eq>{`\\tilde{X}^T \\tilde{X} = \\begin{pmatrix} 8 & 0 & 0 \\\\ 0 & 2 & 2 \\\\ 0 & 2 & 2 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>. With <Eq>{`N - 1 = 3`}</Eq>: <Eq>{`\\Sigma = \\begin{pmatrix} 8/3 & 0 & 0 \\\\ 0 & 2/3 & 2/3 \\\\ 0 & 2/3 & 2/3 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>.

Eigenvalues: <Eq>{`\\lambda_1 = 8/3`}</Eq>, <Eq>{`\\lambda_2 = 4/3`}</Eq>, <Eq>{`\\lambda_3 = 0`}</Eq>. Total variance: <Eq>{`8/3 + 4/3 = 4`}</Eq>.

Explained variance: PC1 = 8/3 / 4 = 66.7%; PC2 = 4/3 / 4 = 33.3%; PC3 = 0%.

**Verification.** <Eq>{`\\text{tr}(\\Sigma) = 8/3 + 2/3 + 2/3 = 12/3 = 4 = \\lambda_1 + \\lambda_2 + \\lambda_3`}</Eq>. $\checkmark$ With 2 PCs explains 100% — data lives in a 2D plane.

**Source.** [Understanding Linear Algebra, §7.1 Exercise 7.1.1](https://understandinglinearalgebra.org/chap7.html) — David Austin · CC-BY-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Computing scores and reconstruction" numero="2">

**Problem.** Centered 2D data with covariance <Eq>{`\\Sigma = \\begin{pmatrix} 4 & 2 \\\\ 2 & 1 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>. Find the PCs, compute the score of sample <Eq>{`x = (2, 1)^T`}</Eq> in PC1, and reconstruct with 1 component.

**Strategy.** Eigendecomposition of <Eq>{`\\Sigma`}</Eq>, then projection and reconstruction.

**Solution.**

Characteristic polynomial: <Eq>{`(4 - \\lambda)(1 - \\lambda) - 4 = 0`}</Eq>, so <Eq>{`\\lambda^2 - 5\\lambda = 0`}</Eq>, giving <Eq>{`\\lambda_1 = 5`}</Eq> and <Eq>{`\\lambda_2 = 0`}</Eq>.

Eigenvector of <Eq>{`\\lambda_1 = 5`}</Eq>: <Eq>{`v_1 = (1/\\sqrt{5})(2, 1)^T`}</Eq>.

Score of <Eq>{`x = (2,1)^T`}</Eq> in PC1: <Eq>{`z_1 = v_1^T x = (2 \\cdot 2 + 1 \\cdot 1)/\\sqrt{5} = 5/\\sqrt{5} = \\sqrt{5}`}</Eq>.

Reconstruction with 1 component: <Eq>{`\\hat x = z_1 v_1 = \\sqrt{5} \\cdot (1/\\sqrt{5})(2,1)^T = (2,1)^T`}</Eq>.

**Verification.** Reconstruction error zero — the datum <Eq>{`x`}</Eq> lives exactly in the PC1 subspace (since <Eq>{`\\lambda_2 = 0`}</Eq>, data is degenerate). $\checkmark$

**Source.** [Introduction to Applied Linear Algebra (VMLS), §10.2](https://web.stanford.edu/~boyd/vmls/) — Boyd, Vandenberghe · CC-BY-NC-ND.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Explained variance and scree plot" numero="3">

**Problem.** A PCA analysis of 50 variables results in the 5 largest eigenvalues: 15, 8, 4, 2, 1. Total variance = 50 (standardized data, each variable has variance 1). Compute: (a) explained variance by each PC; (b) cumulative explained variance; (c) which K to choose for 90%.

**Strategy.** Simple proportions, then cumulative sum.

**Solution.**

(a) ExpVar(k): 15/50 = 30%, 8/50 = 16%, 4/50 = 8%, 2/50 = 4%, 1/50 = 2%.

(b) Cumulative: 30%, 46%, 54%, 58%, 60%.

(c) After 5 PCs, cumulative = 60% — insufficient for 90%. The remaining 45 PCs sum to 40%. To reach 90% probably need ~15–20 PCs (depending on remaining eigenvalue distribution).

**Verification.** Sum of 5 portions = 60%, coherent with remaining total variance of 40%. $\checkmark$

**Source.** [OpenIntro Statistics, §8.3 Exercise 8.27](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/) — Diez, Çetinkaya-Rundel, Barr · CC-BY-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="PCA via SVD and connection with covariance" numero="4">

**Problem.** Show that the PCs obtained via SVD of <Eq>{`\\tilde X`}</Eq> are the same as those from eigendecomposition of <Eq>{`\\Sigma = \\tilde X^T \\tilde X / (N-1)`}</Eq>. Identify how the singular values of <Eq>{`\\tilde X`}</Eq> relate to the eigenvalues of <Eq>{`\\Sigma`}</Eq>.

**Strategy.** Use <Eq>{`\\tilde X = U \\Sigma_{SVD} V^T`}</Eq> and substitute into <Eq>{`\\Sigma`}</Eq>.

**Solution.**

<Eq>{`\\Sigma_{cov} = \\frac{1}{N-1} \\tilde X^T \\tilde X = \\frac{1}{N-1}(V \\Sigma_{SVD}^T U^T)(U \\Sigma_{SVD} V^T)`}</Eq>

<Eq>{`= \\frac{1}{N-1} V \\Sigma_{SVD}^T \\Sigma_{SVD} V^T = V \\cdot \\frac{\\Sigma_{SVD}^2}{N-1} \\cdot V^T`}</Eq>

Therefore: eigenvalues of <Eq>{`\\Sigma_{cov}`}</Eq> are <Eq>{`\\lambda_k = \\sigma_k^2/(N-1)`}</Eq>, and eigenvectors (columns of <Eq>{`V`}</Eq>) are exactly the right singular vectors of <Eq>{`\\tilde X`}</Eq>.

**Verification.** The relation <Eq>{`\\lambda_k = \\sigma_k^2/(N-1)`}</Eq> is consistent: if <Eq>{`\\sigma_k \\to 0`}</Eq>, then <Eq>{`\\lambda_k \\to 0`}</Eq> (PC with no variance). $\checkmark$

**Source.** [Introduction to Applied Linear Algebra (VMLS), §10.3](https://web.stanford.edu/~boyd/vmls/) — Boyd, Vandenberghe · CC-BY-NC-ND.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="PCA applied to Brazilian stock returns" numero="5">

**Problem.** You have daily returns of 5 B3 stocks (PETR4, VALE3, ITUB4, BBDC4, WEGE3) over 252 days. The annualized covariance matrix (in %) is:

<Eq>{`\\Sigma = \\begin{pmatrix} 45 & 20 & 12 & 11 & 8 \\\\ 20 & 38 & 10 & 9 & 6 \\\\ 12 & 10 & 22 & 14 & 5 \\\\ 11 & 9 & 14 & 20 & 4 \\\\ 8 & 6 & 5 & 4 & 15 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>

The 3 largest eigenvalues are approximately 82, 23, 11. Compute: (a) total variance; (b) explained variance by the 3 PCs; (c) economic interpretation.

**Strategy.** Total variance = trace of <Eq>{`\\Sigma`}</Eq>; explained variance = eigenvalue proportion.

**Solution.**

(a) Total variance: <Eq>{`\\text{tr}(\\Sigma) = 45 + 38 + 22 + 20 + 15 = 140`}</Eq>.

(b) 3 PCs explain: <Eq>{`(82 + 23 + 11)/140 = 116/140 \\approx 82{,}9\\%`}</Eq>.

(c) PC1 (82/140 = 58.6%): positively correlated with all stocks — market factor ("beta"). PC2 (23/140 = 16.4%): probably differentiates commodities (PETR4, VALE3) from financials (ITUB4, BBDC4). PC3: remaining idiosyncratic risk.

**Verification.** 3 of 5 components explain 82.9% — consistent with empirical studies of Brazilian yield curves showing dominance of first PCs.

**Source.** [OpenIntro Statistics, §8.3](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/) — Diez et al. · CC-BY-SA. Covariance structure motivated by empirical results from emerging markets.

</Exemplo>
