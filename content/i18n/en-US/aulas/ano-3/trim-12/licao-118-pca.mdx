<Porta nivel="formal" titulo="Mathematical definition">

### Setup and sample covariance

<Definicao titulo="Centered data and covariance matrix">

Data <Eq>{`X \\in \\mathbb{R}^{N \\times d}`}</Eq> (<Eq>{`N`}</Eq> samples, <Eq>{`d`}</Eq> features). Center:

<Equation explicacao="Subtracting the mean from each feature removes the constant component and makes the data have zero mean." latex="\tilde{X} = X - \mathbf{1}\bar{x}^T, \quad \bar{x} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N x_i" />

**Sample covariance matrix:**

<Equation explicacao="Sigma is symmetric positive semidefinite (PSD): all eigenvalues are non-negative." latex="\Sigma = \frac{1}{N-1}\tilde{X}^T \tilde{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}" />

</Definicao>

> "The covariance matrix $\Sigma$ is always symmetric and positive semidefinite. Its eigenvalues are nonnegative and the eigenvectors form an orthonormal basis of $\mathbb{R}^d$." — [Introduction to Applied Linear Algebra (VMLS), §10.1](https://web.stanford.edu/~boyd/vmls/)

### Principal components

<Definicao titulo="PCA: spectral decomposition of covariance">

<Equation explicacao="Sigma is diagonalizable (symmetric PSD): V orthogonal, Lambda diagonal with eigenvalues in decreasing order." latex="\Sigma = V \Lambda V^T, \quad \Lambda = \text{diag}(\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_d), \quad \lambda_1 \geq \cdots \geq \lambda_d \geq 0" />

- **Principal vector** <Eq>{`k`}</Eq>: <Eq>{`v_k`}</Eq> — <Eq>{`k`}</Eq>-th column of <Eq>{`V`}</Eq>.
- **Score** of sample <Eq>{`x_i`}</Eq> on PC <Eq>{`k`}</Eq>: <Eq>{`z_{ik} = v_k^T(x_i - \\bar{x})`}</Eq>.
- **Explained variance** by PC <Eq>{`k`}</Eq>: <Eq>{`\\lambda_k`}</Eq>.
- **Proportion of explained variance**: <Eq>{`\\lambda_k / \\sum_j \\lambda_j`}</Eq>.
- **Total variance**: <Eq>{`\\sum_j \\lambda_j = \\text{tr}(\\Sigma)`}</Eq>.

</Definicao>

### Optimality

<Teorema titulo="PCA maximizes projected variance (Hotelling 1933)">

The first principal component <Eq>{`v_1`}</Eq> is the direction that maximizes the variance of the projection:

<Equation explicacao="Among all unit directions, v1 is the one for which the projected data has the largest variance." latex="v_1 = \arg\max_{\|v\|=1} v^T \Sigma v" />

The <Eq>{`k`}</Eq>-th PC maximizes the remaining variance subject to being orthogonal to <Eq>{`v_1, \\ldots, v_{k-1}`}</Eq>. This is a Rayleigh quotient problem — the solution is the <Eq>{`k`}</Eq>-th eigenvector of <Eq>{`\\Sigma`}</Eq>.

</Teorema>

> "The principal components are the eigenvectors of the data covariance matrix, ordered by decreasing eigenvalue. The first principal component captures the maximum variance; successive components capture maximum residual variance subject to orthogonality." — [Understanding Linear Algebra, §7.1](https://understandinglinearalgebra.org/chap7.html)

### Connection to SVD

<Definicao titulo="PCA via SVD of centered data matrix">

If <Eq>{`\\tilde{X} = U \\Sigma_{SVD} V^T`}</Eq> (SVD of centered matrix):

- **Principal components**: columns of <Eq>{`V`}</Eq> (same as from <Eq>{`\\Sigma_{cov}`}</Eq>).
- **Scores**: <Eq>{`Z = \\tilde{X} V = U \\Sigma_{SVD}`}</Eq>.
- **Covariance eigenvalues**: <Eq>{`\\lambda_k = \\sigma_k^2 / (N-1)`}</Eq>.

SVD of <Eq>{`\\tilde{X}`}</Eq> is **numerically more stable** than decomposing <Eq>{`\\Sigma_{cov}`}</Eq> explicitly (avoids the square-up that amplifies rounding errors).

</Definicao>

### Reconstruction and approximation error

<Equation explicacao="Retaining K components minimizes mean-squared reconstruction error among all rank-K projections (Eckart-Young applied to PCA)." latex="\hat{x}_i = \bar{x} + \sum_{k=1}^K z_{ik}\, v_k" />

Reconstruction error: <Eq>{`\\frac{1}{N}\\sum_i \\|x_i - \\hat{x}_i\\|^2 = \\sum_{k > K} \\lambda_k`}</Eq>.

<Insight>

The **scree plot** plots <Eq>{`\\lambda_k`}</Eq> vs <Eq>{`k`}</Eq>. The "elbow" of the curve indicates where additional variance per component becomes negligible. Rule of thumb: choose <Eq>{`K`}</Eq> such that cumulative variance <Eq>{`\\geq 95\\%`}</Eq> for quantitative analysis, <Eq>{`\\geq 80\\%`}</Eq> for visualization.

</Insight>

Imagine you have a **box of balls** scattered on the floor in the shape of a **cigar** — long in one direction, thin in another.

A **principal component** is just the **"long way" of the cigar** — the main direction where the balls are most spread out.

If I ask you to describe where each ball is with **one single number**, the best number is "how far along the cigar". The other directions matter much less.

PCA finds this "cigar axis" automatically, even in 100 or 1000 dimensions where you can't see.

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

You have data on **height and weight** of 1,000 people. Each person is a point on a graph with 2 axes. But height and weight are **highly correlated**: tall people tend to be heavier.

The points form an **elongated cloud** — like a tilted cigar. PCA finds:

- **Main direction**: the direction of the cigar (mix of height and weight). Captures 90%+ of the variation.
- **Secondary direction**: perpendicular. Captures individual deviations.

If you project each person only onto the **main direction**, you lose little — most of the variation is there. You reduced from 2D to 1D.

In real problems with 100 features, PCA finds the **5-10 main directions** that capture almost all the variation. **Drastically reduces** data size without losing much information.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old">

PCA is the most widely used exploratory analysis tool in data science. **Whenever you hear "dimensionality reduction"**, it's probably PCA or a variant.

### 6-step recipe

1. **Collect data**: matrix <Eq>{`X \\in \\mathbb{R}^{N \\times d}`}</Eq>.
2. **Center**: subtract the mean from each column: <Eq>{`\\tilde{X} = X - \\bar{X}`}</Eq>.
3. **Compute SVD**: <Eq>{`\\tilde{X} = U \\Sigma V^T`}</Eq> (or eigendecomposition of <Eq>{`\\Sigma_{cov}`}</Eq>).
4. **Scree plot**: plot <Eq>{`\\sigma_k^2`}</Eq> as a function of <Eq>{`k`}</Eq>.
5. **Choose <Eq>{`K`}</Eq>**: where the curve "elbows" or cumulative variance <Eq>{`\\geq 95\\%`}</Eq>.
6. **Project**: <Eq>{`Z = \\tilde{X} V_K`}</Eq> are the scores on the <Eq>{`K`}</Eq> PCs.

### Explained variance

<Eq>{`\\text{ExpVar}(k) = \\lambda_k / \\sum_j \\lambda_j`}</Eq>. Cumulative: <Eq>{`\\sum_{k=1}^K \\text{ExpVar}(k)`}</Eq>.

### When PCA works well vs poorly

| Works | Doesn't work |
|---|---|
| Correlated variables | Non-linear structure |
| Similar scales | Strong outliers (use Robust PCA) |
| Want 2D visualization | Want classification (use LDA) |

### Classic case: Iris Dataset (Fisher, 1936)

4 features (petal and sepal length and width). PCA reduces to 2D while keeping 95%+ of the variance. The visualization reveals 3 clusters of species clearly separated — Fisher showed this back in 1936.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

PCA is the first tool in any data pipeline. Understanding it deeply (not just the recipe) is essential.

### Optimality: proof via Rayleigh quotient

We want <Eq>{`v^* = \\arg\\max_{\\|v\\|=1} v^T \\Sigma v`}</Eq>. Using Lagrange multipliers:

<Eq>{`\\mathcal{L}(v, \\mu) = v^T \\Sigma v - \\mu(\\|v\\|^2 - 1)`}</Eq>

Taking the derivative with respect to <Eq>{`v`}</Eq> and setting to zero: <Eq>{`2\\Sigma v = 2\\mu v`}</Eq>, that is <Eq>{`\\Sigma v = \\mu v`}</Eq>. So <Eq>{`v^*`}</Eq> is an eigenvector of <Eq>{`\\Sigma`}</Eq> and the maximum is <Eq>{`\\mu = v^{*T} \\Sigma v^* = \\lambda_1`}</Eq> (largest eigenvalue).

### Typical ML pipeline

```
raw data → standardize → PCA(K components) → model(kNN, SVM, NN, ...)
```

Reduces dimension → reduces overfitting + speeds up training.

### Canonical applications

**Genomics**: microarray with 30,000 genes × 100 patients. PCA reduces to ~10 dimensions → clusters of cancer subtypes (Golub et al. 1999).

**Finance**: yield curve — 3 PCs (level, slope, curvature) capture ~99% of variance (Litterman-Scheinkman 1991). Basis of all fixed-income trading.

**Computer vision**: Eigenfaces (Turk-Pentland 1991) — PCA of centered faces → bases for facial recognition.

**Bioinformatics**: 1000 Genomes Project — PCA of alleles reveals geographic structure (clusters by continent).

### Standardization: classic pitfall

If features have different scales (e.g., salary in reais vs age in years), **without standardizing** the salary dominates variance. Always z-score before PCA unless scales are deliberately comparable.

### Python code

```python
import numpy as np
from sklearn.decomposition import PCA

# Standardize (ALWAYS when scales differ)
X_std = (X - X.mean(axis=0)) / X.std(axis=0, ddof=1)

# PCA
pca = PCA(n_components=10)
Z = pca.fit_transform(X_std)

print("Explained variance:", pca.explained_variance_ratio_)
print("Cumulative:", np.cumsum(pca.explained_variance_ratio_))

# Loadings (eigenvectors)
print("Components (loadings):", pca.components_.shape)
```

### Orthogonality of scores

The PCs are orthogonal by construction: <Eq>{`\\text{Cov}(z_j, z_k) = 0`}</Eq> for <Eq>{`j \\neq k`}</Eq>. This eliminates multicollinearity — scores can be used directly as features in linear models.

### Important variants

| Variant | When to use |
|---|---|
| Kernel PCA | Non-linear structure in data |
| Sparse PCA | Want interpretable components |
| Robust PCA | Data with outliers (Candès-Li-Ma-Wright) |
| Incremental PCA | Streaming data |
| Probabilistic PCA | Missing data, Bayesian inference |

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

PCA is not "just" dimensionality reduction — it is the optimal representation theorem for data with second-moment structure.

### Historical context

Karl Pearson (1901) introduced the idea of "lines of best fit" in multivariate data — what we now call PC1. Harold Hotelling (1933) formalized the complete method for analyzing multiple components in the context of psychometric variables. The connection to SVD is from Eckart-Young (1936). The name "principal component analysis" is Hotelling's.

For 50 years, PCA was mainly multivariate statistics (Pearson, Fisher, Hotelling, Rao). In the 1980s–90s, application in computer vision (Eigenfaces, Turk-Pentland 1991) and signal processing transformed PCA into the standard preprocessing tool in ML.

### Connection to information theory

PCA minimizes information lost in compression when "loss" is measured by mean-squared error. For Gaussian distributions, PCA is **optimal** in Shannon's sense: it maximizes entropy of scores while preserving total variance. For non-Gaussian distributions, Independent Component Analysis (ICA) may be better.

### PCA and statistical inference

How many components to retain? Formal criteria:

- **Kaiser criterion**: retain PCs with <Eq>{`\\lambda_k > 1`}</Eq> (variance above unit, if data standardized).
- **Scree test**: cut at visible "elbow".
- **Marchenko-Pastur distribution**: for pure noise (<Eq>{`X`}</Eq> Gaussian i.i.d.), eigenvalues of <Eq>{`\\Sigma_{cov}`}</Eq> follow the MP distribution. Retain PCs whose <Eq>{`\\lambda_k`}</Eq> exceeds the upper limit of this distribution.
- **Bootstrap**: resample the data and check stability of PCs.

### PCA and Transformers

The multi-head attention in Transformers (Vaswani 2017) can be interpreted as learned PCA via gradient: the product <Eq>{`Q K^T`}</Eq> computes correlations between tokens; softmax normalizes to a distribution; product with <Eq>{`V`}</Eq> is a weighted projection. Essentially, each head learns a subspace projection that resembles PCA with context-dependent weights.

Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA, Hu 2022) — fine-tuning of LLMs — justifies the low-rank perturbation <Eq>{`\\Delta W = BA`}</Eq> precisely because pre-trained weights live on a manifold of low intrinsic dimensionality, analogous to Eckart-Young rank-<Eq>{`K`}</Eq> approximation.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practical application">

### PCA on ENEM micro-data

ENEM has ~200 variables per test-taker (scores, socioeconomic questionnaire, school). PCA on the test-taker × variable matrix:

1. **Center and standardize** (z-score by column).
2. **Compute SVD** of <Eq>{`\\tilde X`}</Eq>.
3. **Scree plot**: identify how many PCs explain 80%, 90%, 95%.
4. **Interpret PC1**: usually the "general performance factor".
5. **Interpret PC2**: usually "school type" (public vs private).
6. **Visualize** in 2D (PC1 × PC2): reveals regional and socioeconomic structure.

INEP researchers do exactly this for equity reports.

### PCA in portfolio management

Given <Eq>{`n`}</Eq> stocks with daily returns:

1. Calculate correlation matrix <Eq>{`R`}</Eq> (covariance of standardized returns).
2. PCA of <Eq>{`R`}</Eq>: PC1 is usually the "market" (all correlated).
3. PC2 might be "sector" (financials vs technology vs commodities).
4. **Non-systematic risk**: variance explained by the later PCs.
5. **Hedge**: short PC1 eliminates market risk (beta hedge).

Institutional managers (banks, funds) use PCA for **risk attribution** — decompose portfolio risk into systematic (PCs) and idiosyncratic components.

</Porta>

## Worked Examples

<Exemplo titulo="PCA of a simple 3D dataset" numero="1">

**Problem.** Given <Eq>{`\\tilde{X} = \\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 & 0 \\\\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\\\ 0 & -1 & -1 \\\\ -2 & 0 & 0 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq> (4 samples, 3 features, already centered), compute the covariance matrix, eigenvalues, and explained variance.

**Strategy.** Compute <Eq>{`\\Sigma = \\tilde{X}^T \\tilde{X} / (N-1)`}</Eq> and decompose.

**Solution.**

<Eq>{`\\tilde{X}^T \\tilde{X} = \\begin{pmatrix} 8 & 0 & 0 \\\\ 0 & 2 & 2 \\\\ 0 & 2 & 2 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>. With <Eq>{`N - 1 = 3`}</Eq>: <Eq>{`\\Sigma = \\begin{pmatrix} 8/3 & 0 & 0 \\\\ 0 & 2/3 & 2/3 \\\\ 0 & 2/3 & 2/3 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>.

Eigenvalues: <Eq>{`\\lambda_1 = 8/3`}</Eq>, <Eq>{`\\lambda_2 = 4/3`}</Eq>, <Eq>{`\\lambda_3 = 0`}</Eq>. Total variance: <Eq>{`8/3 + 4/3 = 4`}</Eq>.

Explained variance: PC1 = 8/3 / 4 = 66.7%; PC2 = 4/3 / 4 = 33.3%; PC3 = 0%.

**Check.** <Eq>{`\\text{tr}(\\Sigma) = 8/3 + 2/3 + 2/3 = 12/3 = 4 = \\lambda_1 + \\lambda_2 + \\lambda_3`}</Eq>. ✓ With 2 PCs explains 100% — data live on a 2D plane.

**Source.** [Understanding Linear Algebra, §7.1 Exercise 7.1.1](https://understandinglinearalgebra.org/chap7.html) — David Austin · CC-BY-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Computing scores and reconstruction" numero="2">

**Problem.** Centered 2D data with covariance <Eq>{`\\Sigma = \\begin{pmatrix} 4 & 2 \\\\ 2 & 1 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>. Find the PCs, compute the score of sample <Eq>{`x = (2, 1)^T`}</Eq> on PC1, and reconstruct with 1 component.

**Strategy.** Eigendecomposition of <Eq>{`\\Sigma`}</Eq>, then projection and reconstruction.

**Solution.**

Characteristic polynomial: <Eq>{`(4 - \\lambda)(1 - \\lambda) - 4 = 0`}</Eq>, so <Eq>{`\\lambda^2 - 5\\lambda = 0`}</Eq>, giving <Eq>{`\\lambda_1 = 5`}</Eq> and <Eq>{`\\lambda_2 = 0`}</Eq>.

Eigenvector of <Eq>{`\\lambda_1 = 5`}</Eq>: <Eq>{`v_1 = (1/\\sqrt{5})(2, 1)^T`}</Eq>.

Score of <Eq>{`x = (2,1)^T`}</Eq> on PC1: <Eq>{`z_1 = v_1^T x = (2 \\cdot 2 + 1 \\cdot 1)/\\sqrt{5} = 5/\\sqrt{5} = \\sqrt{5}`}</Eq>.

Reconstruction with 1 component: <Eq>{`\\hat x = z_1 v_1 = \\sqrt{5} \\cdot (1/\\sqrt{5})(2,1)^T = (2,1)^T`}</Eq>.

**Check.** Zero reconstruction error — the data point <Eq>{`x`}</Eq> lives exactly on the PC1 subspace (since <Eq>{`\\lambda_2 = 0`}</Eq>, data are degenerate). ✓

**Source.** [Introduction to Applied Linear Algebra (VMLS), §10.2](https://web.stanford.edu/~boyd/vmls/) — Boyd, Vandenberghe · CC-BY-NC-ND.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Explained variance and scree plot" numero="3">

**Problem.** A PCA analysis of 50 variables yields the 5 largest eigenvalues: 15, 8, 4, 2, 1. Total variance = 50 (standardized data, each variable has variance 1). Compute: (a) explained variance by each PC; (b) cumulative explained variance; (c) which K for 90%.

**Strategy.** Simple proportion, then cumulative sum.

**Solution.**

(a) ExpVar(k): 15/50 = 30%, 8/50 = 16%, 4/50 = 8%, 2/50 = 4%, 1/50 = 2%.

(b) Cumulative: 30%, 46%, 54%, 58%, 60%.

(c) After 5 PCs, cumulative = 60% — insufficient for 90%. The remaining 45 PCs sum to 40%. For 90% we'd probably need ~15-20 PCs (depending on the distribution of the remaining eigenvalues).

**Check.** Sum of 5 proportions = 60%, consistent with remaining variance of 40%. ✓

**Source.** [OpenIntro Statistics, §8.3 Exercise 8.27](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/) — Diez, Çetinkaya-Rundel, Barr · CC-BY-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="PCA via SVD and connection to covariance" numero="4">

**Problem.** Show that the PCs obtained via SVD of <Eq>{`\\tilde X`}</Eq> are the same as those from eigendecomposition of <Eq>{`\\Sigma = \\tilde X^T \\tilde X / (N-1)`}</Eq>. Identify how singular values of <Eq>{`\\tilde X`}</Eq> relate to eigenvalues of <Eq>{`\\Sigma`}</Eq>.

**Strategy.** Use <Eq>{`\\tilde X = U \\Sigma_{SVD} V^T`}</Eq> and substitute into <Eq>{`\\Sigma`}</Eq>.

**Solution.**

<Eq>{`\\Sigma_{cov} = \\frac{1}{N-1} \\tilde X^T \\tilde X = \\frac{1}{N-1}(V \\Sigma_{SVD}^T U^T)(U \\Sigma_{SVD} V^T)`}</Eq>

<Eq>{`= \\frac{1}{N-1} V \\Sigma_{SVD}^T \\Sigma_{SVD} V^T = V \\cdot \\frac{\\Sigma_{SVD}^2}{N-1} \\cdot V^T`}</Eq>

Therefore: eigenvalues of <Eq>{`\\Sigma_{cov}`}</Eq> are <Eq>{`\\lambda_k = \\sigma_k^2/(N-1)`}</Eq>, and the eigenvectors (columns of <Eq>{`V`}</Eq>) are exactly the right singular vectors of <Eq>{`\\tilde X`}</Eq>.

**Check.** The relation <Eq>{`\\lambda_k = \\sigma_k^2/(N-1)`}</Eq> is consistent: if <Eq>{`\\sigma_k \\to 0`}</Eq>, then <Eq>{`\\lambda_k \\to 0`}</Eq> (PC with no variance). ✓

**Source.** [Introduction to Applied Linear Algebra (VMLS), §10.3](https://web.stanford.edu/~boyd/vmls/) — Boyd, Vandenberghe · CC-BY-NC-ND.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="PCA applied to Brazilian stock returns" numero="5">

**Problem.** You have daily returns of 5 B3 stocks (PETR4, VALE3, ITUB4, BBDC4, WEGE3) over 252 days. The annualized covariance matrix (in %) is:

<Eq>{`\\Sigma = \\begin{pmatrix} 45 & 20 & 12 & 11 & 8 \\\\ 20 & 38 & 10 & 9 & 6 \\\\ 12 & 10 & 22 & 14 & 5 \\\\ 11 & 9 & 14 & 20 & 4 \\\\ 8 & 6 & 5 & 4 & 15 \\end{pmatrix}`}</Eq>

The 3 largest eigenvalues are approximately 82, 23, 11. Compute: (a) total variance; (b) explained variance by the 3 PCs; (c) economic interpretation.

**Strategy.** Total variance = trace of <Eq>{`\\Sigma`}</Eq>; explained variance = proportion of eigenvalues.

**Solution.**

(a) Total variance: <Eq>{`\\text{tr}(\\Sigma) = 45 + 38 + 22 + 20 + 15 = 140`}</Eq>.

(b) 3 PCs explain: <Eq>{`(82 + 23 + 11)/140 = 116/140 \\approx 82.9\\%`}</Eq>.

(c) PC1 (82/140 = 58.6%): positively correlated with all stocks — market factor ("beta"). PC2 (23/140 = 16.4%): probably differentiates commodities (PETR4, VALE3) from financials (ITUB4, BBDC4). PC3: remaining idiosyncratic risk.

**Check.** 3 of 5 components explain 82.9% — consistent with empirical studies of Brazilian yield curves showing dominance of the first PCs.

**Source.** [OpenIntro Statistics, §8.3](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/) — Diez et al. · CC-BY-SA. Covariance structure motivated by empirical results from emerging markets.

</Exemplo>
