### Fundamental definition

<Definicao titulo="Diagonalizable matrix">

Let $A \in \mathcal{M}_n(\mathbb{K})$. We say that $A$ is **diagonalizable** (over $\mathbb{K}$) if there exists an invertible matrix $P$ such that

<Equation explicacao="Spectral decomposition: P has the eigenvectors as columns, D is diagonal with the eigenvalues." latex="A = PDP^{-1}, \qquad D = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \ldots, \lambda_n)" />

Equivalently, $AP = PD$ — each column $p_i$ of $P$ satisfies $Ap_i = \lambda_i p_i$, that is, is an eigenvector of $A$ with eigenvalue $\lambda_i$.

</Definicao>

> "A matrix $A$ is diagonalizable if it is similar to a diagonal matrix — there exists an invertible $P$ such that $P^{-1}AP$ is diagonal." — [Beezer, A First Course in Linear Algebra, §SD](http://linear.ups.edu/html/section-SD.html)

### Equivalent conditions

<Teorema titulo="Criterion of diagonalizability">

The following statements are equivalent for $A \in \mathcal{M}_n(\mathbb{K})$:

1. $A$ is diagonalizable.
2. $A$ possesses $n$ linearly independent eigenvectors.
3. The sum of the dimensions of the eigenspaces equals $n$: $\sum_\lambda \dim E_\lambda = n$.
4. For each eigenvalue $\lambda$, the geometric multiplicity equals the algebraic multiplicity: $m_g(\lambda) = m_a(\lambda)$.
5. $\mathbb{K}^n = \bigoplus_\lambda E_\lambda$ — the space is a direct sum of eigenspaces.

**Proof of equivalence 2 $\Leftrightarrow$ 1.** If $\{v_1, \ldots, v_n\}$ are $n$ linearly independent eigenvectors with eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_n$, form $P = [v_1 \mid \cdots \mid v_n]$. Since the columns are LI, $P$ is invertible. Calculate:

$$AP = A[v_1 \mid \cdots \mid v_n] = [\lambda_1 v_1 \mid \cdots \mid \lambda_n v_n] = [v_1 \mid \cdots \mid v_n]\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_i) = PD$$

Thus $P^{-1}AP = D$. The converse is immediate: if $A = PDP^{-1}$, the columns of $P$ are linearly independent eigenvectors. $\square$

</Teorema>

> "An $n \times n$ matrix $A$ is diagonalizable if and only if $A$ has $n$ linearly independent eigenvectors." — [Beezer, A First Course in Linear Algebra, §SD Theorem DED](http://linear.ups.edu/html/section-SD.html)

### Diagonalization algorithm

1. Compute the characteristic polynomial $p_A(\lambda) = \det(A - \lambda I)$ and find the roots $\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_k$ with algebraic multiplicities $m_a(\lambda_i)$.
2. For each $\lambda_i$, solve $(A - \lambda_i I)v = 0$ and find a basis for $E_{\lambda_i} = \ker(A - \lambda_i I)$. Verify $m_g(\lambda_i) = \dim E_{\lambda_i}$.
3. If $\sum m_g(\lambda_i) = n$: form $P$ with the eigenvectors as columns and $D = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_n)$ (respecting column order).
4. If $\sum m_g(\lambda_i) < n$: $A$ is not diagonalizable — resort to Jordan form.

### Immediate applications

<Insight>

**$A^k$ in one multiplication.** If $A = PDP^{-1}$, then $A^k = PD^kP^{-1}$ because $(PDP^{-1})^k = PD(P^{-1}P)D\cdots DP^{-1} = PD^kP^{-1}$. And $D^k = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1^k, \ldots, \lambda_n^k)$ is trivial. This reduces $k$ matrix multiplications $O(n^3)$ to **a single** composition.

</Insight>

<Equation explicacao="Matrix power via diagonalization: D^k has the eigenvalues raised to k on the diagonal." latex="A^k = P D^k P^{-1}, \quad D^k = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1^k, \ldots, \lambda_n^k)" />

<Equation explicacao="Matrix exponential: each eigenvalue lambda_i generates e^{lambda_i t} on the diagonal." latex="e^{At} = P e^{Dt} P^{-1}, \quad e^{Dt} = \operatorname{diag}(e^{\lambda_1 t}, \ldots, e^{\lambda_n t})" />

For any analytic function $f$: $f(A) = Pf(D)P^{-1}$ with $f(D) = \operatorname{diag}(f(\lambda_i))$.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

Imagine a **messy box** with toys all mixed together. If you separate by color — red ones in a pile, blue ones in another — it becomes much easier to count and play.

**Diagonalizing** a matrix is exactly that: organizing mathematical objects into **separate piles** called eigenvectors. Each pile has a special number (the eigenvalue) that says "how much each thing of this color grows."

After organized, doing many operations at once becomes simple arithmetic: instead of dealing with everything together, you only multiply each pile by its number — independent of the others.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

Diagonalizing a matrix is like **putting on the right glasses** to see better.

Without glasses, $A$ looks complicated — numbers everywhere, everything mixed up. With the right glasses (the change of basis to eigenvectors), you see $D$: a matrix with numbers only on the diagonal and zeros everywhere else.

**Why is it useful?** Imagine you want to calculate $A^{100}$ — multiply $A$ by itself 100 times. Without the glasses: lots of work. With the glasses:

$$A^{100} = P D^{100} P^{-1}$$

And $D^{100}$ is easy — just raise each number on the diagonal to the power 100. Done.

$P$ is the "put on glasses" and $P^{-1}$ is the "take off glasses."

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old teen">

Diagonalization is the **transformation that reveals** what the matrix really does. Instead of seeing numbers mixed together, you see independent directions of stretching.

### Why is this powerful?

Any linear iteration $x_{k+1} = Ax_k$ has a closed-form solution via diagonalization. Write $x_0 = \sum c_i v_i$ (combination of eigenvectors of $A$). Then:

$$x_k = A^k x_0 = \sum_{i=1}^n c_i \lambda_i^k v_i$$

Each component grows or decays independently, with rate $\lambda_i$. These are the **natural modes** of the system.

### Classical application: Binet's formula

The Fibonacci recurrence $F_{n+1} = F_n + F_{n-1}$ in matrix form:

$$\begin{pmatrix} F_{n+1} \\ F_n \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}^n \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$$

Diagonalizing the matrix (eigenvalues $\phi = (1+\sqrt{5})/2$ and $\psi = (1-\sqrt{5})/2$):

$$F_n = \frac{\phi^n - \psi^n}{\sqrt{5}}$$

— the **closed formula of Binet** — without computing $F_1, F_2, \ldots, F_{n-1}$.

### When does it NOT diagonalize?

When $m_g(\lambda) < m_a(\lambda)$ for some $\lambda$. Classic example:

$$\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$$

Eigenvalue 2 with $m_a = 2$, but $m_g = 1$ (only one independent eigenvector). Does not diagonalize over $\mathbb{R}$ nor $\mathbb{C}$. In this case, one uses the **Jordan form** (the next big frontier of linear algebra).

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

Diagonalization transforms **coupled algebra into uncoupled algebra**. It is the base operation of all modal analysis in structural engineering, control, and signal processing.

### Application 1: LTI systems — vector differential equations

$\dot{x} = Ax$, $x(0) = x_0$. If $A = PDP^{-1}$, change variable $y = P^{-1}x$:

$$\dot{y} = P^{-1}\dot{x} = P^{-1}AP y = Dy$$

System is **decoupled**: $\dot{y}_i = \lambda_i y_i$ for each $i$. Solution: $y_i(t) = y_i(0)e^{\lambda_i t}$.

Returning to the original variable: $x(t) = Pe^{Dt}P^{-1}x_0 = \sum_{i=1}^n (P^{-1}x_0)_i e^{\lambda_i t} v_i$.

Stability: $x(t) \to 0$ as $t \to \infty$ if and only if $\operatorname{Re}(\lambda_i) < 0$ for all $i$ (**spectral criterion of stability**).

### Application 2: modal analysis in structures

Coupled mass-spring system: $M\ddot{q} + Kq = 0$. Reducing to generalized eigenproblem $Kv = \lambda Mv$, the **normal modes** (eigenvectors) are the vibration shapes and the **natural frequencies** are $\omega_i = \sqrt{\lambda_i}$. Diagonalization decouples the equations — each mode vibrates independently. This is the basis of the modal superposition method (FEM, SAP2000, ANSYS).

### Application 3: Markov chains

Distribution $\pi_{t+1} = P^T \pi_t$. If $P$ is stochastic irreducible and aperiodic, diagonalizing: $\pi_t = P^T{}^t \pi_0$. The rate of convergence to the stationary distribution is governed by the **second largest eigenvalue** $|\lambda_2|$ (spectral gap).

### Computational cost and practical limits

- Complete eigenvalue decomposition: $O(n^3)$ via iterative QR algorithm.
- $A^k$ via diagonalization: $O(n^3)$ once + $O(n^2)$ per step — huge advantage for $k \gg n$.
- **Caution:** if $\kappa(P)$ (conditioning number of $P$) is high, the decomposition is numerically unstable. For symmetric matrices, $P$ is orthogonal ($\kappa(P) = 1$) — perfect stability.
- For sparse matrices, prefer Lanczos (seeks a few eigenvalues, not all).

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

### Jordan form: when diagonalization fails

When $A$ is not diagonalizable, the canonical decomposition is $A = PJP^{-1}$ with $J$ block-diagonal with Jordan blocks:

$$J_{n_i}(\lambda_i) = \lambda_i I_{n_i} + N_{n_i}, \quad N_{n_i} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & & \\ & 0 & \ddots & \\ & & \ddots & 1 \\ & & & 0 \end{pmatrix}$$

where $N_{n_i}^{n_i} = 0$ (nilpotent). Applications of analytic functions: $f(J_n(\lambda)) = \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} \frac{f^{(k)}(\lambda)}{k!} N^k$ — series truncated by nilpotency.

In numerical practice, Jordan is **ill-conditioned** — the basis $P$ can have very high $\kappa(P)$. Instead one uses the **Schur decomposition** $A = QTQ^*$ with $Q$ unitary and $T$ upper triangular: numerically stable, with $\kappa(Q) = 1$.

### Simultaneous diagonalization

$\{A_i\}$ are simultaneously diagonalizable $\iff$ they commute: $A_iA_j = A_jA_i$ for every pair. In quantum mechanics, **compatible observables** ↔ Hermitian operators that commute ↔ can be measured simultaneously (common basis of eigenvectors). Heisenberg's uncertainty principle arises from non-commutativity of $\hat{x}$ and $\hat{p}$.

### Pseudospectrum and non-normal matrices

For non-normal matrices, eigenvalues can be **misleading**: small perturbation can greatly change the spectral location. The $\epsilon$-pseudospectrum is:

$$\sigma_\epsilon(A) = \{z \in \mathbb{C} : \|(zI - A)^{-1}\|_2 > 1/\epsilon\}$$

Trefethen showed that the pseudospectrum explains *transient growth* phenomena in shear flows (subcritical turbulence in Couette-Poiseuille) and instability in recurrent neural networks.

### Connection to Black-Scholes (L119)

The Hessian matrix of sensitivities $\Gamma_{ij} = \partial^2 V/\partial S_i \partial S_j$ in multi-asset portfolios is symmetric positive semidefinite. Diagonalizing it reveals the **principal risk directions** — basis of risk attribution in portfolios (Litterman, RiskMetrics 1994). The eigenvalues are the variances in uncorrelated directions; the eigenvectors are the **orthogonal risk factors**.

### Modern extensions

- **Koopman operator**: lifts nonlinear dynamics to infinite-dimensional observable space and linearizes — diagonalization of the operator (via DMD, Dynamic Mode Decomposition) identifies coherent patterns in fluid and climate data (Schmid 2010).
- **Spectral analysis in deep learning**: skip connections in ResNets keep jacobian eigenvalues near 1, avoiding explosion/vanishing gradients (He et al. 2016).

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practical application">

### Step-by-step recipe

1. Compute the eigenvalues: solve $\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$.
2. For each eigenvalue $\lambda_i$, find basis for $\ker(A - \lambda_i I)$.
3. Check: $\sum \dim E_{\lambda_i} = n$? If yes, it is diagonalizable.
4. Form $P = [v_1 \mid v_2 \mid \cdots \mid v_n]$ and $D = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_n)$.
5. Verify: $AP = PD$ (simpler than computing $PDP^{-1}$ explicitly).

### Practical application: population growth (IBGE)

Leslie model of 2 age groups — young ($J$) and adults ($A$) — with fertility and survival rates typical for a medium-sized Brazilian municipality:

$$M = \begin{pmatrix} 0{,}6 & 1{,}2 \\ 0{,}4 & 0{,}0 \end{pmatrix}$$

To project 10 cycles: $v_{10} = M^{10} v_0$. Via diagonalization, compute once and apply directly — no need to iterate 10 matrix multiplications.

### Control system stability

A control system $\dot{x} = Ax$ is stable if $\operatorname{Re}(\lambda_i) < 0$ for all $i$. To check stability, you don't need to solve the ODE — just diagonalize (or compute the spectrum) of $A$ and verify where the eigenvalues land in the complex plane.

### When NOT to use diagonalization directly

- **$A$ close to defective**: $\kappa(P)$ high → errors amplified. Use Schur.
- **$A$ large and sparse, wanting few modes**: use Lanczos/Arnoldi (MATLAB `eigs`, SciPy `eigsh`).
- **$A$ real symmetric**: always use `np.linalg.eigh` (faster, stable, guarantees $P$ orthogonal).

</Porta>

## Worked examples

<Exemplo titulo="Diagonalizing a 2×2 matrix (basic)" numero="1">

**Problem:** Diagonalize $A = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$.

**Strategy:** Find eigenvalues and eigenvectors, form $P$ and $D$, verify $AP = PD$.

**Solution:**

1. **Characteristic polynomial:**
$$p_A(\lambda) = \det(A - \lambda I) = (4-\lambda)(3-\lambda) - 2 = \lambda^2 - 7\lambda + 10 = (\lambda - 5)(\lambda - 2)$$
Eigenvalues: $\lambda_1 = 5$, $\lambda_2 = 2$.

2. **Eigenvectors:**

For $\lambda_1 = 5$: $(A - 5I)v = 0 \Rightarrow \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 1 \\ 2 & -2 \end{pmatrix}v = 0$. Basis: $v_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$.

For $\lambda_2 = 2$: $(A - 2I)v = 0 \Rightarrow \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}v = 0$. Basis: $v_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -2 \end{pmatrix}$.

3. **Assembly:**
$$P = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -2 \end{pmatrix}, \quad D = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$$

**Verification:** $AP = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 2 \\ 5 & -4 \end{pmatrix}$ and $PD = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 2 \\ 5 & -4 \end{pmatrix}$. Equal. $\checkmark$

**Source.** [Beezer, A First Course in Linear Algebra §SD, Example DMS22](http://linear.ups.edu/html/section-SD.html) — GNU FDL license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Non-diagonalizable matrix (comprehension)" numero="2">

**Problem:** Determine if $A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$ is diagonalizable. Justify.

**Strategy:** Compute algebraic and geometric multiplicities of the sole eigenvalue.

**Solution:**

1. **Eigenvalues:** $p_A(\lambda) = (3-\lambda)^2 = 0$, so $\lambda = 3$ with $m_a = 2$.

2. **Eigenspace:** $(A - 3I) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$. The system $(A - 3I)v = 0$ requires $v_2 = 0$, with $v_1$ free. Basis: $\left\{\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}\right\}$. Therefore $m_g(3) = 1$.

3. **Conclusion:** $m_g(3) = 1 < 2 = m_a(3)$. There are not two linearly independent eigenvectors. **$A$ is not diagonalizable.**

**Verification:** If there existed $P$ with $P^{-1}AP = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix} = 3I$, we would have $A = P(3I)P^{-1} = 3I$. But $A \neq 3I$. Contradiction. $\checkmark$

**Source.** [Beezer, A First Course in Linear Algebra §SD, Example NDMS3](http://linear.ups.edu/html/section-SD.html) — GNU FDL license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Matrix power via diagonalization (intermediate)" numero="3">

**Problem:** Compute $A^{10}$ for $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$.

**Strategy:** Diagonalize $A$, raise $D$ to power 10 (trivial), compose.

**Solution:**

1. **Eigenvalues:** $p_A(\lambda) = (2-\lambda)(1-\lambda) = 0$, so $\lambda_1 = 2$, $\lambda_2 = 1$.

2. **Eigenvectors:**

$\lambda_1 = 2$: $(A - 2I)v = 0 \Rightarrow \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}v = 0$. $v_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$.

$\lambda_2 = 1$: $(A - I)v = 0 \Rightarrow \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}v = 0$. $v_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}$.

3. **Decomposition:** $P = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$, $D = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$.

$P^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$ (verify: $\det P = -1$, so $P^{-1} = \frac{1}{-1}\begin{pmatrix} -1 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$).

4. **Power:** $D^{10} = \begin{pmatrix} 2^{10} & 0 \\ 0 & 1^{10} \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1024 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$.

$$A^{10} = PD^{10}P^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1024 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1024 & 1023 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$$

**Verification:** $A^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 3 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$. By the formula: $A^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 2^2 & 2^2-1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 3 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$. $\checkmark$

**Source.** [Hefferon, Linear Algebra, ch. 5 §II](https://hefferon.net/linearalgebra/) — CC-BY-SA license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Binet's formula for Fibonacci (advanced)" numero="4">

**Problem:** Derive the closed formula of Binet $F_n = \dfrac{\phi^n - \psi^n}{\sqrt{5}}$, where $\phi = \frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2}$ and $\psi = \frac{1-\sqrt{5}}{2}$.

**Strategy:** Rewrite the recurrence as matrix iteration, diagonalize the transition matrix.

**Solution:**

1. **Matrix form:** $\begin{pmatrix} F_{n+1} \\ F_n \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} F_n \\ F_{n-1} \end{pmatrix}$. Let $M = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.

2. **Eigenvalues of $M$:** $\lambda^2 - \lambda - 1 = 0 \Rightarrow \lambda = \frac{1 \pm \sqrt{5}}{2}$, so $\lambda_1 = \phi$, $\lambda_2 = \psi$.

3. **Eigenvectors:** $v_1 = \begin{pmatrix} \phi \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$, $v_2 = \begin{pmatrix} \psi \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$.

4. **Decomposition:** $\begin{pmatrix} F_{n+1} \\ F_n \end{pmatrix} = M^n \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} = PD^nP^{-1}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$.

$P = \begin{pmatrix} \phi & \psi \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, $\det P = \phi - \psi = \sqrt{5}$. $P^{-1} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -\psi \\ -1 & \phi \end{pmatrix}$.

5. **Second component:** $F_n = \frac{\phi^n(\phi - \psi + \psi \cdot \text{...}) - \psi^n(\ldots)}{\sqrt{5}}$. Computing explicitly the second row of $PD^nP^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}$:

$$F_n = \frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}\left(\phi^n \cdot 1 \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}\sqrt{5} - \psi^n \cdot 1 \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}\sqrt{5}\right) = \frac{\phi^n - \psi^n}{\sqrt{5}}$$

**Verification:** $F_1 = \frac{\phi - \psi}{\sqrt{5}} = \frac{\sqrt{5}}{\sqrt{5}} = 1$. $F_2 = \frac{\phi^2 - \psi^2}{\sqrt{5}} = \frac{(\phi-\psi)(\phi+\psi)}{\sqrt{5}} = \frac{\sqrt{5} \cdot 1}{\sqrt{5}} = 1$. $\checkmark$

**Source.** [Beezer, A First Course in Linear Algebra §SD](http://linear.ups.edu/html/section-SD.html) — GNU FDL license.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Stability of dynamical system (modeling)" numero="5">

**Problem:** The system $\dot{x} = Ax$ with $A = \begin{pmatrix} -3 & 1 \\ 1 & -3 \end{pmatrix}$ and $x(0) = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$. Determine $x(t)$ and classify the stability.

**Strategy:** Diagonalize $A$, solve the decoupled system, compose the solution.

**Solution:**

1. **Eigenvalues:** $p_A(\lambda) = (-3-\lambda)^2 - 1 = \lambda^2 + 6\lambda + 8 = (\lambda+4)(\lambda+2) = 0$. $\lambda_1 = -4$, $\lambda_2 = -2$.

2. **Eigenvectors:**

$\lambda_1 = -4$: $(A + 4I) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$. $v_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}$.

$\lambda_2 = -2$: $(A + 2I) = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$. $v_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$.

3. **General solution:** $x(t) = c_1 e^{-4t}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix} + c_2 e^{-2t}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$.

4. **Initial condition:** $x(0) = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} \Rightarrow c_1 + c_2 = 2$ and $-c_1 + c_2 = 0$, so $c_1 = c_2 = 1$.

$$x(t) = e^{-4t}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix} + e^{-2t}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$$

5. **Stability:** Both eigenvalues are negative ($-4 < 0$, $-2 < 0$), so $x(t) \to 0$ as $t \to \infty$. The system is **asymptotically stable**.

**Verification:** $x(0) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$. $\checkmark$

**Source.** [Hefferon, Linear Algebra, ch. 5 §II](https://hefferon.net/linearalgebra/) — CC-BY-SA license.
</Exemplo>
