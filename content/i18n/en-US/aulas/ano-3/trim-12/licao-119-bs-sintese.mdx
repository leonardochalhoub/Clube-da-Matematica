### The model and canonical equation

<Definicao titulo="Geometric Brownian Motion (GBM)">

Black-Scholes assumes that an asset price $S_t$ evolves according to the stochastic process:

$$dS = \mu S\,dt + \sigma S\,dW_t$$

where $\mu$ is the expected return rate, $\sigma > 0$ is the volatility, and $W_t$ is a standard Brownian motion (Wiener process). By Itô's Lemma, the log-return follows a normal distribution:

$$\ln\frac{S_T}{S_0} \sim N\!\left(\left(\mu - \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\right)T,\; \sigma^2 T\right)$$

</Definicao>

> "Black and Scholes (1973) derived the price of a European call option by assuming that the price of the underlying asset follows a geometric Brownian motion with constant drift and volatility, no dividends, and in a frictionless market." — [OpenStax Business Statistics, Ch. 11](https://openstax.org/details/books/introductory-business-statistics)

<Definicao titulo="The Black-Scholes PDE">

The absence of arbitrage and continuous hedging with a replicating portfolio imply that any derivative $V(S,t)$ satisfies the parabolic PDE:

$$\frac{\partial V}{\partial t} + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2} + rS\frac{\partial V}{\partial S} - rV = 0$$

The change of variables $x = \ln S$, $\tau = T - t$, $u = e^{r\tau}V$ reduces this PDE to the **heat equation**:

$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial \tau} = \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2}$$

</Definicao>

> "The heat equation $u_t = k\,u_{xx}$ is the prototype of a parabolic PDE. Its solution via convolution with the Gaussian kernel is exactly what produces the Black-Scholes formula when the boundary condition of the option payoff is imposed." — [Lebl, Notes on Diffy Qs §4.3](https://www.jirka.org/diffyqs/)

<Definicao titulo="Closed-form solution — European call">

With boundary condition $V(S,T) = \max(S - K, 0)$, the solution of the PDE is:

$$C(S,t) = S\,N(d_1) - K\,e^{-r(T-t)}\,N(d_2)$$

$$d_1 = \frac{\ln(S/K) + (r + \sigma^2/2)(T-t)}{\sigma\sqrt{T-t}}, \quad d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{T-t}$$

where $N(d) = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{d} e^{-u^2/2}\,du$ is the CDF of the standard normal.

**Interpretation:** $N(d_2)$ is the risk-neutral probability of the option finishing in-the-money; $S\,N(d_1)$ is the expected value of the asset received conditional on exercise, discounted to $t=0$.

</Definicao>

<Teorema titulo="Put-call parity">

For European options on assets without dividends, in a market without arbitrage:

$$C - P = S - K\,e^{-r(T-t)}$$

**Proof:** Construct two portfolios with the same payoff at $T$. Portfolio A: buy the call and sell the put. Portfolio B: buy the asset and borrow $Ke^{-r(T-t)}$. Both pay $S_T - K$ at $T$. By no-arbitrage, they are worth the same today.

</Teorema>

### Derivation via replicating portfolio

**Assumptions:**
1. $S_t$ follows GBM: $dS = \mu S\,dt + \sigma S\,dW_t$.
2. Frictionless market, short selling free.
3. $r$ and $\sigma$ constant.
4. No dividends.

**Replicating portfolio:** Long $\Delta = \partial C/\partial S$ shares + short 1 call. Value: $\Pi = \Delta S - C$.

By Itô's Lemma:

$$dC = \frac{\partial C}{\partial t}dt + \frac{\partial C}{\partial S}dS + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial S^2}dt$$

The portfolio $d\Pi = \Delta\,dS - dC$ eliminates the term $dW_t$ when $\Delta = \partial C/\partial S$:

$$d\Pi = -\left(\frac{\partial C}{\partial t} + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial S^2}\right)dt$$

By no-arbitrage: $d\Pi = r\Pi\,dt$. Substituting $\Pi = \Delta S - C$:

$$\frac{\partial C}{\partial t} + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial S^2} + rS\frac{\partial C}{\partial S} - rC = 0$$

### Solution via heat equation

Change of variables $x = \ln S + (r - \sigma^2/2)(T-t)$, $\tau = \sigma^2(T-t)/2$ reduces BS to $u_\tau = u_{xx}$. Initial condition: $u(x,0) = \max(e^x - K, 0)$. Solution via convolution with the Gaussian kernel:

$$u(x,\tau) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi\tau}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} u(y,0)\,e^{-(x-y)^2/(4\tau)}\,dy$$

Undoing the change of variables, one obtains the formula $C = SN(d_1) - Ke^{-rT}N(d_2)$.

### Numerical implementation (Python)

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import norm

def bs_call(S, K, r, sigma, T):
    d1 = (np.log(S/K) + (r + 0.5*sigma**2)*T) / (sigma*np.sqrt(T))
    d2 = d1 - sigma*np.sqrt(T)
    return S*norm.cdf(d1) - K*np.exp(-r*T)*norm.cdf(d2)

def greeks(S, K, r, sigma, T):
    d1 = (np.log(S/K) + (r + 0.5*sigma**2)*T) / (sigma*np.sqrt(T))
    d2 = d1 - sigma*np.sqrt(T)
    phi = norm.pdf(d1)
    return {
        'delta': norm.cdf(d1),
        'gamma': phi / (S*sigma*np.sqrt(T)),
        'vega':  S*phi*np.sqrt(T),
        'theta': -(S*phi*sigma)/(2*np.sqrt(T)) - r*K*np.exp(-r*T)*norm.cdf(d2),
        'rho':   K*T*np.exp(-r*T)*norm.cdf(d2),
    }

# PETR4: S=46.60, K=47, r=14.65%, sigma=35%, T=30/365
print(bs_call(46.60, 47, 0.1465, 0.35, 30/365))  # approx. R$ 1.94
```

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

Black-Scholes is not a formula — it is a **paradigm**. The argument of dynamic replication (long $\Delta$ shares + short 1 call = risk-free portfolio each instant) is the foundation of all modern quantitative finance.

### The Nobel and history

[**Nobel Prize in Economics 1997**](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/1997/summary/): Robert C. Merton and Myron S. Scholes, "for a new method to determine the value of derivatives". Official lecture by Scholes: [*Derivatives in a Dynamic Environment*](https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2018/06/scholes-lecture.pdf). Fischer Black died in August 1995; the Nobel Committee explicitly named him in the citation.

The original paper: Black & Scholes (1973), *The Pricing of Options and Corporate Liabilities*, J. Political Economy 81(3):637–654 — rejected by two journals before being published.

### Known limitations and successor models

| BS Assumption | Reality | Extension |
|---|---|---|
| Log-normality | Fat tails, kurtosis | Jumps (Merton 1976, Kou 2002) |
| $\sigma$ constant | Vol smile, vol term | Heston (1993), SABR (Hagan 2002) |
| Complete market | Frictions, capital | FVA, KVA, MVA (XVA framework) |
| Continuous hedge | Discrete, cost | Replica error analysis (Taleb) |

### What the desk actually operates

Nobody "operates Black-Scholes" — one operates **implied volatility**. The implied volatility surface $\sigma_\text{impl}(K, T)$ is the central object: BS is inverted numerically (Newton-Raphson with Brenner-Subrahmanyam seed $\sigma_0 \approx \sqrt{2\pi/T}\cdot C_\text{ATM}/S$) given the observed market price.

### Connection to Term 12 (PCA and linear algebra)

In a portfolio with $n$ assets, volatility is the covariance matrix $\Sigma$. Cholesky decomposition $\Sigma = LL^T$ generates correlated Brownians for Monte Carlo. PCA (Lesson 118) extracts the principal risk factors — top 3–5 components capture 95%+ of variance in fixed income. This is the foundation of institutional **risk attribution**.

### State of the art (2025-2026)

- **Rough volatility** (Gatheral, El Euch, Rosenbaum 2018): $\sigma_t$ is a fractional process with $H < 1/2$ — reproduces the smile with parameter economy.
- **Neural SDEs**: networks parametrize the volatility dynamics learned from market data.
- **Deep calibration**: surface calibration in milliseconds via neural networks trained offline.

### Recipe: price a European call

1. Gather: $S$ (quote), $K$ (strike), $r$ (annualized Selic), $\sigma$ (estimated implied vol), $T$ (days/365).
2. Calculate $d_1 = \dfrac{\ln(S/K)+(r+\sigma^2/2)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}}$ and $d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{T}$.
3. Read $N(d_1)$ and $N(d_2)$ from the standard normal table or use `scipy.stats.norm.cdf`.
4. Apply: $C = SN(d_1) - Ke^{-rT}N(d_2)$.
5. For the put: $P = C - S + Ke^{-rT}$ (put-call parity).

### B3 Scenario — PETR4

On 04/30/2026: PETR4 at R\$ 46.60. Call strike R\$ 47, expiration 30 days, estimated implied vol 35%, Selic 14.65% p.a.

$$d_1 = \frac{\ln(46{,}60/47)+(0{,}1465+0{,}0613)\cdot 0{,}0822}{0{,}35\cdot 0{,}2868} \approx 0{,}085$$

$$d_2 = 0{,}085 - 0{,}35\cdot\sqrt{0{,}0822} \approx -0{,}015$$

$$N(0{,}085)\approx 0{,}534, \quad N(-0{,}015)\approx 0{,}494$$

$$C \approx 46{,}60\cdot 0{,}534 - 47\cdot e^{-0{,}1465\cdot 0{,}0822}\cdot 0{,}494 \approx \text{R\$ }1{,}94$$

Compare with market price. If the market charges R\$ 2.50, the market's implied vol is higher than 35% — the market prices more uncertainty than the model.

### When BS fails in practice

- **Before earnings**: implied vol explodes — BS assumes $\sigma$ constant, but the market prices the jump.
- **Crisis (2008, 2020)**: real fat tails much heavier than log-normal.
- **Liquidity**: deep OTM options have wide bid-ask spread — theoretical price irrelevant.
- **American option**: early exercise possible — BS has no closed-form solution. Use binomial (Cox-Ross-Rubinstein) or Crank-Nicolson.

## Worked Examples

<Exemplo titulo="Calculating d1 and d2 (application)" numero="1">

**Problem:** For a European call with $S = 100$, $K = 100$, $r = 0{,}05$, $\sigma = 0{,}20$, $T = 1$ year, calculate $d_1$ and $d_2$.

**Strategy:** Apply the formulas directly. Since $S = K$, the term $\ln(S/K) = \ln(1) = 0$.

**Solution:**

$$d_1 = \frac{\ln(100/100)+(0{,}05+0{,}02)\cdot 1}{0{,}20\cdot\sqrt{1}} = \frac{0 + 0{,}07}{0{,}20} = 0{,}35$$

$$d_2 = 0{,}35 - 0{,}20\cdot 1 = 0{,}15$$

**Check:** $d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{T} = 0{,}35 - 0{,}20 = 0{,}15$. Correct. Since $S = K$ (at-the-money option), $d_1 > 0$ is expected when $r > 0$.

**Source.** Exercise adapted from [OpenStax — Introductory Business Statistics, Ch. 6](https://openstax.org/details/books/introductory-business-statistics) — CC-BY 4.0.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Call price via normal table (application)" numero="2">

**Problem:** With $d_1 = 0{,}35$ and $d_2 = 0{,}15$ (Example 1), knowing that $N(0{,}35) \approx 0{,}6368$ and $N(0{,}15) \approx 0{,}5596$, calculate the call price.

**Strategy:** Apply $C = SN(d_1) - Ke^{-rT}N(d_2)$ with values read from the table.

**Solution:**

$$K e^{-rT} = 100\cdot e^{-0{,}05} \approx 100\cdot 0{,}9512 = 95{,}12$$

$$C = 100\cdot 0{,}6368 - 95{,}12\cdot 0{,}5596$$

$$C = 63{,}68 - 53{,}23 \approx 10{,}45$$

**Check:** The call is worth R\$ 10.45 for a stock worth R\$ 100. Intuition: at-the-money option with 20% vol and 1 year should be worth between 7–13% of price — 10.45% is in the expected range.

**Source.** Exercise adapted from [OpenIntro Statistics §3.3](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/) — CC-BY-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Put-call parity — calculating the put (intermediate)" numero="3">

**Problem:** With the data from Example 2 ($C \approx 10{,}45$, $S = 100$, $K = 100$, $r = 0{,}05$, $T = 1$), calculate the price of the corresponding European put.

**Strategy:** Apply put-call parity $P = C - S + Ke^{-rT}$.

**Solution:**

$$P = 10{,}45 - 100 + 95{,}12 = 5{,}57$$

**Check:** By symmetry, at-the-money put with the same parameters should be less than the call when $r > 0$ (carry: holding the stock has an opportunity cost of $r$). Indeed, $P = 5{,}57 < C = 10{,}45$. Consistent.

Additional test: $C - P = 10{,}45 - 5{,}57 = 4{,}88 \approx S - Ke^{-rT} = 100 - 95{,}12 = 4{,}88$. Parity confirmed.

**Source.** [OpenIntro Statistics §3.4 — derivation of parity by arbitrage](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/) — CC-BY-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Delta and hedge ratio (intermediate/advanced)" numero="4">

**Problem:** A desk has a long position in 1,000 calls with $\Delta = N(d_1) = 0{,}6368$ (Example 1). How many shares should it short to create a delta-neutral portfolio?

**Strategy:** Delta-neutrality means the total portfolio has $\Delta_\text{total} = 0$. Delta of each call = 0.6368; delta of each share = 1.

**Solution:**

Portfolio: long 1,000 calls + short $n$ shares.

$$\Delta_\text{portfolio} = 1.000\cdot 0{,}6368 - n\cdot 1 = 0$$

$$n = 636{,}8 \approx 637 \text{ shares}$$

**Check:** If $S$ rises R\$ 1, the 1,000 calls gain $1.000 \times 0{,}6368 \times 1 = $ R\$ 636.80. The 637 shorted shares lose R\$ 637. Net: $-$R\$ 0.20 — approximately zero (rounding error). Hedge works instantaneously; must be rebalanced as $\Delta$ changes.

**Source.** [OpenStax — Introductory Business Statistics, Ch. 11](https://openstax.org/details/books/introductory-business-statistics) — CC-BY 4.0.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Convergence of 120 lessons — where each concept appears in BS (real modeling)" numero="5">

**Problem:** Identify, in the formula $C = SN(d_1) - Ke^{-r(T-t)}N(d_2)$ and its components, where appear: (a) functions of two variables, (b) natural logarithm, (c) the number $e$, (d) derivative, (e) integral, (f) normal distribution, (g) PDE, (h) linear algebra.

**Strategy:** Systematically map each mathematical concept to the component of the formula where it appears.

**Solution:**

(a) **Functions of two variables** (Lessons 2, 51): $C(S,t)$ — domain $S > 0$, $t \in [0,T]$.

(b) **Natural logarithm** (Lesson 7): $\ln(S/K)$ in $d_1$ — measures the log-return needed for the option to go in-the-money.

(c) **The number $e$** (Lesson 7): $e^{-r(T-t)}$ — continuous discount factor; $e = \lim_{n\to\infty}(1+r/n)^n$.

(d) **Derivative** (Lessons 51–67): $\Delta = \partial C/\partial S = N(d_1)$, $\Gamma = \partial^2 C/\partial S^2$, etc. The Greeks are all partial derivatives.

(e) **Integral** (Lessons 82–87): $N(d) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{d} e^{-u^2/2}\,du$ — improper integral computed numerically.

(f) **Normal distribution** (Lessons 76–77): $N(d)$ is the CDF of the standard normal $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$; log-returns normal via CLT.

(g) **PDE** (Lessons 92–95): the formula is the solution of the parabolic BS PDE — reducible to the heat equation.

(h) **Linear algebra** (Lessons 31–35, 114–118): in multi-asset portfolio, vol becomes the covariance matrix $\Sigma$; PCA extracts risk factors.
