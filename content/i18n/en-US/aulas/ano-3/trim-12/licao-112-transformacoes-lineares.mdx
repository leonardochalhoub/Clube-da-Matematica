<Porta nivel="formal" titulo="Rigorous definition">

### Linear transformations

<Definicao titulo="Linear transformation (linear map)">

Let $V$ and $W$ be vector spaces over a field $K$. A function $T: V \to W$ is called a **linear transformation** (or **linear map**) if it satisfies, for all $u, v \in V$ and all $a \in K$:

<Equation explicacao="Preservation of sum: the image of the sum is the sum of the images." latex="T(u + v) = T(u) + T(v)" />

<Equation explicacao="Preservation of scalar: the image of the multiple is the multiple of the image." latex="T(av) = a\,T(v)" />

The two conditions merge into one: $T(au + bv) = aT(u) + bT(v)$ for all $u, v \in V$ and $a, b \in K$.

</Definicao>

> "A linear transformation is a function that goes from one vector space to another and preserves the vector space operations of vector addition and scalar multiplication." — [Beezer — A First Course in Linear Algebra, §LT](http://linear.ups.edu/fcla/section-LT.html)

<Definicao titulo="Immediate consequences of linearity">

Every linear transformation satisfies:

- **$T(0_V) = 0_W$**: the image of the zero vector is zero. (Proof: $T(0_V) = T(0 \cdot 0_V) = 0\,T(0_V) = 0_W$.)
- **$T(-v) = -T(v)$**: the image of the opposite is the opposite of the image.
- **$T\left(\sum_{i=1}^n a_i v_i\right) = \sum_{i=1}^n a_i T(v_i)$**: linearity extends to arbitrary linear combinations.

**Beware:** The affine function $x \mapsto Ax + b$ with $b \neq 0$ **is not linear** — it violates $T(0) = 0$.

</Definicao>

> "If $T$ is a linear transformation, then $T(0) = 0$." — [Beezer — A First Course in Linear Algebra, Theorem LTTZZ, §LT](http://linear.ups.edu/fcla/section-LT.html)

### Matrix representation

<Definicao titulo="Matrix of a linear transformation">

Fix basis $\mathcal{B} = \{v_1, \ldots, v_n\}$ of $V$ and basis $\mathcal{C} = \{w_1, \ldots, w_m\}$ of $W$. The **matrix of $T$ in bases $\mathcal{B}$ and $\mathcal{C}$**, denoted $[T]_{\mathcal{B}}^{\mathcal{C}} \in M_{m \times n}(K)$, is the matrix whose $j$-th column is the vector $T(v_j)$ written in coordinates in basis $\mathcal{C}$:

<Equation explicacao="The j-th column of [T] is T(v_j) in coordinates of C. For any vector v with coordinates x in B, we have T(v) with coordinates [T]x in C." latex="[T]_j = [T(v_j)]_{\mathcal{C}}" />

Result: $T$ is **completely determined** by its values on a basis. Applying $T$ to any $v$ is equivalent to multiplying $[T]$ by the coordinate vector of $v$ in $\mathcal{B}$.

</Definicao>

### Change of basis and similar matrices

<Teorema titulo="Similar matrices represent the same transformation">

Let $[T]_{\mathcal{B}}$ and $[T]_{\mathcal{B}'}$ be the matrices of $T: V \to V$ in bases $\mathcal{B}$ and $\mathcal{B}'$ of $V$, and let $P$ be the change-of-basis matrix from $\mathcal{B}'$ to $\mathcal{B}$. Then:

<Equation explicacao="Similar matrices: same transformation, different bases. P is invertible — its columns are the vectors of B' in coordinates of B." latex="[T]_{\mathcal{B}'} = P^{-1}\,[T]_{\mathcal{B}}\,P" />

Two matrices $A$ and $B$ are **similar** ($A \sim B$) if there exists an invertible $P$ such that $B = P^{-1}AP$. Similar matrices have the same determinant, same trace, and the same eigenvalues.

</Teorema>

> "Two matrices that represent the same linear transformation in different bases are called similar matrices, and $B = P^{-1}AP$ for some invertible matrix $P$." — [Hefferon — Linear Algebra, ch. 3 §III.1](https://hefferon.net/linearalgebra/index.html)

### Composition

<Teorema titulo="Composition of linear transformations">

If $S: U \to V$ and $T: V \to W$ are linear transformations, then $T \circ S: U \to W$ is linear, and in fixed bases:

<Equation explicacao="Composition becomes matrix multiplication. Order: [T][S] (S applied first, T second — same order as composition)." latex="[T \circ S] = [T]\,[S]" />

**Consequence**: matrix multiplication has concrete geometric meaning — it is composition of transformations.

</Teorema>

<Insight>

**Why bases?** A linear transformation $T$ is a geometric object: it exists independent of coordinates. The **matrix** is a *photograph* of $T$ in a specific basis. Changing basis is changing the angle of the photo — the object doesn't change, only the representation. This motivates diagonalization (Lesson 115): finding the basis where the photo becomes simplest.

</Insight>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

Imagine a **magic mirror** at your house's entrance.

You pass with a backpack and the mirror shows the backpack on the other side. You pass with a hat and it shows the hat. If you enter with both backpack AND hat together, the mirror shows both — just like if you had shown each one separately.

**Linear transformation** is that honest mirror: it doesn't invent or hide anything. If you doubled your backpack (made it become two backpacks), the mirror would also double the image.

What the mirror **doesn't do**: it doesn't push you to the side. If you arrived with nothing, the image would be nothing. (A magic mirror that moves things — that's not linear!)

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

You know **rotation**: turning a figure in the plane. And **scaling**: stretching or shrinking. These two operations are **linear transformations**.

Their rule is simple:

- If you add two vectors and then rotate — the result is the same as rotating each one separately and then adding.
- If you double a vector and then rotate — the result is the same as rotating and then doubling.

And here's the magic: **every linear transformation can be written as a matrix**.

Rotation of 90° in the plane:

$$R = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$$

Applying $R$ to the vector $\begin{pmatrix}1 \\ 0\end{pmatrix}$ (pointing right) gives $\begin{pmatrix}0 \\ 1\end{pmatrix}$ (pointing up). The vector rotated exactly 90°!

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old teen">

Linear transformations are **the central operation** of linear algebra. All the matrix theory you've learned since Lesson 31 is, at heart, algebra of linear transformations.

### Why the matrix works?

In basis $\{e_1, \ldots, e_n\}$, any vector $v$ can be written as $v = a_1 e_1 + \cdots + a_n e_n$. By linearity:

$$T(v) = a_1 T(e_1) + \cdots + a_n T(e_n)$$

**Conclusion**: to know what $T$ does to any vector, just know what $T$ does to the basis vectors. And these image vectors $T(e_1), \ldots, T(e_n)$ are the **columns** of the matrix of $T$.

### Canonical transformations of the plane

| Transformation | $2 \times 2$ Matrix | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Identity | $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ | Changes nothing |
| Rotation $\theta$ | $\begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{pmatrix}$ | Rotates by $\theta$ degrees |
| Reflection on $x$-axis | $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$ | Mirrors vertically |
| Scaling $k$ | $kI$ | Multiplies size by $k$ |
| Projection onto $x$ | $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ | Throws onto $x$-axis |

### Composition is multiplication

Applying $S$ and then $T$: $(T \circ S)(v) = T(S(v))$. In matrices: $[T][S]v$. **Beware: order matters.** In general $AB \neq BA$.

<Insight>

**Change of basis** is exactly that: the same transformation viewed from another angle. When Lesson 115 talks about diagonalization, you'll be finding the basis where the matrix becomes diagonal (the simplest possible). Everything starts here.

</Insight>

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

In engineering, linear algebra is: **data (vectors) + operations (matrices) = system**. Linear transformations are the conceptual level above matrices — and mastering this level separates those who "compute" from those who "understand".

### Important categories of transformations

| Type | Condition | Appears in |
|---|---|---|
| Isomorphism | Bijective linear | Change of coordinates |
| Orthogonal projection | $P^2 = P$, $P = P^T$ | Least squares, PCA |
| Reflection | $R^2 = I$, $R \neq I$ | Householder QR |
| Rotation | Orthogonal, $\det = 1$ | Robotics, CG, quantum |
| Nilpotent | $N^k = 0$ for some $k$ | Jordan form, partial diff. |
| Idempotent | $A^2 = A$ | Projections, conditional expectation |

### Computational applications

**Computer graphics**: 3D rendering pipeline uses composition of linear transformations (model → view → projection). Homogeneous coordinates $\mathbb{R}^4$ represent translation as a linear transformation in higher dimension.

**Signal processing**: discrete convolution = multiplication by Toeplitz matrix. The FFT (Fast Fourier Transform) is an orthogonal linear transformation — and its efficient implementation is what makes audio compression possible.

**Machine learning**: each **dense layer** of a neural network is $y = W x + b$ — the linear part $Wx$ is a linear transformation, the $b$ is the affine part. All "information compression" between layers is linear; non-linearities come from activations (ReLU, sigmoid, etc.).

**System control**: an LTI (linear time-invariant) system has dynamics $\dot{x} = Ax + Bu$, $y = Cx + Du$. The matrices $A, B, C, D$ are constant linear transformations.

### Computational cost

Matrix-vector multiplication $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$, $v \in \mathbb{R}^n$: $O(mn)$ operations. Matrix-matrix multiplication: $O(n^3)$ (Strassen: $O(n^{2.807})$). GPUs are specialized hardware for exactly this — all modern AI scales through massive parallelization of matrix multiplications.

**Rule of thumb**: never compute $A^{-1}$ explicitly to solve $Ax = b$. Use LU or Cholesky decomposition — same $O(n^3)$ cost but without accumulating numerical errors.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

### Category $\mathbf{Vect}_K$

Linear transformations are the **morphisms** of the category of vector spaces over $K$. Composition is matrix multiplication. The identity is $\text{id}_V$. Isomorphisms are invertible. This categorical perspective clarifies the structure:

- $\text{Hom}(V, W)$ (the set of all linear transformations from $V$ to $W$) is itself a vector space of dimension $(\dim V)(\dim W)$.
- Composition is bilinear: $\text{Hom}(V, W) \times \text{Hom}(U, V) \to \text{Hom}(U, W)$.

### First isomorphism theorem

Every $T: V \to W$ admits canonical factorization:

$$V \twoheadrightarrow V/\ker T \xrightarrow{\bar{T}} \operatorname{Im}(T) \hookrightarrow W$$

where $\bar{T}$ is an isomorphism. The **Kernel-Image Theorem** (rank-nullity) is a consequence: $\dim V = \dim \ker T + \dim \operatorname{Im}(T)$.

### Computational decompositions

For matrices (representations of transformations):

- **LU**: $A = LU$ — Gaussian elimination, $O(n^3)$, foundation of all direct solvers.
- **QR**: $A = QR$ — Gram-Schmidt or Householder. $O(mn^2)$ for $m \geq n$. Foundation of least squares and QR method for eigenvalues.
- **Cholesky**: $A = LL^T$ for SPD — $O(n^3/3)$, half the cost of LU.
- **SVD**: $A = U\Sigma V^T$ — Lesson 117. The most informative decomposition; always exists, even for rectangular matrices.
- **Jordan**: canonical form over $\mathbb{C}$ — relevant for ODEs and control systems.

### Operators in infinite dimension

In $L^2(\mathbb{R})$, linear transformations include:
- **Multiplication**: $(M_g f)(x) = g(x)f(x)$ — bounded if $g \in L^\infty$.
- **Convolution**: $(K_h f)(x) = \int h(x-y)f(y)\,dy$ — Young's inequality determines boundedness.
- **Differentiation**: $D = d/dx$ — **unbounded**, but closed.
- **Fourier transform**: unitary on $L^2$ (Plancherel's theorem).

### Spectral theorem

A compact self-adjoint operator on Hilbert space has discrete spectral decomposition — real eigenvalues, orthogonal eigenvectors. **Quantum mechanics** lives here: observables are self-adjoint operators on $L^2$, eigenvalues are the possible measurement outcomes.

**Differentiable programming**: the derivative of $f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ at a point is a linear transformation (the Jacobian). Every neural network training via gradient descent iterates over these infinitesimal linearities.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practice — data and Brazilian context">

### How to check linearity: practical walkthrough

For any candidate $T$:

1. Compute $T(u + v)$ and compare with $T(u) + T(v)$. Are they equal for all $u, v$?
2. Compute $T(\lambda v)$ and compare with $\lambda T(v)$. Are they equal for all $\lambda$?
3. Check $T(0) = 0$. If it fails, stop — not linear.

If any condition fails, the function **is not linear**.

### How to construct the matrix: column-by-column algorithm

1. Choose basis $\{e_1, \ldots, e_n\}$ of $V$.
2. Compute $T(e_1), T(e_2), \ldots, T(e_n)$ (images of basis vectors).
3. Each $T(e_j)$, written in coordinates of the basis of $W$, becomes the $j$-th column of the matrix.

**Example**: $T: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^2$, $T(x, y) = (2x + y,\ x - 3y)$.

Canonical basis: $e_1 = (1, 0)$, $e_2 = (0, 1)$.

$T(e_1) = T(1, 0) = (2, 1) \Rightarrow$ column 1: $\begin{pmatrix}2\\1\end{pmatrix}$

$T(e_2) = T(0, 1) = (1, -3) \Rightarrow$ column 2: $\begin{pmatrix}1\\-3\end{pmatrix}$

Matrix: $[T] = \begin{pmatrix}2 & 1 \\ 1 & -3\end{pmatrix}$.

### Application in finance: portfolio return

A portfolio with $n$ assets and weights $w = (w_1, \ldots, w_n)$ has expected return $r_p = w^\top \mu$ where $\mu$ is the vector of expected returns. This is a linear transformation $T: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$, $T(w) = w^\top \mu$. Portfolio optimization (Markowitz) minimizes $w^\top \Sigma w$ subject to $w^\top \mu = r_\text{target}$ — a quadratic problem with linear constraint.

## Worked examples

<Exemplo titulo="Verify linearity of T(x, y) = (2x, 3y)" numero="1">

**Problem:** Determine if $T: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^2$ defined by $T(x, y) = (2x,\, 3y)$ is a linear transformation.

**Strategy:** Check the two conditions of the definition: preservation of sum and preservation of scalar. Use generic vectors.

**Solution:**

Let $u = (x_1, y_1)$ and $v = (x_2, y_2)$ be vectors in $\mathbb{R}^2$, and $a \in \mathbb{R}$ a scalar.

1. **Preservation of sum:**
$$T(u + v) = T(x_1 + x_2,\; y_1 + y_2) = (2(x_1+x_2),\; 3(y_1+y_2))$$
$$= (2x_1 + 2x_2,\; 3y_1 + 3y_2) = (2x_1, 3y_1) + (2x_2, 3y_2) = T(u) + T(v). \checkmark$$

2. **Preservation of scalar:**
$$T(a u) = T(ax_1, ay_1) = (2ax_1,\; 3ay_1) = a(2x_1, 3y_1) = a\,T(u). \checkmark$$

**Verification:** Since both conditions were verified for generic vectors, $T$ is linear. Additionally, $T(0, 0) = (0, 0) = 0$ — consistent with linearity.

**Source.** [Beezer — A First Course in Linear Algebra, §LT Example LTPM](http://linear.ups.edu/fcla/section-LT.html) — GNU FDL license.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Build the matrix of rotation by 60 degrees" numero="2">

**Problem:** Find the $2 \times 2$ matrix representing rotation by $60°$ in the plane $\mathbb{R}^2$.

**Strategy:** Apply the column-by-column algorithm: compute where $T$ takes $e_1 = (1,0)$ and $e_2 = (0,1)$. The images are the columns.

**Solution:**

1. **Image of $e_1$:** The vector $(1, 0)$ points right (angle $0°$). After rotation by $60°$, it points at angle $60°$:
$$T(e_1) = (\cos 60°,\; \sin 60°) = \left(\frac{1}{2},\; \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)$$

2. **Image of $e_2$:** The vector $(0, 1)$ points up (angle $90°$). After rotation by $60°$, it points at angle $150°$:
$$T(e_2) = (\cos 150°,\; \sin 150°) = \left(-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2},\; \frac{1}{2}\right)$$

3. **Build the matrix** (columns = images):
$$[T] = \begin{pmatrix} \cos 60° & \cos 150° \\ \sin 60° & \sin 150° \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1/2 & -\sqrt{3}/2 \\ \sqrt{3}/2 & 1/2 \end{pmatrix}$$

**Verification:** $\det[T] = (1/2)(1/2) - (-\sqrt{3}/2)(\sqrt{3}/2) = 1/4 + 3/4 = 1$. Rotations preserve area — $\det = 1$ is correct. And $[T]^\top [T] = I$ (orthogonal matrix), confirming that norms are preserved.

**Source.** [Hefferon — Linear Algebra, ch. 3 §I Example 1.9](https://hefferon.net/linearalgebra/index.html) — CC-BY-SA license.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Differentiation in P2 — matrix in canonical basis" numero="3">

**Problem:** Define $D: \mathcal{P}_2 \to \mathcal{P}_2$ by $D(p) = p'$ (usual derivative). Show that $D$ is linear and find its matrix in basis $\mathcal{B} = \{1, x, x^2\}$.

**Strategy:** (1) Check linearity using properties of the derivative. (2) Compute $D$ on each basis vector and build the columns.

**Solution:**

1. **Linearity:**
$$D(p + q) = (p + q)' = p' + q' = D(p) + D(q). \checkmark$$
$$D(\lambda p) = (\lambda p)' = \lambda p' = \lambda D(p). \checkmark$$

2. **Images of basis vectors:**

$$D(1) = 0 = 0 \cdot 1 + 0 \cdot x + 0 \cdot x^2 \Rightarrow \text{column 1: } \begin{pmatrix}0\\0\\0\end{pmatrix}$$

$$D(x) = 1 = 1 \cdot 1 + 0 \cdot x + 0 \cdot x^2 \Rightarrow \text{column 2: } \begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\0\end{pmatrix}$$

$$D(x^2) = 2x = 0 \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot x + 0 \cdot x^2 \Rightarrow \text{column 3: } \begin{pmatrix}0\\2\\0\end{pmatrix}$$

3. **Matrix:**
$$[D]_{\mathcal{B}} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$$

**Verification:** Apply $[D]$ to the coordinate vector of $p = 3 + 2x + 5x^2$, which is $(3, 2, 5)^\top$:
$$[D]\begin{pmatrix}3\\2\\5\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}2\\10\\0\end{pmatrix}$$
This corresponds to the polynomial $2 + 10x$. And indeed $p'(x) = 2 + 10x$. Correct.

**Source.** [Beezer — A First Course in Linear Algebra, §LT Example MLTCV](http://linear.ups.edu/fcla/section-LT.html) — GNU FDL license.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Change of basis — same T, different matrices" numero="4">

**Problem:** Let $T: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^2$ be reflection with respect to the $x$-axis: $T(x, y) = (x, -y)$.
(a) Find $[T]$ in canonical basis $\mathcal{B} = \{e_1, e_2\}$.
(b) Find $[T]$ in basis $\mathcal{B}' = \{(1,1),\, (1,-1)\}$.
(c) Confirm that $[T]_{\mathcal{B}'} = P^{-1}[T]_{\mathcal{B}}\,P$.

**Strategy:** (a) Column-by-column algorithm in each basis. (c) Use the change-of-basis formula with $P = $ matrix whose columns are the vectors of $\mathcal{B}'$ in coordinates of $\mathcal{B}$.

**Solution:**

**(a)** Canonical basis:
$$T(e_1) = T(1, 0) = (1, 0) \Rightarrow \text{col. 1: } (1, 0)^\top$$
$$T(e_2) = T(0, 1) = (0, -1) \Rightarrow \text{col. 2: } (0, -1)^\top$$
$$[T]_{\mathcal{B}} = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1\end{pmatrix}$$

**(b)** Basis $\mathcal{B}' = \{v_1, v_2\} = \{(1,1),(1,-1)\}$. Compute images in coordinates of $\mathcal{B}'$:
$$T(v_1) = T(1,1) = (1,-1) = v_2 = 0 \cdot v_1 + 1 \cdot v_2 \Rightarrow \text{col. 1: } (0, 1)^\top$$
$$T(v_2) = T(1,-1) = (1,1) = v_1 = 1 \cdot v_1 + 0 \cdot v_2 \Rightarrow \text{col. 2: } (1, 0)^\top$$
$$[T]_{\mathcal{B}'} = \begin{pmatrix}0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0\end{pmatrix}$$

**(c)** Matrix $P$ (columns are $v_1, v_2$ in canonical coordinates):
$$P = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1\end{pmatrix}, \quad P^{-1} = \frac{1}{-2}\begin{pmatrix}-1 & -1 \\ -1 & 1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}1/2 & 1/2 \\ 1/2 & -1/2\end{pmatrix}$$

$$P^{-1}[T]_{\mathcal{B}}P = \begin{pmatrix}1/2 & 1/2 \\ 1/2 & -1/2\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0\end{pmatrix} = [T]_{\mathcal{B}'}. \checkmark$$

**Verification:** In $\mathcal{B}'$, the reflection swaps $v_1 \leftrightarrow v_2$, so the matrix should be exactly the permutation $\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ — makes sense geometrically.

**Source.** [Hefferon — Linear Algebra, ch. 3 §V Exercise 1.18](https://hefferon.net/linearalgebra/index.html) — CC-BY-SA license.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Composition of transformations and matrix multiplication" numero="5">

**Problem:** Define $S: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^2$ as horizontal shear $S(x, y) = (x + 2y,\, y)$ and $T: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^2$ as reflection $T(x, y) = (-x,\, y)$. Compute (a) matrices $[S]$ and $[T]$; (b) matrix of $T \circ S$; (c) verify by computing $(T \circ S)(3, 1)$ directly and via matrix multiplication.

**Strategy:** Column-by-column algorithm for each transformation. Composition = matrix product $[T][S]$.

**Solution:**

**(a)** Matrices via column-by-column:
$$[S] = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}, \quad [T] = \begin{pmatrix}-1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}$$

**(b)** Composition $T \circ S$ (apply $S$ first, then $T$):
$$[T \circ S] = [T][S] = \begin{pmatrix}-1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}-1 & -2 \\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}$$

**(c)** Verification for $(3, 1)$:

**Directly:** $S(3, 1) = (3 + 2\cdot1,\; 1) = (5, 1)$. Then $T(5, 1) = (-5, 1)$.

**Via matrix:** $[T \circ S]\begin{pmatrix}3\\1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}-1 & -2 \\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}3\\1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}-3-2\\0+1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}-5\\1\end{pmatrix}$.

Result: $(-5, 1)$ — identical. Matrix multiplication = composition of transformations.

**Source.** [Axler — Linear Algebra Done Right 4th ed., §3B Exercise 1](https://linear.axler.net/) — CC-BY-NC license.

</Exemplo>
