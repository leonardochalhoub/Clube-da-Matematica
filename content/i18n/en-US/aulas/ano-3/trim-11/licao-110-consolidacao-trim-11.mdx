### The three pillars of statistical inference

<Definicao titulo="Pillar 1 — Estimation by confidence interval">

Let $X_1, \ldots, X_n \overset{iid}{\sim} N(\mu, \sigma^2)$.

**Two-sided CI of level $1-\alpha$ for $\mu$** ($\sigma$ known):

<Equation explicacao="Confidence interval for mean with known population standard deviation." latex="\bar X \pm z_{\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}" />

**CI with $\sigma$ unknown** — uses Student's t distribution with $n-1$ degrees of freedom:

<Equation explicacao="Confidence interval for mean with sample standard deviation (most common practical use)." latex="\bar X \pm t_{\alpha/2;\,n-1}\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}" />

**Sample size** for maximum margin of error $E$:

<Equation explicacao="Sample size formula: z squared times sigma, divided by E squared." latex="n \geq \left(\frac{z_{\alpha/2}\,\sigma}{E}\right)^2" />

</Definicao>

> "A confidence interval provides a plausible range of values for the population parameter. The correct interpretation: if we repeat the procedure many times, $(1-\alpha)100\%$ of the constructed intervals will contain the true parameter." — [OpenIntro Statistics §5.2](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/)

<Definicao titulo="Pillar 2 — Hypothesis testing (unified view)">

Every hypothesis test follows the same scheme:

1. State $H_0$ and $H_1$.
2. Compute the **test statistic** under $H_0$.
3. Compute the **p-value** = $P(\text{result as or more extreme than observed} \mid H_0)$.
4. If $p \leq \alpha$, reject $H_0$.

**Map of tests:**

| Scenario | Statistic | Distribution |
|---|---|---|
| 1 mean, $\sigma$ known | $Z = (\bar X - \mu_0)/(\sigma/\sqrt{n})$ | $N(0,1)$ |
| 1 mean, $\sigma$ unknown | $T = (\bar X - \mu_0)/(s/\sqrt{n})$ | $t_{n-1}$ |
| 2 independent means | Welch $T$ | $t_{\nu}$ (Welch degrees of freedom) |
| $k$ means | $F = MS_B/MS_W$ | $F_{k-1,\,N-k}$ |
| Contingency table | $\chi^2 = \sum (O-E)^2/E$ | $\chi^2_{(r-1)(c-1)}$ |
| Proportion vs reference value | $Z = (\hat p - p_0)/\sqrt{p_0(1-p_0)/n}$ | $N(0,1)$ |

</Definicao>

<Definicao titulo="Pillar 3 — Regression and predictive modeling">

**Simple linear regression** $\hat Y = \hat\beta_0 + \hat\beta_1 X$:

<Equation explicacao="Ordinary least squares estimators for simple regression." latex="\hat\beta_1 = \frac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}},\quad \hat\beta_0 = \bar Y - \hat\beta_1 \bar X" />

**Multiple regression** $\hat{\mathbf{y}} = X\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}$:

<Equation explicacao="Vector least squares estimator for multiple regression." latex="\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} = (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top \mathbf{y}" />

**Coefficient of determination** and its adjusted version:

<Equation explicacao="Adjusted R-squared penalizes extra predictors that do not contribute to fit." latex="R^2_{\text{adj}} = 1 - \frac{(1-R^2)(n-1)}{n-p-1}" />

</Definicao>

<Teorema titulo="Bayes' rule for belief updating">

Let $\theta$ be the parameter of interest, $D$ the observed data:

<Equation explicacao="The posterior distribution is proportional to the likelihood times the prior." latex="P(\theta \mid D) = \frac{P(D \mid \theta)\,P(\theta)}{P(D)} \propto P(D \mid \theta)\,P(\theta)" />

**Beta-Binomial conjugate:** if $\theta \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)$ and $X \mid \theta \sim \text{Bin}(n, \theta)$, then:

<Equation explicacao="Posterior distribution when the prior is Beta and the likelihood is binomial — the prior updates by summing successes and failures." latex="\theta \mid X \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha + X,\; \beta + n - X)" />

</Teorema>

<Insight>

**Deep unity.** Linear regression, ANOVA, t test and the log-linear model for $\chi^2$ are all cases of the **Generalized Linear Model**: $g(E[Y]) = X\beta$. Mastering GLM is mastering all classical parametric inference.

</Insight>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

Imagine you want to know **how many candies are in a huge jar**.

- You don't count them all — you grab a handful, count, and **guess a range**: "there should be between 200 and 300 candies". That is the **confidence interval**.
- Your friend said "there are exactly 150". You look at your handful and think: "that would be very unlikely if there were 150". So you say "I don't believe that!" — that is the **hypothesis test**.
- Yesterday you already knew a little about the jar. Today you combined that "yesterday's knowledge" with what you counted now, and you got smarter. That is **Bayes** — learning by combining what you knew with what you saw.

All three together are like being a **good detective of numbers**.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

You spent term 11 learning to **draw conclusions from samples**.

**What was and what is now:**

Before, you calculated the average of an entire class — you **had** all the data. Now you learn from a **sample** and make claims about the **world**.

**The three methods:**

1. **Confidence interval:** "With 95% confidence, the average grade at school is between 6.2 and 7.1." You don't know the exact value — you give a plausible range.

2. **Hypothesis test:** "Did the new textbook improve the average?" You collect data, calculate the probability of this result if nothing had improved (the p-value), and decide whether you believe in the change.

3. **Bayes:** You already knew something before collecting data. With Bayes, you combine what you already knew (prior) with what you observed (data) and update your belief.

**Choosing the method:**

- I want to estimate a mean: CI with $t$.
- I want to compare two groups: t test.
- I want to compare several groups: ANOVA.
- I want to cross two categorical variables: chi-squared.
- I want to predict $Y$ from $X$: regression.
- I want to update my belief: Bayes.

</Porta>

### Synthesis: frequentism vs Bayesianism

**Frequentist perspective** (lessons 102–108):

- Parameters ($\mu$, $\sigma$, $\beta$) are **unknown constants** — they don't have distributions.
- CI: coverage over hypothetical repeated experiments.
- $p$-value: "how unlikely would this result be if $H_0$ were true?"

**Bayesian perspective** (lesson 109):

- Parameters have **probability distributions** that reflect uncertainty.
- Credible interval: "with probability 95%, $\theta \in [a, b]$ given the observed data."
- Updating: prior + likelihood = posterior.

### Connections that term 11 revealed

| Relationship | Detail |
|---|---|
| Regression with dummy = t test | 1 binary predictor → intercept and coefficient are group means |
| ANOVA = regression with $k-1$ dummies | Overall F of the model = F of the ANOVA |
| $F = T^2$ when $k = 2$ | ANOVA with 2 groups is identical to two-sided t test |
| MAP with uniform prior = MLE | frequentism and Bayesianism coincide when you have no prior information |

### Recurring errors (never commit these)

1. **$p = 0{,}06$ is not evidence for $H_0$** — it only means "we don't reject $H_0$ at the 5% level".
2. **Statistical significance $\neq$ practical significance** — a tiny effect can be significant with large $n$.
3. **Correlation $\neq$ causality** — regression doesn't prove cause.
4. **$R^2$ high $\neq$ good model** — there may be patterns in the residuals.
5. **p-hacking** — testing multiple hypotheses without multiple comparison correction inflates type I error.

</Porta>

### Everything is generalized linear model (GLM)

Linear regression, ANOVA, t test, and the log-linear model for $\chi^2$ are cases of the **generalized linear model**:

$$g(E[Y]) = X\beta$$

| Method | Distribution of $Y$ | Link function $g$ |
|---|---|---|
| Linear regression | Normal | Identity |
| ANOVA | Normal | Identity |
| Logistic regression | Bernoulli | Logit |
| Poisson regression | Poisson | Log |
| Multinomial regression | Multinomial | Multivariate logit |

Mastering GLM = mastering all classical parametric inference.

### Resampling: non-parametric alternative

- **Bootstrap:** CI and standard error of any statistic without distributional assumption.
- **Permutation:** p-value without normality assumption.
- **Cross-validation:** robust predictive evaluation.

### Power analysis (underutilized in practice)

Before any data collection, calculate sample size to detect the minimum relevant effect with 80% power:

$$n = \frac{2\sigma^2(z_\alpha + z_\beta)^2}{\delta^2}$$

where $\delta$ is the minimum detectable difference, $z_\alpha$ and $z_\beta$ correspond to type I and II errors.

Without power analysis, experiments die before they're born — or produce inconclusive results that nobody can replicate.

### Professional analysis checklist

1. Pre-register hypotheses (OSF, AsPredicted).
2. Power analysis and calculation of $n$.
3. Collect with controlled design (randomization, blinding).
4. Check assumptions (normality, homoscedasticity, independence).
5. Report: estimate + CI + effect size + $p$-value.
6. Residual diagnostics (regression).
7. Multiple comparison correction (Bonferroni, FDR).

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

### Philosophy of statistical inference

Four schools form the current landscape:

- **Neyman-Pearson frequentist:** controls long-run error rates ($\alpha$, $\beta$). CI has frequent coverage, not probability of containing the parameter.
- **Fisherian:** p-value as continuous measure of evidence against $H_0$. Without reject/no-reject dichotomy.
- **Likelihoodist (Edwards):** likelihood ratio as measure of evidence between models. Without prior.
- **Bayesian (Jeffreys, Lindley, McElreath):** parameters have distributions; posterior is the complete answer. Allows direct probability statements about hypotheses.

None is universally superior. Choice depends on context, objectives and the assumptions the analyst accepts.

### Replication crisis and what we learned

In 2015, the Open Science Collaboration replicated 100 psychology studies — only 36% replicated. The causes are instrumental:

1. P-hacking and HARKing (Hypothesizing After Results are Known).
2. Power analysis ignored — small effects, insufficient samples.
3. Multiple comparisons without correction.
4. Data and code not shared.
5. Publication bias (negative results don't appear).

Solutions implemented in the community: pre-registration, direct replication, open science, registered reports, and metrics beyond $p < 0{,}05$ (effect size, BIC, Bayes Factor).

### Current frontier

- **Causal inference (Pearl, Rubin):** DAGs, do-calculus, instrumental variables, RDD, difference-in-differences. Classical statistics estimates associations; causal inference estimates counterfactuals.
- **High-dimensional:** $p \gg n$. Lasso, ridge, elastic net, knockoffs (Candès) for FDR control.
- **Selective inference:** valid p-values post-variable selection.
- **Bayesian computation:** HMC (Stan), variational inference, ABC. Made complex hierarchical models tractable.
- **Predictive vs inferential models:** ML maximize predictive accuracy; inference maximize interpretability and causal validity. Breiman (2001) called this "two cultures".

### Ethics in statistics

Manipulating data, choosing tests post-hoc without disclosure, not reporting pre-specified analyses — are practices that cross the line of scientific integrity. The ASA Statement on P-Values (2016) is mandatory reading for any professional.

</Porta>

### Case 1 — Average rental price in São Paulo

A real estate agency sampled $n = 49$ two-bedroom apartments in the south zone. Sample mean R$ 2,850/month, sample standard deviation R$ 420/month.

**95% CI for average price:**
$t_{0,025;\,48} \approx 2{,}011$. Margin: $2{,}011 \times 420/7 = 120{,}66$.
CI: R$ 2,729 to R$ 2,971.

**Test:** City government claims average rent is R$ 2,700. $T = (2850 - 2700)/(420/7) = 2{,}50$. Two-sided p-value $\approx 0{,}016 < 0{,}05$. Reject $H_0$.

### Case 2 — Comparison of groups on ENEM

Public school students (group 1, $n_1 = 200$, $\bar X_1 = 540$, $s_1 = 80$) vs private (group 2, $n_2 = 200$, $\bar X_2 = 590$, $s_2 = 75$). Welch $T$, 95% CI for difference.

### Case 3 — A/B testing of a landing page

Version A: 120 conversions in 1000 visitors. Version B: 150/1000. z test for difference of proportions:
$\hat p_A = 0{,}12$, $\hat p_B = 0{,}15$, $\hat p = 0{,}135$.
$Z = (0{,}15 - 0{,}12)/\sqrt{0{,}135 \cdot 0{,}865 \cdot (1/1000 + 1/1000)} = 2{,}09$.
Two-sided p-value $\approx 0{,}037 < 0{,}05$. B is better at 5% level.

### Case 4 — Fertilizers and productivity (ANOVA)

4 fertilizers, 25 plants each, productivity (kg/ha). ANOVA $F(3, 96)$. If $F > F_{0,05;\,3,96} \approx 2{,}70$, some fertilizer differs. Post-hoc: Tukey HSD.

### Case 5 — Regression of real estate price

$Y$ = price (R$ thousand), $X_1 = m^2$, $X_2$ = number of bedrooms, $X_3$ = age (years). OLS fit with $n = 200$ properties. Interpretation of each $\hat\beta_i$, $R^2_{\text{adj}}$, and residual diagnostics.

### Case 6 — Bayes in quality control

Proportion of defects $\theta$ in batch of parts. Prior: $\text{Beta}(2, 18)$ (experience: ~10% defects). Inspect $n = 50$, find $x = 8$ defects. Posterior: $\text{Beta}(10, 60)$. Posterior mean: $10/70 \approx 14{,}3\%$. 95% credible interval via Beta quantiles.

## Worked examples

<Exemplo titulo="95% CI for average ENEM score (unknown deviation)" numero="1">

**Problem.** A sample of $n = 36$ ENEM 2023 candidates in the Mathematics subject showed mean $\bar X = 580$ points and sample standard deviation $s = 90$ points. Construct a 95% CI for the population mean $\mu$.

**Strategy.** Since $\sigma$ is unknown, use the $t$ distribution with $n - 1 = 35$ degrees of freedom. Locate $t_{0,025;\,35}$ in table.

**Resolution.**

$t_{0,025;\,35} = 2{,}030$.

Margin of error: $E = 2{,}030 \times \frac{90}{\sqrt{36}} = 2{,}030 \times 15 = 30{,}45$.

95% CI: $(580 - 30{,}45\;;\; 580 + 30{,}45) = (549{,}55\;;\; 610{,}45)$.

**Verification.** The margin grows with $s$ and decreases with $\sqrt{n}$. For $n = 144$, the margin would be $2{,}030 \times 90/12 = 15{,}2$ — half, since we quadrupled $n$.

**Source.** [OpenIntro Statistics §5.2](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/) — Exercise 5.5, p. 222 · CC-BY-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="One-sample t test vs government target" numero="2">

**Problem.** The government claims that average care time at health centers is $\mu_0 = 20$ minutes. An audit samples $n = 25$ care visits: $\bar X = 24{,}8$ min, $s = 7{,}2$ min. At $\alpha = 5\%$ level, is there evidence that the true mean exceeds 20 min?

**Strategy.** One-sided t test to the right ($H_1: \mu > 20$), with $df = 24$.

**Resolution.**

$T = \frac{24{,}8 - 20}{7{,}2/\sqrt{25}} = \frac{4{,}8}{1{,}44} = 3{,}33$.

$t_{0,05;\,24} = 1{,}711$ (one-sided). Since $3{,}33 > 1{,}711$, reject $H_0$.

p-value: $P(t_{24} \geq 3{,}33) \approx 0{,}001 < 0{,}05$. There is evidence that mean time exceeds 20 minutes.

**Verification.** 95% one-sided lower CI: $24{,}8 - 1{,}711 \times 1{,}44 = 22{,}33$ min. The value $\mu_0 = 20$ lies below this limit, confirming rejection.

**Source.** [Statistics, OpenStax §9.4](https://openstax.org/books/statistics/pages/9-4-comparing-two-independent-population-means) — adapted from example 9.10, p. 476 · CC-BY.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="One-factor ANOVA — productivity with 3 fertilizers" numero="3">

**Problem.** Three fertilizers were tested on groups of $n_i = 10$ plants each. Productivity (kg): $\bar X_1 = 12{,}0$, $\bar X_2 = 14{,}5$, $\bar X_3 = 13{,}2$. Overall mean $\bar X = 13{,}23$. $SS_W = 162$ (within-group sum of squares). At 5% level, is there difference among fertilizers?

**Strategy.** ANOVA: compute $SS_B$, obtain $F$, compare with $F_{0,05;\,2,\,27}$.

**Resolution.**

$SS_B = 10[(12{,}0 - 13{,}23)^2 + (14{,}5 - 13{,}23)^2 + (13{,}2 - 13{,}23)^2]$
$= 10[1{,}513 + 1{,}613 + 0{,}001] = 31{,}27$.

$MS_B = 31{,}27/2 = 15{,}64$; $MS_W = 162/27 = 6{,}00$.

$F = 15{,}64 / 6{,}00 = 2{,}61$.

$F_{0,05;\,2,\,27} \approx 3{,}35$. Since $2{,}61 < 3{,}35$, **do not reject $H_0$** — no evidence of difference among fertilizers at 5% level.

**Verification.** p-value $\approx 0{,}092 > 0{,}05$. Result consistent. With $n_i = 20$ per group, power would be higher and rejection might occur.

**Source.** [OpenIntro Statistics §7.5](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/) — structure based on ANOVA example, p. 319 · CC-BY-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Simple linear regression — salary vs years of experience" numero="4">

**Problem.** With $n = 20$ professionals: $\bar X = 8$ years, average salary R$ 6,200 ($\bar Y = 6.200$), $S_{xx} = 180$, $S_{xy} = 54.000$. Fit the regression model and interpret the coefficients.

**Strategy.** Compute $\hat\beta_1$ and $\hat\beta_0$ by least squares formulas; interpret in context.

**Resolution.**

$\hat\beta_1 = S_{xy}/S_{xx} = 54.000/180 = 300$.

$\hat\beta_0 = \bar Y - \hat\beta_1 \bar X = 6.200 - 300 \times 8 = 3.800$.

Model: $\hat Y = 3.800 + 300\,X$ (in reais, $X$ in years).

**Interpretation.** Each additional year of experience is associated with an average salary increase of R$ 300. The intercept R$ 3,800 is the estimated salary for 0 years of experience (extrapolation — use with caution).

**Verification.** For $X = 10$ years: $\hat Y = 3.800 + 3.000 = 6.800$ reais (R$ 6,800). Plausible given $\bar Y = 6.200$ for $\bar X = 8$.

**Source.** [OpenIntro Statistics §8.2](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/) — based on Exercise 8.13 (salary data), p. 360 · CC-BY-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Bayesian updating — proportion of defects in production line" numero="5">

**Problem.** A quality engineer believes a priori that the proportion of defects $\theta$ in a production line has distribution $\text{Beta}(3, 17)$ (prior mean $= 3/20 = 15\%$). He inspects $n = 40$ parts and finds $X = 4$ defects. What is the posterior distribution and what is the new point estimate for $\theta$?

**Strategy.** Use Beta-Binomial conjugate: posterior $= \text{Beta}(\alpha + X, \beta + n - X)$.

**Resolution.**

Posterior: $\text{Beta}(3 + 4,\; 17 + 40 - 4) = \text{Beta}(7, 53)$.

Posterior mean: $7/(7+53) = 7/60 \approx 11{,}7\%$.

95% credible interval: 2.5% and 97.5% quantiles of $\text{Beta}(7, 53)$, approximately $[0{,}049;\; 0{,}221]$.

**Verification.** Before: prior mean 15%. After seeing 4 defects in 40 (MLE = 10%), the posterior shifts to 11.7% — weighted average of prior and data. With larger samples, the posterior would approach the MLE.

**Source.** [Statistical Thinking for the 21st Century, Ch. 15](https://statsthinking21.org/) — structure of Beta-Binomial example, §15.3 · CC-BY-NC.

</Exemplo>
