<Porta nivel="formal" titulo="Rigorous definition">

### Multiple linear regression model

<Definicao titulo="Model and matrix notation">

For $n$ observations and $p$ predictors:

$$Y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_{i1} + \beta_2 X_{i2} + \cdots + \beta_p X_{ip} + \varepsilon_i, \quad \varepsilon_i \stackrel{i.i.d.}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2)$$

In matrix form: $\mathbf{Y} = X\boldsymbol\beta + \boldsymbol\varepsilon$, where:

- $\mathbf{Y} \in \mathbb{R}^n$: response vector.
- $X \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times(p+1)}$: design matrix (first column of 1s for the intercept).
- $\boldsymbol\beta \in \mathbb{R}^{p+1}$: coefficient vector.
- $\boldsymbol\varepsilon \in \mathbb{R}^n$: error vector.

</Definicao>

> "The multiple regression model is $y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \beta_2 x_2 + \cdots + \beta_k x_k + \varepsilon$. The coefficient $\beta_i$ measures the expected change in $y$ per unit change in $x_i$ when all other predictors are held constant." — [OpenIntro Statistics, §8.1](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/), p. 362

<Definicao titulo="OLS estimator and normal equations">

Minimizing $SSE = \|\mathbf{Y} - X\boldsymbol\beta\|^2$ leads to the normal equations:

$$X^TX\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} = X^T\mathbf{Y}$$

whose solution (when $X^TX$ is invertible) is:

<Equation explicacao="OLS matrix estimator: exists when predictors are linearly independent." latex="\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} = (X^TX)^{-1}X^T\mathbf{Y}" />

The variance-covariance matrix of the estimators is:

<Equation explicacao="Estimator covariance: inversely proportional to X'X. More data or more variation in X reduces variance." latex="\text{Cov}(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}) = \sigma^2 (X^TX)^{-1}" />

</Definicao>

### Fit metrics

<Definicao titulo="Adjusted R²">

$R^2$ increases with any added predictor — even irrelevant ones. Adjusted $R^2$ penalizes for the number of parameters:

<Equation explicacao="Adjusted R²: penalizes for complexity. Can drop when adding a weak predictor." latex="\bar R^2 = 1 - \frac{SSE/(n-p-1)}{SST/(n-1)}" />

If $\bar R^2$ increases with a new predictor, the predictor improves the model; if it falls, it hurts.

</Definicao>

### Inference

<Definicao titulo="Partial t-test and global F-test">

**Partial $t$-test** for $H_0: \beta_j = 0$ (with other predictors in the model):

<Equation explicacao="Tests whether predictor Xj has a significant effect given that the others are already in the model." latex="T_j = \frac{\hat\beta_j}{SE(\hat\beta_j)} \sim t_{n-p-1}" />

**Global $F$-test** for $H_0: \beta_1 = \cdots = \beta_p = 0$:

<Equation explicacao="Tests whether the model as a whole explains more than the mean of Y." latex="F = \frac{SSR/p}{SSE/(n-p-1)} \sim F_{p,\,n-p-1}" />

</Definicao>

<Definicao titulo="Multicollinearity and VIF">

When two or more predictors are highly correlated, $X^TX$ becomes nearly singular and the $SE(\hat\beta_j)$ inflate — coefficients become unstable and hard to interpret.

**Variance Inflation Factor:**

<Equation explicacao="VIF measures how much the variance of beta_j increases due to correlation with other predictors. VIF > 10 is concerning." latex="VIF_j = \frac{1}{1 - R_j^2}" />

where $R_j^2$ is the $R^2$ of regressing $X_j$ on the other predictors.

</Definicao>

<Insight>

In simple regression, $\hat\beta_1$ and the correlation coefficient always have the same sign. In multiple regression, the correlation between $X_j$ and $Y$ can have the opposite sign from $\hat\beta_j$ (suppression effect). This occurs because the partial coefficient controls for the other predictors.

</Insight>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

In the previous lesson, you used **one ruler** to predict one thing from another: height to predict weight.

Now imagine you want to predict a person's weight and have **two pieces of information**: height AND age. With two pieces of information together, your prediction gets better!

Multiple regression is like having **several rulers at the same time** — each measuring something different — and all together they make a more accurate prediction.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

In simple regression, you used just **one variable** ($X$) to predict ($Y$). But we almost always have more information available.

For example, to predict the **price of a house** you can use:
- $X_1$ = area in m²
- $X_2$ = number of bedrooms
- $X_3$ = distance from downtown (in km)

Multiple regression fits a formula like:

$$\hat Y = \hat\beta_0 + \hat\beta_1 X_1 + \hat\beta_2 X_2 + \hat\beta_3 X_3$$

Each $\hat\beta_j$ says: "if I increase $X_j$ by one unit, keeping the others fixed, how much does $Y$ change on average?"

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old teen">

Multiple regression generalizes simple regression by including $p$ predictors. The model is:

$$Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_1 + \cdots + \beta_p X_p + \varepsilon$$

**Interpretation of coefficients:** $\hat\beta_j$ is the effect of $X_j$ on $Y$, **holding all other predictors constant** (partial effect or "ceteris paribus").

**Why does this matter?** Imagine predicting an employee's salary using years of experience ($X_1$) and level of education ($X_2$). The effect of experience on salary is different when you control for education than when you ignore education.

**R² vs. adjusted R²:** adding any variable increases $R^2$, even if it is irrelevant. Adjusted $\bar R^2$ penalizes for extra variables — use it to compare models with different numbers of predictors.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

**Matrix algebra of the model**

Given $X \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times(p+1)}$ with column rank $p+1$ (linearly independent predictors), OLS solves:

$$\min_{\boldsymbol\beta} \|\mathbf{Y} - X\boldsymbol\beta\|^2 \implies \hat{\boldsymbol\beta} = (X^TX)^{-1}X^T\mathbf{Y}$$

The vector of fitted values is $\hat{\mathbf{Y}} = H\mathbf{Y}$, where $H = X(X^TX)^{-1}X^T$ is the **hat matrix** (orthogonal projection onto the column space of $X$). Residuals $\mathbf{e} = (I-H)\mathbf{Y}$ are orthogonal to $\hat{\mathbf{Y}}$.

**Multiple ANOVA table**

| Source | SS | df | MS | F |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Regression | $SSR$ | $p$ | $SSR/p$ | $MSR/MSE$ |
| Error | $SSE$ | $n-p-1$ | $SSE/(n-p-1)$ | — |
| Total | $SST$ | $n-1$ | — | — |

**Variable selection:** AIC, BIC, $\bar R^2$, cross-validation. Automatic methods: stepwise (forward, backward, both) — useful but do not replace substantive reasoning.

**Multicollinearity:** $\text{det}(X^TX) \to 0$ implies unstable inversion. Ridge regression ($L_2$): $\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}_{ridge} = (X^TX + \lambda I)^{-1}X^T\mathbf{Y}$ regularizes without selection; Lasso ($L_1$) selects variables automatically.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

Multiple regression dominates empirical analysis in social sciences, epidemiology and finance. Central methodological debates:

**Causal identification:** OLS with controls does not resolve endogeneity. The "potential outcomes model" (Rubin) distinguishes causal effect from conditional association. Design based on exogenous variation (natural experiments, RDD, DiD, IV) is the modern language of causality in economics — recognized by the 2021 Nobel Prize in Economics (Card, Angrist, Imbens). See [nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/2021/summary/](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/2021/summary/).

**Overfitting and validation:** in small samples with many predictors ($p \approx n$), OLS overfits. Regularization (ridge, lasso, elastic net) and $k$-fold cross-validation are standard in ML; in econometrics, using separate test data remains the norm.

**Robust standard errors:** heteroscedasticity and serial correlation violate $\text{Cov}(\boldsymbol\varepsilon) = \sigma^2 I$. White (HC) and Newey-West (HAC) errors correct the variance matrix without requiring functional form for heteroscedasticity.

**High-dimensional regression ($p > n$):** OLS has no unique solution. Lasso via coordinate descent, PCR, PLS. Post-selection inference (debiased Lasso) is an active research area.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Brazilian application">

**Real estate prices in São Paulo (data simulated per IBGE FipeZap pattern)**

Regression of sale price $Y$ (in R\$ thousands) on: area $X_1$ (m²), number of bedrooms $X_2$ and distance from metro $X_3$ (km). With $n=150$ transactions:

$$\hat Y = 180 + 4.2 X_1 + 35 X_2 - 12 X_3$$

- $\hat\beta_1 = 4.2$: each additional m² is worth, on average, R\$ 4,200 (controlling for bedrooms and metro).
- $\hat\beta_2 = 35$: each extra bedroom adds R\$ 35,000 (controlling for area and metro).
- $\hat\beta_3 = -12$: each km farther from metro reduces R\$ 12,000 (controlling for area and bedrooms).
- $R^2 = 0.78$, $\bar R^2 = 0.77$: the model explains 77% of price variability after adjusting for complexity.

**Global F-test:** $F = 172.4$ with p-value less than 0.001 — the three predictors together are highly significant.

**Caution:** multicollinearity between area and bedrooms ($r = 0.69$) inflates the $SE$ — VIFs calculated: $VIF_1 = 2.3$, $VIF_2 = 2.1$, $VIF_3 = 1.1$. All below 10: acceptable multicollinearity.

</Porta>

## Worked examples

<Exemplo numero="106.1" titulo="Fit and interpretation with two predictors">

**Problem.** Regression of annual salary $Y$ (R\$ thousands) on experience $X_1$ (years) and education $X_2$ (years of study) produced: $\hat Y = -12 + 2.8 X_1 + 5.2 X_2$, with $n=50$, $R^2=0.74$, $\bar R^2=0.73$.

Interpret each coefficient and calculate the prediction for a professional with 10 years of experience and 16 years of education.

**Strategy.** Interpret each $\hat\beta_j$ as a partial effect (ceteris paribus). Substitute values into the fitted equation.

**Solution.**

- $\hat\beta_0 = -12$: intercept (no meaningful substantive interpretation alone — no professional has zero years of experience and education).
- $\hat\beta_1 = 2.8$: holding education constant, each additional year of experience is associated with R\$ 2,800/year more, on average.
- $\hat\beta_2 = 5.2$: holding experience constant, each additional year of education is associated with R\$ 5,200/year more.

Prediction: $\hat Y = -12 + 2.8 \times 10 + 5.2 \times 16 = -12+28+83.2 = 99.2$ thousand R\$/year.

**Verification.** $\bar R^2 = 0.73$ close to $R^2 = 0.74$: only 2 predictors, little penalization — parsimonious model.

**Source.** [OpenIntro Statistics, §8.1, Example 8.1](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/) — CC-BY-SA

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="106.2" titulo="Adjusted R² in model selection">

**Problem.** Three models to predict household electricity consumption ($n=80$):

| Model | Predictors | $R^2$ | $\bar R^2$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| M1 | area | 0.61 | 0.61 |
| M2 | area + number of residents | 0.71 | 0.70 |
| M3 | area + number of residents + income + temperature + day of week | 0.74 | 0.70 |

Which model to prefer?

**Strategy.** Compare $\bar R^2$ — penalizes for number of predictors.

**Solution.**

M2 and M3 have the same $\bar R^2 = 0.70$, but M3 uses 5 predictors against 2 in M2. By the principle of parsimony (Occam's razor), **M2 is preferred**: adding 3 predictors does not improve $\bar R^2$ — they do not contribute net information.

Note that $R^2$ of M3 (0.74) exceeds M2 (0.71) — but $R^2$ always grows with additional predictors, even irrelevant ones.

**Verification.** Could confirm with AIC/BIC: $AIC = n\ln(SSE/n) + 2(p+1)$ — lower is better.

**Source.** [OpenIntro Statistics, §8.3, Table 8.7](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/) — CC-BY-SA

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="106.3" titulo="Global F-test and ANOVA table">

**Problem.** Regression with $p=3$ predictors, $n=40$: $SST=2400$, $SSE=720$. Build the ANOVA table and test $H_0: \beta_1=\beta_2=\beta_3=0$ at 5% level.

**Strategy.** Calculate SSR, df, MS, F; compare with critical value $F_{3,36;\,0.05}$.

**Solution.**

$SSR = 2400-720 = 1680$; $R^2 = 1680/2400 = 0.70$.

| Source | SS | df | MS | F |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Regression | 1680 | 3 | 560 | 28.0 |
| Error | 720 | 36 | 20 | — |
| Total | 2400 | 39 | — | — |

$F_{3,36;\,0.05} \approx 2.87$. Since $F = 28.0 \gg 2.87$, we reject $H_0$: the model as a whole is highly significant.

**Verification.** $MSE = 20 \implies \hat\sigma = \sqrt{20} \approx 4.47$ — residual standard deviation in units of $Y$.

**Source.** [OpenStax Statistics, §13.4](https://openstax.org/books/statistics/pages/13-introduction) — CC-BY

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="106.4" titulo="Detect multicollinearity via VIF">

**Problem.** Regression of vehicle price ($Y$) on power ($X_1$), displacement ($X_2$) and weight ($X_3$). Auxiliary regressions: $R_1^2=0.89$ ($X_1$ on $X_2, X_3$), $R_2^2=0.91$, $R_3^2=0.85$. Calculate the VIFs and evaluate.

**Strategy.** $VIF_j = 1/(1-R_j^2)$.

**Solution.**

$VIF_1 = 1/(1-0.89) = 9.09$; $VIF_2 = 1/(1-0.91) = 11.1$; $VIF_3 = 1/(1-0.85) = 6.67$.

$VIF_2 = 11.1 > 10$: displacement exhibits severe multicollinearity with the other variables.

**Action:** remove $X_2$ from the model or combine $X_1$ and $X_2$ into a composite index (principal component analysis).

**Verification.** After removing $X_2$, recalculate VIFs for remaining predictors.

**Source.** [OpenIntro Statistics, §8.4](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/) — CC-BY-SA

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="106.5" titulo="Dummy variable and interaction">

**Problem.** Predict salary $Y$ (R\$ thousands/year) using experience $X_1$ (years) and gender $D$ (1=female, 0=male). Result: $\hat Y = 45 + 2.5X_1 - 8D$.

Interpret the coefficient of $D$ and calculate predicted salaries for: (a) man with 10 years of experience; (b) woman with 10 years of experience.

**Strategy.** The dummy variable creates two different intercepts (one per gender), maintaining equal slope.

**Solution.**

$\hat\beta_D = -8$: controlling for experience, women earn on average R\$ 8,000/year less than men — difference net (not causal, since we do not control for sector, position, hours).

(a) Man ($D=0$), $X_1=10$: $\hat Y = 45 + 25 - 0 = 70$ thousand R\$/year.

(b) Woman ($D=1$), $X_1=10$: $\hat Y = 45 + 25 - 8 = 62$ thousand R\$/year.

**Verification.** The difference is constant ($-8$) for any experience level — non-interaction model assumes additive effect. To allow different slopes, add $X_1 \times D$.

**Source.** [OpenIntro Statistics, §8.2, Example 8.4](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/) — CC-BY-SA

</Exemplo>
