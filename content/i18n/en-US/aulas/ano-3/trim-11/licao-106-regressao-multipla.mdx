<Porta nivel="formal" titulo="Rigorous definition">

### Multiple linear regression model

<Definicao titulo="Model and matrix notation">

For <Eq>{`n`}</Eq> observations and <Eq>{`p`}</Eq> predictors:

$$Y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_{i1} + \beta_2 X_{i2} + \cdots + \beta_p X_{ip} + \varepsilon_i, \quad \varepsilon_i \stackrel{i.i.d.}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2)$$

In matrix form: <Eq>{`\\mathbf{Y} = X\\boldsymbol\\beta + \\boldsymbol\\varepsilon`}</Eq>, where:

- <Eq>{`\\mathbf{Y} \\in \\mathbb{R}^n`}</Eq>: response vector.
- <Eq>{`X \\in \\mathbb{R}^{n\\times(p+1)}`}</Eq>: design matrix (first column of 1s for the intercept).
- <Eq>{`\\boldsymbol\\beta \\in \\mathbb{R}^{p+1}`}</Eq>: coefficient vector.
- <Eq>{`\\boldsymbol\\varepsilon \\in \\mathbb{R}^n`}</Eq>: error vector.

</Definicao>

> "The multiple regression model is <Eq>{`y = \\beta_0 + \\beta_1 x_1 + \\beta_2 x_2 + \\cdots + \\beta_k x_k + \\varepsilon`}</Eq>. The coefficient <Eq>{`\\beta_i`}</Eq> measures the expected change in <Eq>{`y`}</Eq> per unit change in <Eq>{`x_i`}</Eq> when all other predictors are held constant." — [OpenIntro Statistics, §8.1](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/), p. 362

<Definicao titulo="OLS estimator and normal equations">

Minimizing <Eq>{`SSE = \\|\\mathbf{Y} - X\\boldsymbol\\beta\\|^2`}</Eq> leads to the normal equations:

$$X^TX\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} = X^T\mathbf{Y}$$

whose solution (when <Eq>{`X^TX`}</Eq> is invertible) is:

<Equation explicacao="Matrix OLS estimator: exists when predictors are linearly independent." latex="\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} = (X^TX)^{-1}X^T\mathbf{Y}" />

The variance-covariance matrix of the estimators is:

<Equation explicacao="Estimator Cov: inversely proportional to X'X. More data or more variation in X reduces variance." latex="\text{Cov}(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}) = \sigma^2 (X^TX)^{-1}" />

</Definicao>

### Goodness-of-fit metrics

<Definicao titulo="Adjusted R²">

<Eq>{`R^2`}</Eq> increases with any added predictor — even an irrelevant one. Adjusted <Eq>{`R^2`}</Eq> penalizes for the number of parameters:

<Equation explicacao="Adjusted R²: penalizes for complexity. May drop when adding a weak predictor." latex="\bar R^2 = 1 - \frac{SSE/(n-p-1)}{SST/(n-1)}" />

If <Eq>{`\\bar R^2`}</Eq> increases with a new predictor, the predictor improves the model; if it drops, it hurts it.

</Definicao>

### Inference

<Definicao titulo="Partial t-test and global F-test">

**Partial <Eq>{`t`}</Eq>-test** for <Eq>{`H_0: \\beta_j = 0`}</Eq> (with other predictors in the model):

<Equation explicacao="Tests if predictor Xj has a significant effect given the others are already in the model." latex="T_j = \frac{\hat\beta_j}{SE(\hat\beta_j)} \sim t_{n-p-1}" />

**Global <Eq>{`F`}</Eq>-test** for <Eq>{`H_0: \\beta_1 = \\cdots = \\beta_p = 0`}</Eq>:

<Equation explicacao="Tests if the model as a whole explains more than the mean of Y." latex="F = \frac{SSR/p}{SSE/(n-p-1)} \sim F_{p,\,n-p-1}" />

</Definicao>

<Definicao titulo="Multicollinearity and VIF">

When two or more predictors are highly correlated, <Eq>{`X^TX`}</Eq> becomes near-singular and <Eq>{`SE(\\hat\\beta_j)`}</Eq> inflates — coefficients become unstable and difficult to interpret.

**Variance Inflation Factor:**

<Equation explicacao="VIF measures how much the variance of beta_j increases due to correlation with other predictors. VIF > 10 is concerning." latex="VIF_j = \frac{1}{1 - R_j^2}" />

where <Eq>{`R_j^2`}</Eq> is the <Eq>{`R^2`}</Eq> of the regression of <Eq>{`X_j`}</Eq> on the other predictors.

</Definicao>

<Insight>

In simple regression, <Eq>{`\\hat\\beta_1`}</Eq> and the correlation coefficient always have the same sign. In multiple regression, the correlation between <Eq>{`X_j`}</Eq> and <Eq>{`Y`}</Eq> can have the opposite sign to <Eq>{`\\hat\\beta_j`}</Eq> (suppression effect). This occurs because the partial coefficient controls for the other predictors.

</Insight>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

In the previous lesson, you used **one ruler** to predict one thing with another: height to predict weight.

Now imagine you want to predict a person's weight and you have **two pieces of information**: height AND age. With two pieces of information together, your prediction becomes better!

Multiple regression is like having **several rulers at the same time** — each one measures something different — and all together they make a more accurate prediction.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

In simple regression, you used only **one variable** (<Eq>{`X`}</Eq>) to predict (<Eq>{`Y`}</Eq>). But we almost always have more information available.

For example, to predict the **price of a house** you can use:
- <Eq>{`X_1`}</Eq> = area in m²
- <Eq>{`X_2`}</Eq> = number of bedrooms
- <Eq>{`X_3`}</Eq> = distance from the center (in km)

Multiple regression fits a formula like:

$$\hat Y = \hat\beta_0 + \hat\beta_1 X_1 + \hat\beta_2 X_2 + \hat\beta_3 X_3$$

Each <Eq>{`\\hat\\beta_j`}</Eq> says: "if I increase <Eq>{`X_j`}</Eq> by one unit, while keeping the others fixed, how much does <Eq>{`Y`}</Eq> change on average?"

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old">

Multiple regression generalizes simple regression by including <Eq>{`p`}</Eq> predictors. The model is:

$$Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_1 + \cdots + \beta_p X_p + \varepsilon$$

**Interpretation of coefficients:** <Eq>{`\\hat\\beta_j`}</Eq> is the effect of <Eq>{`X_j`}</Eq> on <Eq>{`Y`}</Eq>, **keeping all other predictors constant** (partial effect or "ceteris paribus").

**Why does this matter?** Imagine predicting an employee's salary using years of experience (<Eq>{`X_1`}</Eq>) and education level (<Eq>{`X_2`}</Eq>). The effect of experience on salary is different when you control for education than when you ignore it.

**R² vs. adjusted R²:** adding any variable increases <Eq>{`R^2`}</Eq>, even if it is irrelevant. The <Eq>{`\\bar R^2`}</Eq> (adjusted) penalizes for extra variables — use it to compare models with different numbers of predictors.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

**Matrix algebra of the model**

Given <Eq>{`X \\in \\mathbb{R}^{n\\times(p+1)}`}</Eq> with column rank <Eq>{`p+1`}</Eq> (LI predictors), OLS solves:

$$\min_{\boldsymbol\beta} \|\mathbf{Y} - X\boldsymbol\beta\|^2 \implies \hat{\boldsymbol\beta} = (X^TX)^{-1}X^T\mathbf{Y}$$

The vector of fitted values is <Eq>{`\\hat{\\mathbf{Y}} = H\\mathbf{Y}`}</Eq>, where <Eq>{`H = X(X^TX)^{-1}X^T`}</Eq> is the **hat matrix** (orthogonal projection onto the column space of <Eq>{`X`}</Eq>). The residuals <Eq>{`\\mathbf{e} = (I-H)\\mathbf{Y}`}</Eq> are orthogonal to <Eq>{`\\hat{\\mathbf{Y}}`}</Eq>.

**Multiple ANOVA table**

| Source | SS | df | MS | F |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Regression | <Eq>{`SSR`}</Eq> | <Eq>{`p`}</Eq> | <Eq>{`SSR/p`}</Eq> | <Eq>{`MSR/MSE`}</Eq> |
| Error | <Eq>{`SSE`}</Eq> | <Eq>{`n-p-1`}</Eq> | <Eq>{`SSE/(n-p-1)`}</Eq> | — |
| Total | <Eq>{`SST`}</Eq> | <Eq>{`n-1`}</Eq> | — | — |

**Variable selection:** AIC, BIC, <Eq>{`\\bar R^2`}</Eq>, cross-validation. Automatic methods: stepwise (forward, backward, both) — useful but do not replace substantive reasoning.

**Multicollinearity:** <Eq>{`\\text{det}(X^TX) \\to 0`}</Eq> implies unstable inversion. Ridge regression (<Eq>{`L_2`}</Eq>): <Eq>{`\\hat{\\boldsymbol\\beta}_{ridge} = (X^TX + \\lambda I)^{-1}X^T\\mathbf{Y}`}</Eq> regularizes without selection; Lasso (<Eq>{`L_1`}</Eq>) selects variables automatically.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

Multiple regression dominates empirical analysis in social sciences, epidemiology, and finance. Central methodological debates:

**Causal identification:** OLS with controls does not solve endogeneity. The "potential outcome model" (Rubin) distinguishes causal effect from conditional association. Design based on exogenous variation (natural experiments, RDD, DiD, IV) is the modern language of causality in economics — recognized by the 2021 Nobel Prize in Economics (Card, Angrist, Imbens). See [nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/2021/summary/](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/2021/summary/).

**Overfitting and validation:** in small samples with many predictors (<Eq>{`p \\approx n`}</Eq>), OLS overfits. Regularization (ridge, lasso, elastic net) and <Eq>{`k`}</Eq>-fold cross-validation are the standard in ML; in econometrics, the use of separate test data is still the norm.

**Robust standard errors:** heteroscedasticity and serial correlation violate <Eq>{`\\text{Cov}(\\boldsymbol\\varepsilon) = \\sigma^2 I`}</Eq>. White (HC) and Newey-West (HAC) errors correct the variance matrix without requiring a functional form for heteroscedasticity.

**High-dimensional regression (<Eq>{`p > n`}</Eq>):** OLS has no unique solution. Lasso via coordinate descent, PCR, PLS. Post-selection inference (debiased Lasso) is an active area of research.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Brazilian application">

**Real estate prices in São Paulo (simulated data according to IBGE FipeZap standard)**

Regression of sale price <Eq>{`Y`}</Eq> (in R$ thousand) on: area <Eq>{`X_1`}</Eq> (m²), number of bedrooms <Eq>{`X_2`}</Eq>, and distance to subway <Eq>{`X_3`}</Eq> (km). With <Eq>{`n=150`}</Eq> transactions:

$$\hat Y = 180 + 4{,}2 X_1 + 35 X_2 - 12 X_3$$

- <Eq>{`\\hat\\beta_1 = 4{,}2`}</Eq>: each additional m² is worth, on average, R$ 4,200 (controlling for bedrooms and subway).
- <Eq>{`\\hat\\beta_2 = 35`}</Eq>: each extra bedroom adds R$ 35,000 (controlling for area and subway).
- <Eq>{`\\hat\\beta_3 = -12`}</Eq>: each km further from the subway reduces R$ 12,000 (controlling for area and bedrooms).
- <Eq>{`R^2 = 0{,}78`}</Eq>, <Eq>{`\\bar R^2 = 0{,}77`}</Eq>: the model explains 77% of price variability adjusting for complexity.

**Global F-test:** <Eq>{`F = 172{,}4`}</Eq> with <Eq>{`p`}</Eq>-value less than 0.001 — the three predictors together are highly significant.

**Caution:** multicollinearity between area and bedrooms (<Eq>{`r = 0{,}69`}</Eq>) inflates <Eq>{`SE`}</Eq> — calculated VIFs: <Eq>{`VIF_1 = 2{,}3`}</Eq>, <Eq>{`VIF_2 = 2{,}1`}</Eq>, <Eq>{`VIF_3 = 1{,}1`}</Eq>. All below 10: acceptable multicollinearity.

</Porta>

## Solved examples

<Exemplo numero="106.1" titulo="Fit and interpretation with two predictors">

**Problem.** Regression of annual salary <Eq>{`Y`}</Eq> (R$ thousand) on experience <Eq>{`X_1`}</Eq> (years) and education <Eq>{`X_2`}</Eq> (years of study) produced: <Eq>{`\\hat Y = -12 + 2{,}8 X_1 + 5{,}2 X_2`}</Eq>, with <Eq>{`n=50`}</Eq>, <Eq>{`R^2=0{,}74`}</Eq>, <Eq>{`\\bar R^2=0{,}73`}</Eq>.

Interpret each coefficient and calculate the prediction for a professional with 10 years of experience and 16 years of study.

**Strategy.** Interpret each <Eq>{`\\hat\\beta_j`}</Eq> as a partial effect (ceteris paribus). Substitute the values into the fitted equation.

**Resolution.**

- <Eq>{`\\hat\\beta_0 = -12`}</Eq>: intercept (no substantive isolated interpretation — no professional has zero years of experience and study).
- <Eq>{`\\hat\\beta_1 = 2{,}8`}</Eq>: controlling for education, each additional year of experience is associated with R$ 2,800/year more, on average.
- <Eq>{`\\hat\\beta_2 = 5{,}2`}</Eq>: controlling for experience, each additional year of study is associated with R$ 5,200/year more.

Prediction: <Eq>{`\\hat Y = -12 + 2{,}8\\times10 + 5{,}2\\times16 = -12+28+83{,}2 = 99{,}2`}</Eq> thousand R$/year.

**Verification.** <Eq>{`\\bar R^2 = 0{,}73`}</Eq> close to <Eq>{`R^2 = 0{,}74`}</Eq>: only 2 predictors, little penalty — parsimonious model.

**Source.** [OpenIntro Statistics, §8.1, Example 8.1](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/) — CC-BY-SA

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="106.2" titulo="Adjusted R² in model selection">

**Problem.** Three models to predict household electricity consumption (<Eq>{`n=80`}</Eq>):

| Model | Predictors | <Eq>{`R^2`}</Eq> | <Eq>{`\\bar R^2`}</Eq> |
|---|---|---|---|
| M1 | area | 0.61 | 0.61 |
| M2 | area + # residents | 0.71 | 0.70 |
| M3 | area + # residents + income + temp + day of week | 0.74 | 0.70 |

Which model to prefer?

**Strategy.** Compare <Eq>{`\\bar R^2`}</Eq> — penalizes for number of predictors.

**Resolution.**

M2 and M3 have the same <Eq>{`\\bar R^2 = 0{,}70`}</Eq>, but M3 uses 5 predictors against M2's 2. By the principle of parsimony (Occam's razor), **M2 is preferred**: adding 3 predictors does not improve <Eq>{`\\bar R^2`}</Eq> — they do not contribute net information.

Note that M3's <Eq>{`R^2`}</Eq> (0.74) exceeds M2's (0.71) — but <Eq>{`R^2`}</Eq> always grows with added predictors, even irrelevant ones.

**Verification.** Could confirm with AIC/BIC: <Eq>{`AIC = n\\ln(SSE/n) + 2(p+1)`}</Eq> — lower is better.

**Source.** [OpenIntro Statistics, §8.3, Table 8.7](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/) — CC-BY-SA

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="106.3" titulo="Global F-test and ANOVA table">

**Problem.** Regression with <Eq>{`p=3`}</Eq> predictors, <Eq>{`n=40`}</Eq>: <Eq>{`SST=2400`}</Eq>, <Eq>{`SSE=720`}</Eq>. Build the ANOVA table and test <Eq>{`H_0: \\beta_1=\\beta_2=\\beta_3=0`}</Eq> at the 5% level.

**Strategy.** Calculate SSR, df, MS, F; compare with critical value <Eq>{`F_{3,36;\\,0{,}05}`}</Eq>.

**Resolution.**

<Eq>{`SSR = 2400-720 = 1680`}</Eq>; <Eq>{`R^2 = 1680/2400 = 0{,}70`}</Eq>.

| Source | SS | df | MS | F |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Regression | 1680 | 3 | 560 | 28.0 |
| Error | 720 | 36 | 20 | — |
| Total | 2400 | 39 | — | — |

<Eq>{`F_{3,36;\\,0{,}05} \\approx 2{,}87`}</Eq>. Since <Eq>{`F = 28{,}0 \\gg 2{,}87`}</Eq>, we reject <Eq>{`H_0`}</Eq>: the model as a whole is highly significant.

**Verification.** <Eq>{`MSE = 20 \\implies \\hat\\sigma = \\sqrt{20} \\approx 4{,}47`}</Eq> — residual standard deviation in units of <Eq>{`Y`}</Eq>.

**Source.** [OpenStax Statistics, §13.4](https://openstax.org/books/statistics/pages/13-4-facts-about-the-f-distribution) — CC-BY

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="106.4" titulo="Detecting multicollinearity via VIF">

**Problem.** Regression of vehicle price (<Eq>{`Y`}</Eq>) on horsepower (<Eq>{`X_1`}</Eq>), engine displacement (<Eq>{`X_2`}</Eq>), and weight (<Eq>{`X_3`}</Eq>). Auxiliary regressions: <Eq>{`R_1^2=0{,}89`}</Eq> (<Eq>{`X_1`}</Eq> on <Eq>{`X_2, X_3`}</Eq>), <Eq>{`R_2^2=0{,}91`}</Eq>, <Eq>{`R_3^2=0{,}85`}</Eq>. Calculate VIFs and evaluate.

**Strategy.** <Eq>{`VIF_j = 1/(1-R_j^2)`}</Eq>.

**Resolution.**

<Eq>{`VIF_1 = 1/(1-0{,}89) = 9{,}09`}</Eq>; <Eq>{`VIF_2 = 1/(1-0{,}91) = 11{,}1`}</Eq>; <Eq>{`VIF_3 = 1/(1-0{,}85) = 6{,}67`}</Eq>.

<Eq>{`VIF_2 = 11{,}1 > 10`}</Eq>: engine displacement shows severe multicollinearity with the other variables.

**Action:** remove <Eq>{`X_2`}</Eq> from the model or combine <Eq>{`X_1`}</Eq> and <Eq>{`X_2`}</Eq> into a composite index (principal component analysis).

**Verification.** After removing <Eq>{`X_2`}</Eq>, recalculate VIFs for the remaining predictors.

**Source.** [OpenIntro Statistics, §8.4](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/) — CC-BY-SA

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="106.5" titulo="Dummy variable and interaction">

**Problem.** Predict salary <Eq>{`Y`}</Eq> (R$ thousand/year) using experience <Eq>{`X_1`}</Eq> (years) and gender <Eq>{`D`}</Eq> (1=female, 0=male). Result: <Eq>{`\\hat Y = 45 + 2{,}5X_1 - 8D`}</Eq>.

Interpret the coefficient of <Eq>{`D`}</Eq> and calculate predicted salaries for: (a) male with 10 years of experience; (b) female with 10 years of experience.

**Strategy.** The dummy variable creates two different intercepts (one per gender), keeping the slope equal.

**Resolution.**

<Eq>{`\\hat\\beta_D = -8`}</Eq>: controlling for experience, women earn on average R$ 8,000/year less than men — gross difference (not causal, as we did not control for sector, role, hours).

(a) Male (<Eq>{`D=0`}</Eq>), <Eq>{`X_1=10`}</Eq>: <Eq>{`\\hat Y = 45 + 25 - 0 = 70`}</Eq> thousand R$/year.

(b) Female (<Eq>{`D=1`}</Eq>), <Eq>{`X_1=10`}</Eq>: <Eq>{`\\hat Y = 45 + 25 - 8 = 62`}</Eq> thousand R$/year.

**Verification.** The difference is constant (<Eq>{`-8`}</Eq>) for any level of experience — model without interaction assumes additive effect. To allow different slopes, add <Eq>{`X_1 \\times D`}</Eq>.

**Source.** [OpenIntro Statistics, §8.2, Example 8.4](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/) — CC-BY-SA

</Exemplo>
