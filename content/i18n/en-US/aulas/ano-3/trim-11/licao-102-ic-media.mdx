### Pivotal statistics and the CI for the mean

<Definicao titulo="Confidence interval (definition)">

Let $X_1, \ldots, X_n$ be an iid sample with mean $\mu$. A $(1-\alpha)$ **confidence interval** for $\mu$ is a pair of statistics $(L_n, U_n)$ such that:

<Equation explicacao="Coverage probability: the random interval captures the fixed parameter mu with probability 1 - alpha." latex="P(L_n \leq \mu \leq U_n) = 1 - \alpha" />

The parameter $\mu$ is **fixed**; the randomness lies in $(L_n, U_n)$, which vary from sample to sample.

</Definicao>

> "A 95% confidence interval means that if we construct many confidence intervals from many different samples, we expect 95% of these intervals to contain the true population parameter." — [OpenStax Statistics, §8.1](https://openstax.org/books/statistics/pages/8-1-a-single-population-mean-using-the-normal-distribution)

### Case 1: $\sigma$ known (z-pivot)

<Definicao titulo="CI with known sigma">

By the CLT, $Z = (\bar X - \mu)/(\sigma/\sqrt{n}) \overset{\text{approx.}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0,1)$. Therefore:

<Equation explicacao="Two-sided 1-alpha level CI for mu when sigma is known." latex="P\!\left(-z_{\alpha/2} \leq \frac{\bar X - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}} \leq z_{\alpha/2}\right) = 1 - \alpha" />

Rearranging for $\mu$:

<Equation explicacao="The z-confidence interval for mu. Extremes are sample mean plus and minus the margin of error." latex="\mathrm{CI}_{1-\alpha}(\mu) = \left[\bar X - z_{\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}},\ \bar X + z_{\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\right]" />

</Definicao>

### Case 2: $\sigma$ unknown (t-pivot)

<Definicao titulo="CI with unknown sigma — Student's t">

Replacing $\sigma$ with $s = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n-1}\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2}$, the pivotal statistic is:

<Equation explicacao="Student's t-statistic: follows the t-distribution with n-1 degrees of freedom when the population is normal." latex="T = \frac{\bar X - \mu}{s/\sqrt{n}} \sim t_{n-1}" />

The resulting CI is:

<Equation explicacao="Student's t CI for mu. Uses the t-quantile with n-1 degrees of freedom." latex="\mathrm{CI}_{1-\alpha}(\mu) = \left[\bar X - t_{\alpha/2,\,n-1}\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}},\ \bar X + t_{\alpha/2,\,n-1}\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}\right]" />

The $t_{n-1}$ distribution has heavier tails than $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$; for $n \geq 30$, the differences are small.

</Definicao>

> "When the population is not normal but $n$ is large, the t-distribution still approximates the behavior of the pivot well due to the robustness of the CLT." — [OpenIntro Statistics, §4.2](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/)

### Reference quantiles

| Level $(1-\alpha)$ | $z_{\alpha/2}$ | $t_{\alpha/2,\,29}$ | $t_{\alpha/2,\,9}$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 90% | 1.645 | 1.699 | 1.833 |
| 95% | 1.960 | 2.045 | 2.262 |
| 99% | 2.576 | 2.756 | 3.250 |

### Margin of error and sample size

<Definicao titulo="Margin of error and minimum n">

The **margin of error** is:

<Equation explicacao="Half the width of the CI: defines the precision of the estimate." latex="E = z_{\alpha/2}\,\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}" />

To ensure a maximum margin $E$ with confidence $1-\alpha$:

<Equation explicacao="Minimum sample size for precision E, with known sigma." latex="n \geq \left\lceil\frac{z_{\alpha/2}^2\,\sigma^2}{E^2}\right\rceil" />

</Definicao>

<Insight>

The CI does not say "there is a 95% probability that $\mu$ is in this interval" — $\mu$ is fixed, not random in the frequentist view. The correct statement is: if we repeated the procedure infinitely many times with new samples, 95% of the constructed intervals would capture $\mu$. The distinction is subtle but mathematically important.

</Insight>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

Imagine a target for archery is hidden behind a curtain. You shoot an arrow and it gets stuck in the curtain. You don't know where the target is — but you know you usually hit **close** to it.

So you draw a **big circle** around the arrow hole and say: "I am 95% sure the target is inside this circle."

That circle is the **confidence interval**. The more you practice (the more data you have), the smaller the circle can be — and you become more precise.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

You want to know the **average height** of children in Brazil. It's impossible to measure everyone! So you measure **50 children** and calculate their average: let's say, $\bar X = 1.52$ m.

But this average isn't exactly the real average $\mu$. It might be a little higher or lower. Math tells us the **interval around $\bar X$** where $\mu$ likely is.

**Simple 95% CI recipe:**

1. Calculate the sample mean $\bar X$.
2. Calculate the standard error: $\mathrm{SE} = s / \sqrt{n}$.
3. CI = $[\bar X - 1.96 \cdot \mathrm{SE},\ \bar X + 1.96 \cdot \mathrm{SE}]$.

If you do this with 100 different samples, **about 95 intervals** will contain the real average. Five might not — that's the "price" of uncertainty.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old">

### Why isn't giving just the sample mean enough?

Because $\bar X$ is a point estimate — it varies from sample to sample. The CI quantifies this uncertainty honestly.

### Complete recipe

For a 95% CI with unknown $\sigma$:

1. Calculate $\bar X$ (sample mean) and $s$ (sample deviation).
2. Calculate the standard error: $\mathrm{SE} = s/\sqrt{n}$.
3. Identify the t-quantile: for $n = 20$, use $t_{0.025,\,19} \approx 2.093$.
4. CI: $\bar X \pm 2.093 \cdot s/\sqrt{20}$.

### What does the confidence level mean?

**It does not mean** "there is a 95% chance $\mu$ is in this specific interval." It means: this procedure, if repeated many times, captures $\mu$ in 95% of cases.

### How does the CI respond to your choices?

| Action | Effect on CI |
|---|---|
| Increase $n$ | Narrows (more precise) |
| Increase confidence level | Widens (safer, less informative) |
| Higher variability $s$ | Widens (less consistent data) |

### Numerical example

Salary of $n = 25$ employees: $\bar X = 3,800$, $s = 600$. 95% CI:

$$\bar X \pm t_{0.025,\,24} \cdot \frac{s}{\sqrt{25}} = 3,800 \pm 2.064 \cdot 120 = 3,800 \pm 248$$

CI: $[3,552;\; 4,048]$ currency units.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

### Derivation of the CI by the pivotal method

The method consists of finding a function of the data and the parameter — the **pivotal statistic** — whose distribution is known and does not depend on $\theta$.

**For known $\sigma$**: the pivot is $Z = (\bar X - \mu)\sqrt{n}/\sigma \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$ exactly (if the population is normal) or approximately (by the CLT).

**For unknown $\sigma$**: the pivot is $T = (\bar X - \mu)\sqrt{n}/S \sim t_{n-1}$ exactly when the population is normal (Gossett/Student, 1908). For large $n$, $t_{n-1} \to \mathcal{N}(0,1)$.

### Student's t-distribution

$T \sim t_\nu$ has the density function:

$$f_T(t) = \frac{\Gamma\!\left(\frac{\nu+1}{2}\right)}{\sqrt{\nu\pi}\,\Gamma\!\left(\frac{\nu}{2}\right)}\left(1 + \frac{t^2}{\nu}\right)^{-(\nu+1)/2}$$

For $\nu \to \infty$, it converges to $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$. Heavier tails reflect the additional uncertainty of estimating $\sigma$.

### Bootstrap as an alternative

When population normality is doubtful and $n$ is small, the **percentile bootstrap** generates the CI empirically: resample $B = 2000$ times with replacement, calculate $\bar X^*$ in each replicate, use the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the $B$ means as the limits of the 95% CI.

### Comparison z vs. t

| Criterion | z-CI | t-CI |
|---|---|---|
| $\sigma$ | known | estimated by $s$ |
| Pivotal distribution | $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ | $t_{n-1}$ |
| Width for small $n$ | smaller (but invalid if $\sigma$ uncertain) | larger (conservative, correct) |
| For $n \geq 30$ | small differences | small differences |

### Expected length of the CI

$$\ell = 2\,t_{\alpha/2,\,n-1}\,\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}$$

The expected width decreases as $O(1/\sqrt{n})$: to reduce it by half, quadruple $n$.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

### Frequentist versus Bayesian interpretation

The frequentist CI guarantees coverage over repeated samples, not over the parameter. The Bayesian **credible interval** $[\theta_L, \theta_U]$ satisfies $P(\theta_L \leq \mu \leq \theta_U \mid \mathbf{x}) = 0.95$ — a legitimate statement about $\mu$ given the data, upon specification of a prior.

For a normal-normal conjugate prior: $\mu \mid \mathbf{x} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_n, \tau_n^2)$ where the posterior parameters combine information from the prior and the likelihood. With a non-informative prior, the credible interval and the frequentist CI coincide numerically, but the interpretation is fundamentally different.

### CI for quantiles and other functionals

The CI by the t-pivot is specific to the mean. For the median, use the interval based on order statistics or bootstrap. For proportions, the Wilson interval outperforms the Wald (the classic "plug-in") in terms of actual coverage, especially for $p$ close to 0 or 1.

### Sample size and power: the link with hypothesis testing

The CI and hypothesis testing are dual: rejecting $H_0: \mu = \mu_0$ at level $\alpha$ is equivalent to $\mu_0$ being outside the $(1-\alpha)$ CI. This means the sample size calculated to detect a difference $\delta$ with power $1-\beta$ is:

$$n = \frac{(z_{\alpha/2} + z_\beta)^2\,\sigma^2}{\delta^2}$$

For clinical research with $\alpha = 0.05$ and 80% power ($z_\beta = 0.842$): $n \propto (1.96 + 0.842)^2/\delta^2 = 7.85/\delta^2$.

### Robustness

The t-CI is robust to violations of normality for moderate $n$ (by the CLT), but is sensitive to outliers (via $s$). Robust alternatives: CI based on M-estimators or quantiles with bootstrap. For symmetric heavy-tailed distributions, the CI based on the sample median may have more stable coverage.

### CI in machine learning

In ML, the CI is used to: (1) report uncertainty of evaluation metrics (accuracy, AUC) on test sets; (2) compare models via corrected paired tests (Dietterich 5×2cv); (3) bandit problems (UCB: select arm with highest $\hat\mu + c\,\mathrm{SE}$, balancing exploration and exploitation).

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practical application">

### CI for average salary in HR research

A tech company collected salaries of $n = 36$ mid-level employees. Results: $\bar X = 8,500$, $s = 1,200$.

**95% CI with Student's t** ($t_{0.025,\,35} \approx 2.030$):

$$\bar X \pm t \cdot \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}} = 8,500 \pm 2.030 \cdot \frac{1,200}{\sqrt{36}} = 8,500 \pm 406$$

95% CI: $[8,094;\; 8,906]$.

**Interpretation**: if this procedure were repeated with 100 distinct samples, we would expect 95 of the intervals to contain the true average salary of mid-level employees at the company.

### Benefit processing time research

A government agency monitors the analysis time for benefit requests. Sample of $n = 50$ cases: $\bar X = 42$ days, $s = 12$ days. 95% CI:

$$42 \pm 2.010 \cdot \frac{12}{\sqrt{50}} = 42 \pm 3.41$$

CI: $[38.6;\; 45.4]$ days.

Legal target: 45 days. Since 45 is within the CI, the data are consistent with meeting the target — but also with an average time of 38.6 days. The sample is not decisive on its own; a formal hypothesis test would be needed.

### Approval rate in exams

Sample of 200 essays from candidates at a private school: approval rate (score 500 or higher) of $\hat p = 0.78$. 95% CI for proportion (Wilson):

$$\hat p \pm 1.96\sqrt{\frac{\hat p(1-\hat p)}{n}} = 0.78 \pm 1.96\sqrt{\frac{0.78 \cdot 0.22}{200}} = 0.78 \pm 0.057$$

CI: $[72.3\%;\; 83.7\%]$.

## Solved examples

<Exemplo numero="102.1" titulo="CI with known sigma (basic)">

**Problem.** A laboratory measures glucose concentration in blood samples. The equipment has $\sigma = 5$ mg/dL (known from calibration). A sample of $n = 25$ patients gives $\bar X = 98$ mg/dL. Construct the 95% CI for the true mean concentration $\mu$.

**Strategy.** Since $\sigma$ is known, use the z-pivot. The two-sided 95% quantile is $z_{0.025} = 1.960$.

**Resolution.**

$$E = z_{0.025} \cdot \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} = 1.960 \cdot \frac{5}{\sqrt{25}} = 1.960 \cdot 1 = 1.96$$

$$\mathrm{CI}_{95\%}(\mu) = [98 - 1.96;\; 98 + 1.96] = [96.04;\; 99.96] \text{ mg/dL}$$

**Verification.** The CI width is $2 \times 1.96 = 3.92$ mg/dL. For $n = 100$ (four times larger), $E = 1.96 \cdot 5/10 = 0.98$ — half the width, consistent with $\mathrm{SE} \propto 1/\sqrt{n}$.

**Source.** [OpenStax Statistics, §8.1, Example 8.1](https://openstax.org/books/statistics/pages/8-1-a-single-population-mean-using-the-normal-distribution) — CC-BY.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="102.2" titulo="CI with unknown sigma — Student's t (intermediate)">

**Problem.** A survey of $n = 16$ students on weekly study hours produced $\bar X = 18$ h and $s = 4$ h. Construct the 95% CI for the true mean.

**Strategy.** $\sigma$ unknown, small $n$: use $t_{n-1} = t_{15}$. Quantile: $t_{0.025,\,15} = 2.131$.

**Resolution.**

$$E = t_{0.025,\,15} \cdot \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}} = 2.131 \cdot \frac{4}{\sqrt{16}} = 2.131 \cdot 1 = 2.131$$

$$\mathrm{CI}_{95\%}(\mu) = [18 - 2.13;\; 18 + 2.13] = [15.87;\; 20.13] \text{ hours}$$

**Verification.** If we had erroneously used $z_{0.025} = 1.960$, $E = 1.96$ — underestimating the uncertainty of estimating $\sigma$. The difference $2.131 - 1.960 = 0.171$ is the "cost" of not knowing $\sigma$.

**Source.** [OpenIntro Statistics, §4.2, Example 4.4](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/) — CC-BY-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="102.3" titulo="Sample size for prescribed margin of error (intermediate)">

**Problem.** A tax audit wants to estimate the average value of electronic invoices with a maximum margin of error of 50 at 99% confidence. Previous studies indicate $\sigma \approx 300$. What is the minimum sample size?

**Strategy.** Use the formula $n \geq (z_{\alpha/2}\,\sigma/E)^2$ with $z_{0.005} = 2.576$.

**Resolution.**

$$n \geq \left(\frac{z_{\alpha/2}\,\sigma}{E}\right)^2 = \left(\frac{2.576 \times 300}{50}\right)^2 = (15.456)^2 = 238.9$$

Rounding up: $n = 239$ invoices.

**Verification.** With $n = 239$: $E = 2.576 \times 300/\sqrt{239} = 2.576 \times 19.41 = 50.0$ — exactly at the prescribed limit. Correct.

**Source.** [OpenStax Statistics, §8.1, Example 8.4](https://openstax.org/books/statistics/pages/8-1-a-single-population-mean-using-the-normal-distribution) — CC-BY.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="102.4" titulo="Correct interpretation of CI — conceptual trap (advanced)">

**Problem.** An engineer calculates the 95% CI for the average strength of steel bars: $[425;\; 435]$ MPa. Their colleague claims: "There is a 95% probability that $\mu$ is between 425 and 435 MPa." Is this statement correct?

**Strategy.** Identify what is random and what is fixed in the frequentist interpretation.

**Resolution.**

The statement is **incorrect** in the frequentist view. Once calculated, the interval $[425;\; 435]$ is deterministic — it either contains $\mu$ or it does not. The parameter $\mu$ is fixed; it does not have a probability distribution.

The correct statement is: **the procedure** that generated this interval has a 95% coverage property. If we repeated the experiment many times and calculated a CI each time, we would expect 95% of them to contain $\mu$.

A valid version: "We are **95% confident** that $\mu \in [425;\; 435]$" — provided "confident" is understood as referring to the procedure, not the probability of the specific parameter.

**Verification.** In the Bayesian approach, with a non-informative Jeffreys prior, the 95% credible interval would have the same width and be interpretable as "95% probability that $\mu$ is in this interval given the data" — but this requires specification of a prior.

**Source.** [OpenIntro Statistics, §4.2, Section "Common misconceptions"](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/) — CC-BY-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="102.5" titulo="CI by t-distribution with level comparison (advanced)">

**Problem.** Monthly income of $n = 10$ families in a low-income neighborhood: 1200, 850, 1500, 970, 1100, 800, 1350, 1050, 920, 1080. Construct the 90%, 95%, and 99% CIs for the average income.

**Strategy.** Calculate $\bar X$ and $s$ of the sample; apply the t-formula with 9 degrees of freedom.

**Resolution.**

Sum: $1200+850+1500+970+1100+800+1350+1050+920+1080 = 10,820$. Mean: $\bar X = 1082$.

Deviation: $s^2 = \frac{\sum(X_i - \bar X)^2}{n-1}$. Calculating squared deviations: $(118)^2 + (-232)^2 + (418)^2 + (-112)^2 + (18)^2 + (-282)^2 + (268)^2 + (-32)^2 + (-162)^2 + (-2)^2$:

$= 13924 + 53824 + 174724 + 12544 + 324 + 79524 + 71824 + 1024 + 26244 + 4 = 433960$

$s^2 = 433960/9 \approx 48218$, $s \approx 219.6$.

$\mathrm{SE} = 219.6/\sqrt{10} \approx 69.4$.

| Level | $t_{\alpha/2,\,9}$ | Margin | CI |
|---|---|---|---|
| 90% | 1.833 | 127.2 | $[954.8;\; 1209.2]$ |
| 95% | 2.262 | 157.0 | $[925.0;\; 1239.0]$ |
| 99% | 3.250 | 225.6 | $[856.4;\; 1307.6]$ |

**Verification.** The three intervals are nested: 99% CI $\supset$ 95% CI $\supset$ 90% CI. More confidence implies a wider interval. Consistent with theory.

**Source.** [OpenIntro Statistics, §4.2, Exercise 4.11](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/) — CC-BY-SA.

</Exemplo>
