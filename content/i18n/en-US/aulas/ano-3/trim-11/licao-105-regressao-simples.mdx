<Porta nivel="formal" titulo="Rigorous definition">

### Simple linear regression model

<Definicao titulo="Population model and assumptions (LINE)">

The model postulates that the mean response of $Y$ is linear in $X$:

$$Y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_i + \varepsilon_i, \quad i = 1, \ldots, n$$

**Assumptions (LINE mnemonic):**

- **L**inearity: $E[Y \mid X] = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X$.
- **I**ndependence: the errors $\varepsilon_i$ are mutually independent.
- **N**ormality: $\varepsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$.
- **E**quivariance (homoscedasticity): $\text{Var}(\varepsilon_i) = \sigma^2$ is constant.

</Definicao>

> "The regression equation is written as $\hat{y} = a + bx$, where $b$ is the slope and $a$ is the $y$-intercept." — [OpenStax Statistics, §12.3](https://openstax.org/books/statistics/pages/12-3-the-regression-equation)

<Definicao titulo="Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) estimators">

OLS estimators minimize $SSE = \sum_{i=1}^n (Y_i - \hat Y_i)^2$. The closed-form solution is:

<Equation explicacao="Sample slope: covariance of X and Y divided by the variance of X." latex="\hat\beta_1 = \frac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \bar X)(Y_i - \bar Y)}{\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \bar X)^2}" />

<Equation explicacao="Intercept: forces the line to pass through the data centroid." latex="\hat\beta_0 = \bar Y - \hat\beta_1 \bar X" />

The residual $e_i = Y_i - \hat Y_i$ is the vertical distance of each point from the fitted line.

</Definicao>

### Variance decomposition and R²

<Definicao titulo="SST = SSR + SSE and coefficient of determination">

<Equation explicacao="Decomposition: total variance = explained variance + residual variance." latex="SST = SSR + SSE" />

<Equation explicacao="R² measures the fraction of total variability explained by the model." latex="R^2 = \frac{SSR}{SST} = 1 - \frac{SSE}{SST} \in [0, 1]" />

For simple regression, $R^2 = r^2$, where $r$ is the Pearson correlation coefficient.

</Definicao>

> "The coefficient of determination $r^2$ is the square of the correlation coefficient $r$. It tells you the fraction of total variability in the response that is explained by the least-squares line." — [OpenIntro Statistics, §7.2](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/), p. 331

### Inference on the slope

<Definicao titulo="Sigma estimation and t-test for the slope">

Unbiased estimate of $\sigma^2$:

<Equation explicacao="Residual variance with n-2 degrees of freedom (we lose two: one for beta0, one for beta1)." latex="\hat\sigma^2 = MSE = \frac{SSE}{n-2}" />

Standard error of $\hat\beta_1$:

<Equation explicacao="The smaller the dispersion of X and the larger the n, the smaller the SE of the slope." latex="SE(\hat\beta_1) = \frac{\hat\sigma}{\sqrt{S_{xx}}}" />

Test statistic for $H_0: \beta_1 = 0$:

<Equation explicacao="Under H0, the statistic follows a t-distribution with n-2 degrees of freedom." latex="T = \frac{\hat\beta_1}{SE(\hat\beta_1)} \sim t_{n-2}" />

</Definicao>

<Teorema titulo="Gauss-Markov Theorem (BLUE)">

Under the assumptions of linearity, independence, and homoscedasticity (without requiring normality), OLS estimators are the Best Linear Unbiased Estimators (BLUE):

$$E[\hat\beta_j] = \beta_j \quad \text{and} \quad \text{Var}(\hat\beta_j) \leq \text{Var}(\tilde\beta_j)$$

for any other linear unbiased estimator $\tilde\beta_j$.

</Teorema>

<Insight>

The residual plot ($e_i$ vs. $\hat Y_i$) is the most powerful test of the assumptions: a random pattern around zero confirms linearity and homoscedasticity; a "funnel" indicates heteroscedasticity; a curve indicates non-linearity.

</Insight>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

You have several dots scattered on a sheet of paper. You want to draw **a straight line** that passes "in the middle" of all of them — not too high, not too low.

The rule is: add up the size of the "gaps" between each dot and the line. We want this sum to be the **smallest possible**. This special line is called the **least squares line**.

The closer the dots are to the line, the better it is. If all dots were exactly on the line, the $R^2$ would be 1 — perfect!

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

You measured the height and weight of 15 classmates and wrote it down on a graph. The dots form a pattern: taller people tend to weigh more. You want a **formula** that, given the height, predicts the weight.

**Linear regression** finds the line $\hat Y = \hat\beta_0 + \hat\beta_1 X$ that minimizes the errors. It has two parts:

- $\hat\beta_1$ (slope): how much weight changes for every extra centimeter of height.
- $\hat\beta_0$ (intercept): where the line crosses the vertical axis (predicted weight when height = 0, which might not make sense outside the data range).

The $R^2$ says "how much of the variation in weight is explained by height". If $R^2 = 0.85$, height explains 85% of the variation in weight — very good!

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old">

Simple linear regression models the relationship between two quantitative variables. The goal is to find the line $\hat Y = \hat\beta_0 + \hat\beta_1 X$ that minimizes the **sum of squared residuals** (SSE).

**Why squared?** Because it penalizes large deviations more than small ones, and eliminates the sign (positive and negative residuals do not cancel each other out).

The estimators are:

$$\hat\beta_1 = \frac{\sum (X_i - \bar X)(Y_i - \bar Y)}{\sum (X_i - \bar X)^2}, \qquad \hat\beta_0 = \bar Y - \hat\beta_1 \bar X$$

The $R^2$ between 0 and 1 measures the fit: 0 = horizontal line (variable $X$ does not help predict $Y$); 1 = perfect fit.

**Cautions:** extrapolation beyond the data is risky; correlation does not imply causation; an outlier can distort the entire line.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

**Matrix formulation (1 predictor)**

For $n$ observations: $\mathbf{Y} = X\boldsymbol\beta + \boldsymbol\varepsilon$, where $X \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times 2}$ has a first column of 1s. The OLS estimator is $\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} = (X^TX)^{-1}X^T\mathbf{Y}$.

**Residual properties**

$\sum e_i = 0$, $\sum X_i e_i = 0$, $\sum \hat Y_i e_i = 0$. Residuals are orthogonal to the predictor and the fitted response.

**Regression ANOVA table**

| Source | SS | df | MS | F |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Regression | $SSR$ | 1 | $SSR/1$ | $MSR/MSE$ |
| Error | $SSE$ | $n-2$ | $SSE/(n-2)$ | — |
| Total | $SST$ | $n-1$ | — | — |

$F = T^2$ for simple regression; both test $H_0: \beta_1 = 0$.

**CI for $\beta_1$:**

$$\hat\beta_1 \pm t_{n-2;\alpha/2} \cdot SE(\hat\beta_1)$$

**Mandatory diagnostics**: residual vs. fitted values plot; Q-Q plot of residuals; residual vs. each predictor plot.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

Least squares regression was systematized by Legendre (1805) and Gauss (1809), who competed for priority — Gauss claimed to have had the method since 1795. Francis Galton used the technique in the 1880s to study biological inheritance, coining the term *regression to the mean* upon observing that children of very tall parents tended to be less tall than their parents.

The Gauss-Markov Theorem establishes that OLS produces the linear unbiased estimators with the smallest variance under homoscedasticity — without requiring normality. Normality of errors is only necessary for exact inference with the $t$-distribution.

In econometrics, the most common violation is **endogeneity** ($\text{Cov}(X_i, \varepsilon_i) \neq 0$), which makes OLS biased and inconsistent. The solution is instrumental variables (IV/2SLS). In time series, autocorrelation of errors requires Newey-West correction for standard errors.

In the context of machine learning, OLS is a special case of a linear neural network with MSE loss function and no regularization. $L_2$ regularization (ridge) shrinks $\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}$ toward zero; $L_1$ (lasso) produces sparsity — both generalize better when $p$ is large relative to $n$.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practical application">

**ENEM 2023: scores by family income bracket**

A survey of 200 ENEM candidates recorded monthly family income $X$ (in thousand BRL) and total score $Y$. The regression analysis produced:

- $\bar X = 3.2$; $\bar Y = 612$; $S_{xx} = 187.4$; $S_{xy} = 2,248.8$.
- $\hat\beta_1 = 2248.8 / 187.4 \approx 12.0$ points per thousand BRL of income.
- $\hat\beta_0 = 612 - 12.0 \times 3.2 = 573.6$ points.
- $R^2 = 0.61$: income explains 61% of the variability in scores.

**Careful interpretation**: the line predicts that a candidate from a family with an income of 5,000 BRL/month will have, on average, a score of $573.6 + 12.0 \times 5 = 633.6$. But this does not imply causation — income is correlated with access to better schools, prep courses, nutrition, etc.

**Salary prediction with schooling (IBGE PNAD 2023)**

Regression with $X$ = years of schooling and $Y$ = monthly salary (BRL): $\hat Y = 820 + 215X$. Each additional year of schooling is associated with 215 BRL more in the average salary. With $R^2 = 0.54$, schooling explains 54% of salary variation — also consider sector, region, and gender.

</Porta>

## Solved examples

<Exemplo numero="105.1" titulo="Calculate the regression line with small data">

**Problem.** Five municipalities in the interior of São Paulo recorded GDP per capita $X$ (in thousand BRL/year) and HDI $Y$:

| $X$ | 18 | 24 | 30 | 36 | 42 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $Y$ | 0.62 | 0.68 | 0.72 | 0.78 | 0.84 |

Find the least squares line and interpret the coefficients.

**Strategy.** Calculate $\bar X$, $\bar Y$, $S_{xx}$, $S_{xy}$, then apply the estimator formulas.

**Resolution.**

$\bar X = (18+24+30+36+42)/5 = 30$; $\bar Y = (0.62+0.68+0.72+0.78+0.84)/5 = 0.728$.

$S_{xx} = (18-30)^2+(24-30)^2+(30-30)^2+(36-30)^2+(42-30)^2 = 144+36+0+36+144 = 360$.

$S_{xy} = (18-30)(0.62-0.728)+\ldots = (-12)(-0.108)+(-6)(-0.048)+0+(6)(0.052)+(12)(0.112)$
$= 1.296+0.288+0+0.312+1.344 = 3.24$.

$\hat\beta_1 = 3.24/360 = 0.009$ (HDI point per thousand BRL of GDP per capita).

$\hat\beta_0 = 0.728 - 0.009 \times 30 = 0.458$.

Line: $\hat Y = 0.458 + 0.009 X$.

**Verification.** For $X=30$: $\hat Y = 0.458+0.009\times30 = 0.728 = \bar Y$. Correct — the line passes through the centroid.

**Source.** [OpenStax Statistics, §12.3, Example 12.5](https://openstax.org/books/statistics/pages/12-3-the-regression-equation) — CC-BY

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="105.2" titulo="Calculate and interpret R²">

**Problem.** With the data from the previous example, calculate $SST$, $SSE$, $SSR$ and $R^2$.

**Strategy.** Calculate $\hat Y_i$ for each point, then the three sums of squares.

**Resolution.**

| $X$ | $Y$ | $\hat Y$ | $e = Y-\hat Y$ | $e^2$ | $(Y-\bar Y)^2$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 18 | 0.62 | 0.620 | 0 | 0 | 0.01166 |
| 24 | 0.68 | 0.674 | 0.006 | 0.000036 | 0.00230 |
| 30 | 0.72 | 0.728 | -0.008 | 0.000064 | 0.000064 |
| 36 | 0.78 | 0.782 | -0.002 | 0.000004 | 0.00274 |
| 42 | 0.84 | 0.836 | 0.004 | 0.000016 | 0.01254 |

$SSE = 0.000120$; $SST = 0.02930$; $SSR = 0.02918$.

$R^2 = 0.02918/0.02930 \approx 0.996$.

**Verification.** $R^2$ very close to 1 — makes sense: the points are almost perfectly aligned.

**Source.** [OpenIntro Statistics, §7.2, Exercise 7.9](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/) — CC-BY-SA

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="105.3" titulo="t-test for the slope">

**Problem.** With $n=25$ pairs of observations, we obtained $\hat\beta_1 = 3.42$ and $SE(\hat\beta_1) = 1.14$. Test $H_0: \beta_1 = 0$ at the 5% level (two-tailed).

**Strategy.** Calculate the $T$ statistic and compare with the critical value $t_{23;\,0.025}$.

**Resolution.**

$T = \hat\beta_1 / SE(\hat\beta_1) = 3.42/1.14 = 3.00$.

Degrees of freedom: $n-2 = 23$. Critical value $t_{23;\,0.025} \approx 2.069$.

Since $|T| = 3.00 > 2.069$, we reject $H_0$ at the 5% level.

**Verification.** p-value: $P(|t_{23}| > 3.00) \approx 0.006 < 0.05$. Consistent with rejection.

**Source.** [OpenStax Statistics, §12.4, Example 12.8](https://openstax.org/books/statistics/pages/12-4-testing-the-significance-of-the-correlation-coefficient) — CC-BY

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="105.4" titulo="Point prediction and confidence interval">

**Problem.** The fitted line is $\hat Y = 42.6 + 1.8 X$ with $n=20$, $MSE = 9.61$, $\bar X = 15$, $S_{xx} = 280$. Obtain: (a) point prediction for $X^* = 20$; (b) 95% CI for the mean value of $Y$ when $X = 20$.

**Strategy.** Substitute $X^*$ into the fitted line. Use the CI formula for the conditional mean.

**Resolution.**

(a) $\hat Y^* = 42.6 + 1.8 \times 20 = 42.6 + 36 = 78.6$.

(b) $SE(\hat Y^*) = \hat\sigma\sqrt{\frac{1}{n} + \frac{(X^*-\bar X)^2}{S_{xx}}} = \sqrt{9.61}\sqrt{\frac{1}{20}+\frac{25}{280}} = 3.10 \times \sqrt{0.0500+0.0893} = 3.10 \times 0.373 = 1.156$.

$t_{18;\,0.025} \approx 2.101$.

95% CI: $78.6 \pm 2.101 \times 1.156 = 78.6 \pm 2.43 = (76.2; 81.0)$.

**Verification.** The further $X^*$ is from $\bar X$, the larger the SE — CI widens at the extremes.

**Source.** [OpenIntro Statistics, §7.3](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/) — CC-BY-SA

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="105.5" titulo="Residual diagnosis and assumption violation">

**Problem.** A regression of energy consumption $(Y)$ vs. temperature $(X)$ produced a residual vs. $\hat Y$ plot with a "U" shape (negative residuals in the center, positive at the ends). Which assumption was violated and what to do?

**Strategy.** Identify the pattern in the residual plot and relate it to the linearity assumption.

**Resolution.**

"U" pattern (systematic curvature) in residuals vs. $\hat Y$ indicates violation of the **linearity** assumption: the real relationship between $X$ and $Y$ is not linear.

**Corrective action:** include $X^2$ in the model (polynomial regression) or apply a transformation to $X$ (e.g., $\log X$, $\sqrt{X}$).

Other common patterns:
- Funnel (variance increasing with $\hat Y$) → homoscedasticity violated → transform $Y$ (e.g., $\log Y$) or use robust errors.
- Diagonal bands → discrete or grouped data → mixed effect.

**Verification.** After including $X^2$, the new residual plot should be random around zero.

**Source.** [OpenIntro Statistics, §7.4, Figure 7.17](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/) — CC-BY-SA

</Exemplo>
