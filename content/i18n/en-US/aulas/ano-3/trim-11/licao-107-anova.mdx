### The problem: comparing k means with a single test

Suppose you have $k \geq 2$ independent groups and want to know if the population means $\mu_1, \ldots, \mu_k$ are equal. Performing $\binom{k}{2}$ separate $t$-tests inflates the Type I error rate. ANOVA solves this with a single global test.

<Definicao titulo="One-way ANOVA model (fixed effects)">

Each observation $Y_{ij}$ (subject $j$ in group $i$) follows:

$$Y_{ij} = \mu + \alpha_i + \varepsilon_{ij}, \quad i = 1, \ldots, k;\; j = 1, \ldots, n_i$$

where:
- $\mu$ is the grand mean
- $\alpha_i$ is the effect of group $i$, with constraint $\sum_{i=1}^k n_i \alpha_i = 0$
- $\varepsilon_{ij} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$ i.i.d. — normal errors with common variance

The hypotheses are:

$$H_0: \alpha_1 = \alpha_2 = \cdots = \alpha_k = 0 \quad \Leftrightarrow \quad \mu_1 = \mu_2 = \cdots = \mu_k$$
$$H_1: \text{there exists at least one } i \text{ such that } \alpha_i \neq 0$$

</Definicao>

> "In a one-way analysis of variance problem, we are interested in comparing the means of $k$ populations. If the means are all equal, we say the treatments, or factor levels, are not different from one another. If at least one mean differs, we say the treatments are different." — [OpenStax Statistics §13.1](https://openstax.org/books/statistics/pages/13-1-one-way-anova)

### Total variance decomposition

<Definicao titulo="Sum of squares: SST = SSB + SSW">

Let $\bar{Y} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i,j} Y_{ij}$ be the grand mean and $\bar{Y}_i = \frac{1}{n_i}\sum_j Y_{ij}$ be the mean of group $i$. We define:

$$SST = \sum_{i=1}^k \sum_{j=1}^{n_i} (Y_{ij} - \bar{Y})^2 \quad \text{(total sum of squares)}$$

$$SSB = \sum_{i=1}^k n_i (\bar{Y}_i - \bar{Y})^2 \quad \text{(between groups)}$$

$$SSW = \sum_{i=1}^k \sum_{j=1}^{n_i} (Y_{ij} - \bar{Y}_i)^2 \quad \text{(within groups)}$$

**Theorem (decomposition):** $SST = SSB + SSW$. The proof follows from the algebraic identity

$$Y_{ij} - \bar{Y} = \underbrace{(\bar{Y}_i - \bar{Y})}_{\text{group effect}} + \underbrace{(Y_{ij} - \bar{Y}_i)}_{\text{within-group error}}$$

by squaring and summing — the cross terms cancel because $\sum_j (Y_{ij} - \bar{Y}_i) = 0$.

</Definicao>

### F-statistic and ANOVA table

<Definicao titulo="Mean squares and F-statistic">

Dividing each SS by its respective degrees of freedom gives the <strong>mean squares</strong>:

$$MS_B = \frac{SSB}{k-1}, \qquad MS_W = \frac{SSW}{N-k}$$

where $N = \sum_{i=1}^k n_i$ and $k - 1$ and $N - k$ are the between and within degrees of freedom.

The test statistic is:

$$F = \frac{MS_B}{MS_W}$$

Under $H_0$, $F \sim F_{k-1,\, N-k}$ (Snedecor's F-distribution). We reject $H_0$ if $F > F_{\alpha;\, k-1,\, N-k}$.

| Source | SS | df | MS | F |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Between groups | $SSB$ | $k-1$ | $MS_B = SSB/(k-1)$ | $MS_B / MS_W$ |
| Within groups | $SSW$ | $N-k$ | $MS_W = SSW/(N-k)$ | — |
| Total | $SST$ | $N-1$ | — | — |

</Definicao>

> "The one-way ANOVA test statistic $F$ is the ratio of two independent chi-square variables divided by their respective degrees of freedom... Under the null hypothesis, $F$ follows an $F$ distribution with $k-1$ and $N-k$ degrees of freedom." — [OpenStax Statistics §13.2](https://openstax.org/books/statistics/pages/13-2-the-f-distribution-and-the-f-ratio)

### Model assumptions

<Definicao titulo="Three assumptions of one-way ANOVA">

1. **Independence:** observations are independent between groups and within each group.
2. **Normality:** within each group, $Y_{ij} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_i, \sigma^2)$. For $n_i \geq 30$, the Central Limit Theorem makes the test robust.
3. **Homoscedasticity (equal variances):** $\sigma_1^2 = \sigma_2^2 = \cdots = \sigma_k^2 = \sigma^2$. Verified with Levene's or Bartlett's test.

If assumption 3 fails severely, use <strong>Welch's ANOVA</strong> (adjusts degrees of freedom). If normality fails for small $n$, use <strong>Kruskal-Wallis</strong> (non-parametric alternative).

</Definicao>

### Effect size

<Definicao titulo="Eta squared">

$$\eta^2 = \frac{SSB}{SST}$$

Cohen's conventions: $\eta^2 \approx 0.01$ (small), $0.06$ (medium), $0.14$ (large). The less biased version is $\omega^2 = (SSB - (k-1) MS_W)/(SST + MS_W)$.

</Definicao>

<Teorema titulo="Why F and not multiple t-tests?">

With $k$ groups, $\binom{k}{2}$ pairwise comparisons would be needed. For $k = 5$, that's 10 $t$-tests. If each test has level $\alpha = 0.05$, the probability of at least one Type I error is:

$$P(\text{at least 1 false positive}) = 1 - (1 - 0.05)^{10} \approx 0.40$$

ANOVA keeps the $\alpha$ level controlled globally with a single test. This is why the F-test precedes any post-hoc analysis.

</Teorema>

<Insight>

$E[MS_W] = \sigma^2$ <strong>always</strong> (under $H_0$ and $H_1$) — $MS_W$ is an unbiased estimator of error variance. However, $E[MS_B] = \sigma^2 + \frac{\sum n_i \alpha_i^2}{k-1}$, which is $\sigma^2$ only when $H_0$ is true. Therefore, $F > 1$ on average when $H_1$ holds, and $F \approx 1$ when $H_0$ holds.

</Insight>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

Imagine you have <strong>three school classes</strong>. You want to know if one class is much faster at solving math problems than the others.

You measure the time for each child. Then you look at two things:

- Do the three classes have <strong>very different average times</strong>? (distance between groups)
- Inside each class, are the children's times similar or very spread out? (mess inside)

If the class averages are very different AND each class is consistent inside, you can say with confidence: "Class A is indeed faster, it wasn't just luck."

ANOVA is exactly this comparison: "difference between classes" versus "mess inside the classes." If the first is much larger than the second, the classes are truly different.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

You want to know if <strong>three fertilizers</strong> make plants grow differently.

With each fertilizer, you plant 10 flowers. After a month, you measure the height of each.

<strong>Why not compare two by two?</strong> With 3 groups, there would be 3 comparisons. With 5 groups, there would be 10. Each comparison has a chance of giving a wrong result by luck. If you do many comparisons, you will "find" a difference where none exists.

<strong>The solution:</strong> ask <strong>one single question</strong>: "does any fertilizer make a difference?" This is ANOVA.

The idea:

- Calculate the average of each group.
- See how much the averages differ from each other — this is the <strong>variance between groups</strong> (SSB).
- See how much the plants in each group differ from each other — this is the <strong>variance within groups</strong> (SSW).
- The $F$ statistic is the ratio: $F = SSB/SSW$ (simplified).
- If $F$ is large, the fertilizers make a difference. If it is small, they probably don't.

</Porta>

### The decomposition that makes it work

For each observation $Y_{ij}$ (value $j$ of group $i$), write:

$$Y_{ij} - \bar{Y} = (\bar{Y}_i - \bar{Y}) + (Y_{ij} - \bar{Y}_i)$$

The first term is the "group effect" — how far the group mean is from the grand mean. The second is the "noise" within the group.

Squaring and summing over all $i, j$, the cross products cancel (because $\sum_j(Y_{ij} - \bar{Y}_i) = 0$ for each $i$), leaving:

$$SST = SSB + SSW$$

This is the <strong>orthogonal decomposition</strong> of total variance.

### When to reject $H_0$?

Under $H_0$, $MS_B$ and $MS_W$ estimate the same thing ($\sigma^2$), so $F \approx 1$. If any group mean is different, $MS_B$ becomes inflated and $F$ grows. Reject $H_0$ when $F > F_{\alpha;\, k-1,\, N-k}$ (critical value from the F-table).

### After rejecting: post-hoc

ANOVA says "at least one group differs." To know which one, use:

- <strong>Tukey HSD</strong>: pairwise comparisons with FWER control. Most powerful for planned comparisons.
- <strong>Bonferroni</strong>: divide $\alpha$ by the number of comparisons. Simple, conservative.
- <strong>Scheffé</strong>: for any linear contrast, not just pairs.

### Assumptions — how to check

1. Independence: by experimental design.
2. Normality: histogram or Q-Q plot of each group; Shapiro-Wilk for small $n$.
3. Homoscedasticity: boxplots of groups; Levene's test ($p > 0.05$ = variances reasonably equal).

<Insight>

<strong>Rule of thumb.</strong> If the ratio between the largest and smallest group variance is less than 4:1, ANOVA is robust to homoscedasticity. For larger ratios, use Welch.

</Insight>

</Porta>

### ANOVA as a linear model

One-way ANOVA is equivalent to linear regression with $k-1$ dummy variables (group indicators). The global F-test of the regression coincides with the ANOVA $F$. This unifies the entire analysis within the GLM framework.

Matrix-wise, the model is $\mathbf{Y} = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$, where $\mathbf{X}$ is the design matrix with group indicators. The OLS estimator results in group means as predictions.

### Distributions under H₀

Under $H_0$:
- $SSB/\sigma^2 \sim \chi^2_{k-1}$
- $SSW/\sigma^2 \sim \chi^2_{N-k}$
- Both sums are <strong>independent</strong> (due to projection orthogonality)

Thus $F = (SSB/\sigma^2)/(k-1) \div (SSW/\sigma^2)/(N-k)$ follows $F_{k-1,\, N-k}$ exactly under normality. For $k=2$, $F = T^2$ where $T$ is the two-sample $t$-statistic with pooled variance.

### Power and sample size calculation

The non-centrality parameter of the $F$ distribution under $H_1$ is $\lambda = \sum_i n_i \alpha_i^2 / \sigma^2$. Power increases with effect size $f = \sqrt{\eta^2/(1-\eta^2)}$ (Cohen's $f$) and with $n$ per group.

To detect medium effect ($f = 0.25$), $k = 4$ groups, 80% power, $\alpha = 0.05$: approximately 45 subjects per group are needed (180 total). Use `statsmodels.stats.power.FTestAnovaPower` or `pwr::pwr.anova.test()` in R.

### Computational assumption checking

```python
from scipy import stats
import numpy as np

# Levene's test (homoscedasticity)
stat, p = stats.levene(group1, group2, group3)

# ANOVA (scipy)
F, p_anova = stats.f_oneway(group1, group2, group3)

# Tukey post-hoc
import statsmodels.stats.multicomp as mc
tukey = mc.pairwise_tukeyhsd(np.concatenate([group1, group2, group3]),
                              np.repeat(['A','B','C'], [len(group1), len(group2), len(group3)]),
                              alpha=0.05)
```

### Welch's ANOVA (unequal variances)

When Levene's test rejects homoscedasticity, use:

$$F^* = \frac{\sum_i w_i (\bar{Y}_i - \bar{Y}^*)^2 / (k-1)}{1 + \frac{2(k-2)}{k^2-1} \sum_i \frac{(1 - w_i/W)^2}{n_i - 1}}$$

where $w_i = n_i/s_i^2$, $W = \sum w_i$, and $\bar{Y}^* = \sum w_i \bar{Y}_i / W$. The denominator degrees of freedom are adjusted by Welch's formula.

</Porta>

### Historical context: Fisher and agriculture

Ronald A. Fisher developed ANOVA in the 1920s while working at the Rothamsted Experimental Station (England). The original problem was comparing wheat yields of different varieties in field plots — exactly the type of one-way experiment with replications. The book <em>Statistical Methods for Research Workers</em> (1925) introduced the technique; <em>The Design of Experiments</em> (1935) formalized the theory of balanced experiments.

The notation $F$ for the statistic honors Fisher, proposed by George Snedecor.

### ANOVA as a special case of the General Linear Model (GLM)

Every ANOVA — one-way, two-way, with interactions, repeated measures — is a special case of the equation $\mathbf{Y} = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$ with hypotheses of the form $\mathbf{L}\boldsymbol{\beta} = \mathbf{0}$ (linear contrasts). The F-test is the likelihood ratio test for the restriction $\mathbf{L}\boldsymbol{\beta} = \mathbf{0}$.

Direct extensions:
- <strong>ANCOVA</strong>: continuous covariates added to the model to reduce $SSW$.
- <strong>MANOVA</strong>: multiple dependent variables — Wilks' $\Lambda$, Pillai's trace statistics.
- <strong>Mixed effects ANOVA</strong>: random effects for subjects or sample groups (`lme4` package in R, `statsmodels.MixedLM` in Python).

### Bayesian critique and effect size

A significant $p$-value does not imply practical relevance. With $N = 10,000$, ANOVA detects $\eta^2 = 0.001$ (negligible effect). Modern practice requires reporting $F$, df, $p$, $\eta^2$ (or $\omega^2$, less biased), and confidence intervals for post-hoc differences.

<strong>Bayesian ANOVA</strong> (Rouder et al., 2012) computes the Bayes factor $BF_{10}$ for the model with effect vs. null model. `BayesFactor` package in R. For psychology and behavioral sciences, $BF > 10$ is considered strong evidence for $H_1$.

### DOE and fractional experiments

For $k$ factors with $l$ levels each, a full factorial experiment requires $l^k$ units — exponential. In quality engineering (and pharmaceutical industry), one uses:
- <strong>Fractional factorial</strong> $2^{k-p}$: half, quarter, eighth of combinations, losing estimability of high-order interactions.
- <strong>Latin squares</strong>: two-dimensional blocking, controls two sources of variation with $l^2$ units.
- <strong>Response surface methodology</strong>: curvature effects (Box-Wilson, Box-Behnken).

Fisher invented all these designs as well. One-way ANOVA is just the gateway.

</Porta>

### Context: comparing corporate health insurance plans

A medium-sized company (1,200 employees) wants to evaluate if average monthly costs per beneficiary differ among three health insurance providers it is considering. It obtained cost data from a pilot sample for each provider.

| Provider | $n$ | Mean cost | Std. dev |
|---|---|---|---|
| Provider A | 30 | R$ 420.00 | R$ 45.00 |
| Provider B | 30 | R$ 460.00 | R$ 50.00 |
| Provider C | 30 | R$ 395.00 | R$ 42.00 |

Step-by-step decision:

1. <strong>Formulate hypotheses.</strong> $H_0: \mu_A = \mu_B = \mu_C$ (average costs equal). $H_1:$ at least one differs.
2. <strong>Check assumptions.</strong> Independence: each employee appears in only one provider (independent samples). Normality: $n = 30$ per group — CLT ensures robustness. Homoscedasticity: ratio of max/min std dev = 50/42 $\approx$ 1.2 — acceptable (less than 2:1).
3. <strong>Calculate.</strong> Using spreadsheet or R/Python, we get (hypothetical values consistent with data): $SSB \approx 50,220$, $SSW \approx 175,400$, $F(2, 87) \approx 12.47$. Critical value $F_{0.05;\, 2,\, 87} \approx 3.10$.
4. <strong>Decide.</strong> $F = 12.47 > 3.10$ — reject $H_0$.
5. <strong>Tukey post-hoc.</strong> Provider B differs from A and C; A and C do not differ from each other.
6. <strong>Effect size.</strong> $\eta^2 = 50,220 / (50,220 + 175,400) \approx 0.22$ — large effect.
7. <strong>Management decision.</strong> Providers A and C have similar and lower costs. HR recommends hiring A or C, also considering coverage and network.

### Most common errors

- <strong>Multiple t-tests without correction</strong>: inflates Type I error.
- <strong>Skipping post-hoc</strong>: rejected ANOVA does not identify which groups differ.
- <strong>Not checking homoscedasticity</strong>: if variances are very unequal, use Welch.
- <strong>Reporting only p-value</strong>: always include $F$, df, $\eta^2$, and post-hoc.

### Reporting format (APA)

"Monthly costs differed significantly among the three providers, $F(2, 87) = 12.47$, $p < 0.001$, $\eta^2 = 0.22$. Tukey post-hoc comparisons indicated that Provider B had significantly higher costs than the others ($p < 0.01$), while A and C did not differ from each other ($p = 0.74$)."

<Exemplo titulo="Degrees of freedom and ANOVA table (application)" numero="1">

<strong>Problem:</strong> A researcher compares 4 diets. Each group has 15 participants. Fill in the degrees of freedom in the ANOVA table.

<strong>Strategy:</strong> Degrees of freedom follow directly from the formulas: $df_B = k - 1$, $df_W = N - k$, $df_T = N - 1$.

<strong>Solution:</strong>

$k = 4$ groups, $n_i = 15$ each, $N = 4 \times 15 = 60$.

| Source | SS | df | MS | F |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Between | $SSB$ | $4 - 1 = 3$ | $SSB/3$ | $MS_B/MS_W$ |
| Within | $SSW$ | $60 - 4 = 56$ | $SSW/56$ | — |
| Total | $SST$ | $60 - 1 = 59$ | — | — |

Check: $3 + 56 = 59$ ✓

<strong>Verification:</strong> $df_T = df_B + df_W$. Always check $3 + 56 = 59$. If it doesn't close, there is a calculation error.

<strong>Source.</strong> [OpenStax — Statistics §13.2](https://openstax.org/books/statistics/pages/13-2-the-f-distribution-and-the-f-ratio) — CC-BY 4.0.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Calculating F and deciding (application)" numero="2">

<strong>Problem:</strong> Three groups with $n_1 = n_2 = n_3 = 10$. $SSB = 60$ and $SSW = 135$. Calculate $F$ and decide at $\alpha = 0.05$ (critical $F_{2,\, 27} \approx 3.35$).

<strong>Strategy:</strong> Calculate $MS_B = SSB/df_B$ and $MS_W = SSW/df_W$, then $F = MS_B/MS_W$.

<strong>Solution:</strong>

1. $k = 3$, $N = 30$. $df_B = 2$, $df_W = 27$.
2. $MS_B = 60/2 = 30$.
3. $MS_W = 135/27 = 5$.
4. $F = 30/5 = 6.0$.
5. $6.0 > 3.35$ — <strong>reject $H_0$</strong>. Evidence that at least one mean differs.

<strong>Verification:</strong> $SST = 60 + 135 = 195$. $\eta^2 = 60/195 \approx 0.31$ — large effect. Makes sense with high $F$.

<strong>Source.</strong> [OpenStax — Statistics §13.3](https://openstax.org/books/statistics/pages/13-3-facts-about-the-f-distribution) — CC-BY 4.0.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Calculating SSB from group means (intermediate)" numero="3">

<strong>Problem:</strong> Four groups with $n = 20$ each. Means: $\bar{Y}_1 = 8$, $\bar{Y}_2 = 10$, $\bar{Y}_3 = 12$, $\bar{Y}_4 = 10$. Calculate $SSB$.

<strong>Strategy:</strong> Calculate the grand mean $\bar{Y}$ and use $SSB = \sum_i n_i(\bar{Y}_i - \bar{Y})^2$.

<strong>Solution:</strong>

1. Grand mean: $\bar{Y} = (8 + 10 + 12 + 10)/4 = 40/4 = 10$ (balanced groups: mean of means).
2. Weighted squared deviations:
   - Group 1: $20 \cdot (8 - 10)^2 = 20 \cdot 4 = 80$
   - Group 2: $20 \cdot (10 - 10)^2 = 0$
   - Group 3: $20 \cdot (12 - 10)^2 = 20 \cdot 4 = 80$
   - Group 4: $20 \cdot (10 - 10)^2 = 0$
3. $SSB = 80 + 0 + 80 + 0 = 160$.

<strong>Verification:</strong> Groups 2 and 4 have means equal to the grand mean, contributing zero to SSB. Groups 1 and 3, symmetric, contribute equally. Makes structural sense.

<strong>Source.</strong> [OpenIntro Statistics §7.5](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/) — CC-BY-SA 3.0.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Checking assumptions and deciding on alternative test (intermediate)" numero="4">

<strong>Problem:</strong> A researcher has 3 groups with $n = 8$ each. Standard deviations are $s_1 = 3$, $s_2 = 3.2$, $s_3 = 9.5$. Shapiro-Wilk in one group gives $p = 0.012$. What test to use?

<strong>Strategy:</strong> Check the two critical assumptions: normality and homoscedasticity. If both or one fails seriously with small $n$, choose alternative.

<strong>Solution:</strong>

1. <strong>Normality:</strong> Shapiro-Wilk $p = 0.012 < 0.05$ — evidence of non-normality. With $n = 8$, CLT doesn't help.
2. <strong>Homoscedasticity:</strong> ratio of variances $s_3^2/s_1^2 = 9.5^2/3^2 = 90.25/9 \approx 10$ — far above the tolerable 4:1 limit.
3. <strong>Decision:</strong> Two assumptions violated with small $n$. Parametric ANOVA is not appropriate.
4. <strong>Alternative:</strong> <strong>Kruskal-Wallis</strong> — non-parametric test analogous to one-way ANOVA, based on ranks. Does not assume normality or homoscedasticity.

<strong>Verification:</strong> If only homoscedasticity failed (normality ok), Welch's ANOVA would suffice. With additional non-normality and small $n$, non-parametric is the correct choice.

<strong>Source.</strong> [OpenIntro Statistics §7.5](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/) — CC-BY-SA 3.0.
</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Complete analysis: three battery brands (real modeling)" numero="5">

<strong>Problem:</strong> A quality engineer tests the duration (in hours) of three AA battery brands. Each brand is tested on 12 identical devices. Means: brand P = 18.5 h, brand Q = 22.0 h, brand R = 19.8 h. $SSB = 183.6$ and $SSW = 396.0$. Conduct the complete ANOVA at $\alpha = 0.05$ and interpret.

<strong>Strategy:</strong> Calculate df, MS, F; compare with critical; if reject, identify which brand differs using means.

<strong>Solution:</strong>

1. $k = 3$, $n_i = 12$, $N = 36$. $df_B = 2$, $df_W = 33$.
2. $MS_B = 183.6 / 2 = 91.8$.
3. $MS_W = 396.0 / 33 = 12.0$.
4. $F = 91.8 / 12.0 = 7.65$.
5. $F_{0.05;\, 2,\, 33} \approx 3.28$ (table interpolation). $7.65 > 3.28$ — <strong>reject $H_0$</strong>.
6. $\eta^2 = 183.6 / (183.6 + 396.0) = 183.6 / 579.6 \approx 0.317$ — large effect.
7. <strong>Post-hoc (inspection of means):</strong> Brand Q has mean 22.0 h, clearly above the others. A formal Tukey HSD analysis would confirm which differences are significant.

<strong>Verification:</strong> Formal report: $F(2, 33) = 7.65$, $p < 0.01$, $\eta^2 = 0.32$. Brand Q has significantly longer duration than P; the difference between Q and R requires formal post-hoc.

<strong>Source.</strong> [Navarro — Learning Statistics with R ch. 14](https://learningstatisticswithr.com/) — CC-BY-SA 4.0.
</Exemplo>
