<Porta nivel="formal" titulo="Rigorous definition">

### Bayes' theorem

<Definicao titulo="Bayes' rule (general form)">

Let $\theta$ be a parameter (or hypothesis) and $D$ the observed data. Definitions:

- $P(\theta)$ — **prior**: probability distribution of the parameter *before* observing data.
- $P(D \mid \theta)$ — **likelihood**: probability of the data given the parameter.
- $P(D)$ — **marginal evidence**: $P(D) = \int P(D \mid \theta) P(\theta)\, d\theta$ (or discrete sum).
- $P(\theta \mid D)$ — **posterior**: updated distribution after observing $D$.

Bayes' rule:

<Equation explicacao="The posterior is proportional to likelihood times prior. The denominator P(D) is just a normalizing constant." latex="P(\theta \mid D) = \frac{P(D \mid \theta)\, P(\theta)}{P(D)} \propto P(D \mid \theta)\, P(\theta)" />

</Definicao>

> "Bayes' theorem is a basic result of conditional probability, but its interpretation changes everything: it offers a formal recipe for updating beliefs in light of evidence." — [OpenIntro Statistics §3.6](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/)

### Conjugate priors: the Beta-Bernoulli case

<Definicao titulo="Beta-Bernoulli conjugate family">

When $X_1, \ldots, X_n \stackrel{\text{iid}}{\sim} \text{Bernoulli}(\theta)$ and the prior is $\theta \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)$, the posterior has closed form:

<Equation explicacao="After s successes in n trials, the posterior is another Beta with updated parameters." latex="\theta \mid D \;\sim\; \text{Beta}(\alpha + s,\; \beta + n - s)" />

where $s = \sum_i X_i$ is the number of successes. The Beta is **conjugate** to the Bernoulli: prior and posterior belong to the same family.

The Beta distribution has density:

<Equation explicacao="Density of Beta(a,b): defined on [0,1], controlled by parameters a and b." latex="f(\theta; a, b) = \frac{\theta^{a-1}(1-\theta)^{b-1}}{B(a,b)}, \quad \theta \in [0, 1]" />

with $B(a, b) = \Gamma(a)\Gamma(b)/\Gamma(a+b)$.

</Definicao>

### Point estimators

<Definicao titulo="MAP and MLE">

Given the posterior $P(\theta \mid D)$:

- **MAP** (*maximum a posteriori*): $\hat\theta_{\text{MAP}} = \arg\max_\theta P(\theta \mid D)$. Maximizes the posterior — includes prior information.
- **MLE** (*maximum likelihood*): $\hat\theta_{\text{MLE}} = \arg\max_\theta P(D \mid \theta)$. Maximizes the likelihood — ignores the prior.
- **Posterior mean** (*Bayes quadratic estimator*): $E[\theta \mid D]$.

**Relationship:** With uniform prior ($P(\theta) = c$), MAP = MLE. As $n \to \infty$, posterior concentrates at $\hat\theta_{\text{MLE}}$ — prior becomes irrelevant as data increases.

For Beta-Bernoulli:

<Equation explicacao="MAP and posterior mean for the Beta-Bernoulli case. Compare with MLE = s/n (empirical proportion)." latex="\hat\theta_{\text{MAP}} = \frac{\alpha + s - 1}{\alpha + \beta + n - 2}, \quad E[\theta \mid D] = \frac{\alpha + s}{\alpha + \beta + n}" />

</Definicao>

### Credible interval

<Definicao titulo="HPD credible interval">

A **credible interval at level** $1 - \alpha$ is any interval $I$ with $P(\theta \in I \mid D) = 1 - \alpha$.

The **HPD** (*highest posterior density*) is the shortest among all credible intervals of the same level.

<Insight>
The credible interval is interpreted directly as "the probability that the parameter lies in $I$ is $1-\alpha$" — which the frequentist CI does not say. The frequentist CI only states that the construction procedure covers the true parameter in $1-\alpha$ of repeated samples.
</Insight>

</Definicao>

### Bayes Factor

<Definicao titulo="Bayes Factor">

To compare hypotheses $H_0$ and $H_1$:

<Equation explicacao="Ratio of marginal evidences. Measures how much the data favor H1 over H0, independent of priors." latex="BF_{10} = \frac{P(D \mid H_1)}{P(D \mid H_0)}" />

Jeffreys scale: $BF > 10$ — strong evidence for $H_1$; $BF < 1/10$ — strong for $H_0$; $1/3 < BF < 3$ — inconclusive.

</Definicao>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

You are with your mother looking for your **favorite toy**. You think it is in the bedroom — that is your **belief before looking** (the *prior*).

You go to the living room and find the **toy box open** there. Now you believe more that it is in the living room. You **changed your mind** based on what you saw (the *evidence*).

Bayes is exactly that:

1. You have a belief before (prior).
2. You see a clue (data).
3. You update your belief (posterior).

It is a mathematical way to **learn from the world**.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

Imagine that you suspect a coin is **slightly biased** — but you are not sure. This suspicion is your **prior**.

You flip the coin 10 times and get **heads 8 times**. This is strange for a fair coin. You update your suspicion — now you think it is **much more likely** that the coin is biased. This new guess is the **posterior**.

Bayes' formula does this update with precision:

$$P(\text{biased} \mid \text{8 heads}) = \frac{P(\text{8 heads} \mid \text{biased}) \times P(\text{biased})}{P(\text{8 heads})}$$

What you will see in this lesson: how to do this calculation for any situation.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old teen">

Frequentist statistics (term 10 and early 11) treats the parameter $\theta$ as **fixed and unknown** — you can estimate it, but you cannot talk about "probability of $\theta$". The frequentist CI does not say "the population mean is between $a$ and $b$ with 95% chance" — it says the *procedure* for constructing the CI captures the true $\theta$ in 95% of repeated samples.

**Bayesian statistics** changes the starting point: $\theta$ is treated as a **random variable** with a probability distribution. This allows direct questions such as "what is the probability that $\theta > 0{,}5$?" — the answer is the integral of the posterior.

**The central equation:**

$$P(\theta \mid D) \propto \underbrace{P(D \mid \theta)}_{\text{likelihood}} \times \underbrace{P(\theta)}_{\text{prior}}$$

**How to choose the prior?**

- *Informative prior*: you have prior knowledge (previous trials, expert opinion). Ex: $\text{Beta}(3, 3)$ — coin slightly fair.
- *Vague prior (diffuse)*: you know little. Ex: $\text{Beta}(1, 1) = \text{Uniform}[0,1]$.
- *Conjugate prior*: family chosen so the posterior has closed form.

**The Beta-Bernoulli case:** For coin flips or yes/no trials, if $\theta \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)$ and we observe $s$ successes in $n$ trials:

$$\theta \mid D \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha + s, \,\beta + n - s)$$

The posterior mean is $E[\theta \mid D] = \dfrac{\alpha + s}{\alpha + \beta + n}$ — a weighted average between the prior and the sample proportion $s/n$.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

**The parametric inference problem:** given the model $X_i \mid \theta \stackrel{\text{iid}}{\sim} p(x \mid \theta)$, we want to learn $\theta$ from $x_1, \ldots, x_n$.

**Frequentist paradigm:** $\theta$ is fixed — point estimator $\hat\theta$ and sampling distribution.

**Bayesian paradigm:** $\theta \sim \pi(\theta)$ (prior). After the data, the posterior is:

$$\pi(\theta \mid \mathbf{x}) = \frac{\prod_{i=1}^n p(x_i \mid \theta) \cdot \pi(\theta)}{\int \prod_{i=1}^n p(x_i \mid \theta) \cdot \pi(\theta)\, d\theta}$$

The integral in the denominator — the marginal evidence $p(\mathbf{x})$ — is often intractable in high dimensions, motivating computational methods (MCMC, VI).

**Asymptotic properties (Bernstein–von Mises):** under regularity conditions, when $n \to \infty$:

$$\pi(\theta \mid \mathbf{x}) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}\!\left(\hat\theta_{\text{MLE}},\; \frac{1}{n\,\mathcal{I}(\hat\theta)}\right)$$

where $\mathcal{I}(\theta)$ is the Fisher information. The posterior converges to the MLE — the prior dilutes with sufficient data.

**Common conjugate families:**

| Likelihood | Conjugate prior | Posterior |
|---|---|---|
| Bernoulli/Binomial | Beta($\alpha$, $\beta$) | Beta($\alpha$+$s$, $\beta$+$n$−$s$) |
| Poisson | Gamma($\alpha$, $\beta$) | Gamma($\alpha$+$\sum x_i$, $\beta$+$n$) |
| Normal (known var.) | Normal($\mu_0$, $\sigma_0^2$) | Weighted Normal |
| Multinomial | Dirichlet | Dirichlet |

**Point estimation:** The expected loss (Bayes risk) is minimized by:
- Quadratic loss → posterior mean.
- Absolute loss → posterior median.
- 0-1 loss → MAP (posterior mode).

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

**Historical context:** Thomas Bayes (1701–1761) published posthumously in 1763 the essay "An Essay towards solving a Problem in the Doctrine of Chances". Laplace independently rediscovered and generalized it to the modern form in *Théorie analytique des probabilités* (1812). The subjective probability interpretation was formalized by Bruno de Finetti (1930s) and Leonard Jimmie Savage (1954).

During the 20th century, the frequentist school (Fisher, Neyman, Pearson) dominated applied statistics — the Bayesian paradigm was seen as "unscientific" for treating parameters as random. The reversal began in the second half of the century with Harold Jeffreys, I.J. Good, and especially with the popularization of MCMC methods (Gelfand & Smith, 1990; Geman & Geman, 1984 for the *Gibbs sampler*).

**Philosophical foundations:** The subjective interpretation (Savage-de Finetti) treats probability as a coherent degree of personal belief — a rational agent must have beliefs representable by a probability measure (Savage's representation theorem). The objective interpretation (Jeffreys, Bernardo) seeks "non-informative" priors that reflect genuine ignorance — but the notion of ignorance depends on parametrization (Borel-Kolmogorov problem).

**In modern practice:** Bayesian inference is standard in hierarchical models (shrinkage, multilevel), probabilistic Machine Learning (Gaussian Processes, Bayesian Neural Networks), clinical research (FDA allows Bayesian approaches for adaptive trials), and tracking (Kalman filter as special case of Bayesian Gaussian inference). *Stan* (Carpenter et al., 2017) and *PyMC* made Bayesian inference computationally viable for arbitrary models via NUTS (No-U-Turn Sampler).

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Brazilian applications">

**Scenario 1 — Medical diagnosis**

Prevalence of disease X in the Brazilian population: 1 in 1,000 ($P(D) = 0{,}001$). Test has sensitivity 99% ($P(\text{pos} \mid D) = 0{,}99$) and specificity 95% ($P(\text{neg} \mid \neg D) = 0{,}95$).

What is $P(D \mid \text{pos})$?

$$P(D \mid \text{pos}) = \frac{0{,}99 \times 0{,}001}{0{,}99 \times 0{,}001 + 0{,}05 \times 0{,}999} \approx 1{,}94\%$$

Counterintuitive result: even with a positive test, the probability of having the disease is less than 2% — because the prevalence is very low. Bayes formalizes why screening in low-risk populations generates many false positives.

**Scenario 2 — ENEM: estimating correct answers**

An ENEM question has 5 options. Without studying, $P(\text{correct}) = 0{,}2$. If the student studied well, $P(\text{correct}) = 0{,}8$. Prior on "studied well": 60% of candidates. After observing 40 correct answers out of 50 questions, what is the posterior on "studied well"?

The Beta-Binomial model allows calculating this iteratively or via direct Bayes with two states.

**Scenario 3 — Default rate (credit)**

A bank has historical data showing 8% default on personal credit. For a new customer, prior: $\text{Beta}(8, 92)$ (expected value 8%). After 10 payments made: posterior $\text{Beta}(8, 102)$, with mean $\approx 7{,}3\%$. Risk updates with each payment.

</Porta>

## Worked examples

<Exemplo numero="1" titulo="Discrete Bayes' rule — diagnosis (basic)">

**Problem.** A disease affects 2% of the population. A test has sensitivity 90% and specificity 85%. A patient tests positive. What is the probability he has the disease?

**Strategy.** Apply Bayes' formula with partition $\{D, \neg D\}$ and calculate $P(D \mid +)$.

**Solution.**

$P(D) = 0{,}02$, $P(\neg D) = 0{,}98$, $P(+ \mid D) = 0{,}90$, $P(+ \mid \neg D) = 0{,}15$.

Total evidence:
$$P(+) = P(+ \mid D)\,P(D) + P(+ \mid \neg D)\,P(\neg D)$$
$$= 0{,}90 \times 0{,}02 + 0{,}15 \times 0{,}98 = 0{,}018 + 0{,}147 = 0{,}165$$

Posterior:
$$P(D \mid +) = \frac{0{,}90 \times 0{,}02}{0{,}165} = \frac{0{,}018}{0{,}165} \approx 0{,}109 = 10{,}9\%$$

**Verification.** With only 2% prevalence, even a relatively good test generates many false positives. The answer $\approx 11\%$ makes sense: most positives come from the huge healthy population.

**Source.** [OpenIntro Statistics §3.6, medical diagnosis example](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/) — CC-BY-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="2" titulo="Sequential Beta-Bernoulli update (intermediate)">

**Problem.** An urn has unknown proportion $\theta$ of red balls. Prior: $\theta \sim \text{Beta}(2, 2)$. Draw with replacement: 1st sample — 3 red in 5 draws; 2nd sample — 4 red in 6 draws. Calculate the posterior after each sample and the final posterior mean.

**Strategy.** Beta-Bernoulli: after $s$ successes in $n$ trials, $\text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta) \to \text{Beta}(\alpha+s, \beta+n-s)$. Apply iteratively.

**Solution.**

Prior: $\text{Beta}(2, 2)$, mean $= 2/4 = 0{,}50$.

After 1st sample ($s = 3$, $n = 5$):
$$\text{Beta}(2+3,\; 2+5-3) = \text{Beta}(5, 4), \quad E[\theta] = \frac{5}{9} \approx 0{,}556$$

After 2nd sample ($s = 4$, $n = 6$):
$$\text{Beta}(5+4,\; 4+6-4) = \text{Beta}(9, 6), \quad E[\theta] = \frac{9}{15} = 0{,}600$$

**Verification.** Total data: 7 red in 11 draws, sample proportion = $7/11 \approx 0{,}636$. Posterior mean 0.60 is between the prior (0.50) and the sample proportion — makes sense. With weak prior, the posterior converges to the MLE as $n$ grows.

**Source.** [Think Bayes §3 — Allen Downey](https://greenteapress.com/wp/think-bayes/) — CC-BY-NC-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="3" titulo="MAP vs MLE vs posterior mean (intermediate)">

**Problem.** For the Beta-Bernoulli model with $\alpha = 3$, $\beta = 3$, after 6 successes in 10 trials, calculate the MLE, MAP, and posterior mean. Interpret the difference.

**Strategy.** MLE maximizes the likelihood; MAP maximizes the posterior; posterior mean is $E[\theta \mid D]$.

**Solution.**

MLE: $\hat\theta_{\text{MLE}} = s/n = 6/10 = 0{,}600$.

Posterior: $\text{Beta}(3+6, 3+4) = \text{Beta}(9, 7)$.

MAP (mode of Beta$(a, b)$ with $a, b > 1$ is $(a-1)/(a+b-2)$):
$$\hat\theta_{\text{MAP}} = \frac{9 - 1}{9 + 7 - 2} = \frac{8}{14} \approx 0{,}571$$

Posterior mean:
$$E[\theta \mid D] = \frac{9}{9 + 7} = \frac{9}{16} = 0{,}5625$$

**Verification.** Ordering: mean $(0{,}5625)$ between MAP $(0{,}571)$ and the mode of Beta(9,7). MLE $(0{,}60)$ is largest — the prior "pulls" toward 0.5 (symmetric prior around 0.5). With large $n$, all three converge to the MLE.

**Source.** [Think Bayes §4, §6 — Allen Downey](https://greenteapress.com/wp/think-bayes/) — CC-BY-NC-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="4" titulo="95% Credible interval Beta (advanced)">

**Problem.** After 12 successes in 20 trials with prior $\text{Beta}(1, 1)$ (uniform), calculate the central 95% credible interval for $\theta$.

**Strategy.** Posterior $\text{Beta}(13, 9)$. The central 95% interval is given by the 2.5% and 97.5% percentiles of the Beta distribution.

**Solution.**

Posterior: $\text{Beta}(1+12, 1+8) = \text{Beta}(13, 9)$.

Posterior mean: $13/(13+9) = 13/22 \approx 0{,}591$.

By table or software (R: `qbeta(c(0.025, 0.975), 13, 9)`):

2.5% percentile: $\approx 0{,}376$. 97.5% percentile: $\approx 0{,}779$.

**95% credible interval: $(0{,}376;\; 0{,}779)$.**

**Verification.** Direct interpretation: "given the uniform prior and the data, the probability that $\theta$ is between 0.376 and 0.779 is 95%". Note the interval is not centered at 0.6 — it is asymmetric because the Beta is asymmetric in this case.

**Source.** [Introduction to Probability §4.1 — Grinstead & Snell](https://chance.dartmouth.edu/teaching_aids/books_articles/probability_book/book.html) — GNU FDL.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="5" titulo="Bayes factor — hypothesis comparison (proof)">

**Problem.** To test whether $\theta = 0{,}5$ (fair coin) versus $\theta = 0{,}7$ (biased coin), with equiprobable prior ($P(H_0) = P(H_1) = 0{,}5$), calculate the Bayes factor and the posterior probability of $H_1$ after 8 heads in 10 flips.

**Strategy.** Calculate $P(D \mid H_i)$ for each hypothesis, then apply Bayes.

**Solution.**

$$P(D \mid H_0) = \binom{10}{8}(0{,}5)^{10} = 45 \times \frac{1}{1024} \approx 0{,}0439$$

$$P(D \mid H_1) = \binom{10}{8}(0{,}7)^8(0{,}3)^2 = 45 \times 0{,}05765 \times 0{,}09 \approx 0{,}2335$$

Bayes factor:
$$BF_{10} = \frac{0{,}2335}{0{,}0439} \approx 5{,}32$$

With prior $P(H_0) = P(H_1) = 0{,}5$:
$$P(H_1 \mid D) = \frac{BF_{10}}{1 + BF_{10}} = \frac{5{,}32}{6{,}32} \approx 0{,}842$$

**Verification.** $BF_{10} \approx 5{,}32$ — moderate evidence for $H_1$ (Jeffreys scale: between 3 and 10 is "moderate"). The posterior probability of a biased coin went from 50% to 84%. Consistent with the data (8 out of 10 favors $\theta = 0{,}7$).

**Source.** [OpenIntro Statistics §3.7 — Diez, Çetinkaya-Rundel, Barr](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/) — CC-BY-SA.

</Exemplo>
