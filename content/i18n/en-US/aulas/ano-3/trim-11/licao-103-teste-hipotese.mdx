### The five elements of a hypothesis test

<Definicao titulo="Formal structure of the test">

1. **Hypotheses**: $H_0$ (null) — the claim to be tested — versus $H_1$ (alternative).
2. **Test statistic** $T = T(X_1, \ldots, X_n)$: a calculated value from the sample with a known distribution under $H_0$.
3. **Significance level** $\alpha$: the maximum tolerable probability of rejecting $H_0$ when it is true. Typical: $\alpha = 0.05$.
4. **Rejection region** $\mathcal{R}$: the set of values of $T$ that lead to rejection of $H_0$.
5. **Decision**: reject $H_0$ if $T \in \mathcal{R}$ (or equivalently, if $p\text{-value} \leq \alpha$).

</Definicao>

> "The null hypothesis $H_0$ represents a claim of skepticism. It is the status quo that would be maintained unless there is sufficient evidence against it." — [OpenIntro Statistics, §5.1](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/)

### Errors and test power

<Definicao titulo="Type I Error, Type II Error, and Power">

|  | $H_0$ true | $H_0$ false |
|---|---|---|
| **Reject $H_0$** | Type I Error ($\alpha$) | Correct decision (power $= 1-\beta$) |
| **Fail to reject $H_0$** | Correct decision | Type II Error ($\beta$) |

- **Type I Error** (false positive): reject $H_0$ when it is true. Probability controlled by $\alpha$.
- **Type II Error** (false negative): fail to reject $H_0$ when it is false. Probability $\beta$ (depends on $H_1$, $\sigma$, $n$).
- **Power** $= 1 - \beta$: probability of detecting the true effect.

For fixed sample size, decreasing $\alpha$ increases $\beta$ (trade-off). To increase power without sacrificing $\alpha$: increase $n$.

</Definicao>

### Formal definition of p-value

<Definicao titulo="p-value">

The **p-value** is the probability that the test statistic assumes a value as extreme or more extreme than observed, assuming $H_0$ is true:

<Equation explicacao="Two-tailed p-value: considers deviations in both directions from the null mean." latex="p = P(\lvert T \rvert \geq \lvert t_{\mathrm{obs}} \rvert \mid H_0) \quad \text{(two-tailed)}" />

<Equation explicacao="One-tailed p-value to the right: detects effects above mu0." latex="p = P(T \geq t_{\mathrm{obs}} \mid H_0) \quad \text{(one-tailed } H_1: \mu > \mu_0\text{)}" />

</Definicao>

> "The p-value measures how consistent the data are with $H_0$. A small p-value indicates that the data are incompatible with $H_0$ — not that $H_0$ is false with probability $1-p$." — [OpenIntro Statistics, §5.1](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/)

### Types of alternative hypothesis

<Definicao titulo="Two-tailed and one-tailed tests">

For $H_0: \mu = \mu_0$:

- $H_1: \mu \neq \mu_0$ — **two-tailed**. Rejection region on both sides: $\lvert T \rvert > t_{\alpha/2}$.
- $H_1: \mu > \mu_0$ — **one-tailed to the right**. Rejection region: $T > t_\alpha$.
- $H_1: \mu < \mu_0$ — **one-tailed to the left**. Rejection region: $T < -t_\alpha$.

The two-tailed test is more conservative (two-tailed p-value = $2 \times$ corresponding one-tailed p-value).

</Definicao>

<Insight>

The p-value **is not**: the probability that $H_0$ is true; the probability that the result occurred by chance; the probability that the effect is real. It is merely a measure of compatibility between the data and $H_0$ under a specified model. The American Statistical Association (ASA) issued a formal statement in 2016 warning about p-value misuse.

</Insight>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

Your friend says he has a **normal** die. You suspect it is loaded. How do you prove it?

You assume: "okay, I'll pretend the die is normal." This is the **null hypothesis** — the status quo.

Then you roll it 60 times. If you got a 6 in 40 of those times (should be around 10), something is very wrong! That's a **small p-value** — the results are too strange if the die were normal.

When the result is too strange to be a coincidence, you reject: "that die is loaded!"

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

Imagine you suspect that a coin is biased. You flip it 100 times and get heads 70 times.

**Step 1**: Null hypothesis — "the coin is fair, heads comes up 50% of the time."

**Step 2**: Alternative hypothesis — "the coin is biased."

**Step 3**: Key question — if the coin were fair, what is the probability of getting 70 or more heads in 100 flips?

**Step 4**: Calculate that number. It is the **p-value**. If it is very small (less than 5%), you conclude: it does not make sense to explain this by luck — the coin is biased!

**Rule of thumb**: p-value less than 0.05 (5%) indicates that the data are hard to explain by the null hypothesis. We reject it.

</Porta>

### The logic of hypothesis testing

Hypothesis testing is a formal protocol for making decisions based on evidence, while controlling the probability of error.

**The 5-step framework:**

1. **Formulate $H_0$ and $H_1$**: $H_0$ is the status quo (no effect, no difference), $H_1$ is what you want to demonstrate.

2. **Choose $\alpha$**: usually 0.05 (5%) for social sciences, 0.01 (1%) for medicine, $5 \times 10^{-7}$ for particle physics.

3. **Calculate the test statistic**: a number that summarizes how far the data are from $H_0$.

4. **Calculate the p-value**: probability of observing something as extreme as what was observed, if $H_0$ were true.

5. **Decide**: if $p \leq \alpha$, reject $H_0$. If $p > \alpha$, "fail to reject" (note: this does not mean $H_0$ is true!).

### Type I Error vs. Type II Error

You never have certainty. The two possible errors:

| Error | When it occurs | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Type I (false positive) | Reject $H_0$ when it is true | Announce an effect that does not exist |
| Type II (false negative) | Fail to reject $H_0$ when it is false | Miss a real effect |

$\alpha$ controls Type I. Increasing $n$ reduces Type II.

### Example

A factory claims the average lifetime of its light bulbs is $\mu_0 = 1000$ h. A sample of $n = 25$ bulbs gives $\bar X = 980$ h with $s = 50$ h.

$H_0: \mu = 1000$ h versus $H_1: \mu < 1000$ h (manufacturer overstates).

Test statistic t: $T = (980 - 1000)/(50/\sqrt{25}) = -20/10 = -2.0$.

For $t_{24}$ one-tailed with $\alpha = 0.05$: critical value $\approx -1.711$. Since $-2.0 < -1.711$, we reject $H_0$.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

### The test as an optimal decision rule

Neyman-Pearson theory formalizes the problem: given the trade-off between Type I and Type II errors, what is the **optimal rejection region** for simple hypotheses $H_0: \theta = \theta_0$ vs $H_1: \theta = \theta_1$?

**Neyman-Pearson Lemma**: the most powerful rejection region for level $\alpha$ is:

$$\mathcal{R}_{NP} = \left\{\mathbf{x} : \frac{L(\theta_1 \mid \mathbf{x})}{L(\theta_0 \mid \mathbf{x})} > c_\alpha\right\}$$

where $c_\alpha$ is chosen so that $P(\mathbf{X} \in \mathcal{R}_{NP} \mid H_0) = \alpha$.

For composite hypotheses, the generalized likelihood ratio test (GLRT) extends this result.

### Test power and sample size calculation

**Power** $1 - \beta = P(\text{reject } H_0 \mid H_1)$ depends on:

- True distance $\delta = |\mu_1 - \mu_0|$ (effect size)
- $\sigma$ (variability)
- $n$ (sample size)
- $\alpha$ (significance level)

To detect difference $\delta$ with power $1-\beta$ and level $\alpha$ (two-tailed test for mean):

$$n = \frac{(z_{\alpha/2} + z_\beta)^2 \sigma^2}{\delta^2}$$

For $\alpha = 0.05$ and power 80% ($z_\beta = 0.842$): $n = (1.96 + 0.842)^2 \sigma^2/\delta^2 \approx 7.85\,\sigma^2/\delta^2$.

### p-value as a random variable

Under $H_0$, the p-value has distribution $\mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)$. Therefore $P(p \leq \alpha \mid H_0) = \alpha$ exactly — confirming that $\alpha$ controls Type I error. Under $H_1$, the p-value distribution is concentrated near 0.

### Multiple testing and FDR

When performing $m$ simultaneous tests, the probability of at least one false positive (FWER) is $1 - (1-\alpha)^m$. For $m = 20$ and $\alpha = 0.05$: FWER $= 1 - 0.95^{20} \approx 64\%$.

Bonferroni correction: use $\alpha/m$ per test. Alternative: FDR control (Benjamini-Hochberg, 1995) — more powerful when many tests are performed on high-dimensional data (genomics, neuroimaging).

### Statistical significance vs. practical significance

A result can be statistically significant but have such a small effect that it is clinically irrelevant. Cohen's standardized **effect size**:

$$d = \frac{|\bar X - \mu_0|}{s}$$

$d = 0.2$ (small), $d = 0.5$ (medium), $d = 0.8$ (large) — Cohen's conventions.

### The replicability crisis and the debate on p-values

In 2016, the American Statistical Association published an unprecedented statement listing six principles on p-value use, warning that:

1. The p-value is not the probability that $H_0$ is true.
2. A p-value above 0.05 does not mean there is no effect.
3. Scientific decisions should not be based solely on whether $p < 0.05$.

The replicability movement (Nosek et al., 2015: only 36% of 100 psychology studies replicated) revealed that mechanical p-value use, combined with p-hacking, HARKing, and publication bias, produced a scientific literature systematically inflated in effect sizes.

### Alternatives to NHST

- **Equivalence and non-inferiority**: test whether $|\mu - \mu_0| < \delta$ (effect is small enough to ignore). Standard in pharmaceutical generic trials.
- **Bayes Factor**: $\mathrm{BF}_{10} = P(\text{data} \mid H_1)/P(\text{data} \mid H_0)$ — directly compares hypotheses. Interpretable: $\mathrm{BF} > 10$ is strong evidence for $H_1$.
- **Bayesian analysis**: posterior $P(\theta \mid \text{data})$ gives direct probability on parameters. Avoids frequentist confusion.
- **e-values and anytime-valid inference**: extension that allows data peeking without inflating $\alpha$, solving the early stopping problem.

### Higgs boson detection and the $5\sigma$ criterion

The discovery of the Higgs boson at the LHC in 2012 used the criterion $p < 2.9 \times 10^{-7}$ (equivalent to $5\sigma$ in standard Normal). The stricter-than-usual criterion reflects: (1) the irreversibility of announcing a new particle; (2) multiple tests over time; (3) cost of a false positive (construction of new experiments). François Englert and Peter Higgs received the [Nobel Prize in Physics 2013](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/2013/summary/) for the theoretical prediction confirmed experimentally.

### Multiple testing in business context

A/B testing on e-commerce platforms often involves hundreds of simultaneous experiments. Controlling FDR instead of FWER allows 5% of significant tests to be false positives (as expected), balancing the cost of false discoveries with the power to detect true effects. Shopify and Netflix have publicly reported using Bayesian frameworks to overcome NHST problems at scale.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Brazilian practical application">

### Anvisa: efficacy of a new medication

A laboratory wants to register a painkiller. The protocol requires demonstrating that the mean reduction in pain is greater than placebo by at least 1 point on a 0-to-10 scale.

$H_0: \mu_{\text{treatment}} - \mu_{\text{placebo}} \leq 1$ (ineffective)
$H_1: \mu_{\text{treatment}} - \mu_{\text{placebo}} > 1$ (effective)

With $\alpha = 0.01$ (medical rigor) and 90% power, the laboratory calculates the necessary $n$ and plans the clinical trial. If $p < 0.01$, it requests registration.

### Quality control: bottle filling process

A factory fills 1-liter bottles. The process is in control if $\mu = 1000$ mL. Every hour, $n = 9$ bottles are collected.

$H_0: \mu = 1000$ mL versus $H_1: \mu \neq 1000$ mL (two-tailed).

If the sample mean falls outside $1000 \pm 3\sigma/\sqrt{9} = 1000 \pm \sigma$, an alarm is triggered. This is the limit of the $\bar X$ control chart with $3\sigma$ (equivalent to $\alpha \approx 0.003$ per point).

### ENEM: comparing school performance

INEP uses hypothesis tests to verify whether a school's mean score differs significantly from the state average. With $n$ large (all schools), practically any difference becomes "statistically significant" — hence the importance of reporting effect size (difference in scale points), not just the p-value.

</Porta>

## Worked examples

<Exemplo numero="103.1" titulo="Two-tailed test for mean with z (basic)">

**Problem.** A company claims the average weight of its coffee packages is $\mu_0 = 500$ g. A sample of $n = 36$ packages gives $\bar X = 492$ g with $\sigma = 24$ g (known). At level $\alpha = 0.05$, do the data contradict the claim?

**Strategy.** $H_0: \mu = 500$, $H_1: \mu \neq 500$ (two-tailed). Use the z-statistic since $\sigma$ is known.

**Solution.**

$$Z = \frac{\bar X - \mu_0}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}} = \frac{492 - 500}{24/\sqrt{36}} = \frac{-8}{4} = -2.00$$

Two-tailed p-value: $p = 2\,P(Z \leq -2.00) = 2 \times 0.0228 = 0.0456$.

Since $p = 0.0456 < \alpha = 0.05$, **we reject $H_0$**. The data contradict the company's claim at the 5% level.

**Verification.** The critical value for two-tailed with $\alpha = 0.05$ is $z_{0.025} = 1.960$. Since $|-2.00| = 2.00 > 1.960$, rejection by the statistic agrees with the p-value. Consistent.

**Source.** [OpenStax Statistics, §9.2, Example 9.3](https://openstax.org/books/statistics/pages/9-2-outcomes-and-the-type-i-and-type-ii-errors) — CC-BY.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="103.2" titulo="Identification of Type I and II errors (conceptual)">

**Problem.** A quality test checks whether a batch of medications has average active ingredient concentration of 50 mg ($H_0$). At level 5%, the batch is approved or rejected. (a) What constitutes a Type I Error in this context? (b) What about Type II Error? (c) Which is more serious?

**Strategy.** Map the formal definitions to the specific context.

**Solution.**

(a) **Type I Error**: reject $H_0$ when $\mu = 50$ mg — that is, reject a batch that is actually in specification. Consequence: waste of good product, rework costs.

(b) **Type II Error**: fail to reject $H_0$ when $\mu \neq 50$ mg — that is, approve a batch out of specification. Consequence: sub-dosed or over-dosed medication reaches the patient.

(c) In most pharmaceutical contexts, **Type II Error is more serious**: a medication out of specification can harm the patient. For this reason, clinical trials use small $\alpha$ but require high power (low $\beta$), increasing $n$.

**Verification.** The asymmetry between the errors justifies calibrating $\alpha$ and $\beta$ differently by context — medicine uses $\alpha = 0.01$ and power of 80-90%.

**Source.** [OpenIntro Statistics, §5.2, Example 5.4](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/) — CC-BY-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="103.3" titulo="Power calculation and sample size (intermediate)">

**Problem.** A researcher wants to detect that the average response time at an urgent care center changed from $\mu_0 = 30$ min to $\mu_1 = 27$ min ($\delta = 3$ min), with $\sigma = 10$ min, $\alpha = 0.05$ (two-tailed), and 80% power. What is the minimum $n$?

**Strategy.** Apply the sample size formula for power: $n = (z_{\alpha/2} + z_\beta)^2 \sigma^2/\delta^2$.

**Solution.**

$z_{0.025} = 1.960$, $z_{0.20} = 0.842$ (80% power $\Rightarrow \beta = 0.20$).

$$n = \frac{(1.960 + 0.842)^2 \times 10^2}{3^2} = \frac{(2.802)^2 \times 100}{9} = \frac{7.851 \times 100}{9} \approx 87.2$$

Round: $n = 88$ visits.

**Verification.** If $\delta = 6$ min (doubled effect): $n = 7.851 \times 100/36 \approx 22$. Larger effect requires smaller sample — consistent.

**Source.** [OpenIntro Statistics, §5.3, Example 5.7](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/) — CC-BY-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="103.4" titulo="One-tailed test — medication (intermediate)">

**Problem.** A new anticoagulant claims to reduce mean clotting time from $\mu_0 = 12$ s to below that. A sample of $n = 20$ patients gives $\bar X = 11.2$ s and $s = 2$ s. At level $\alpha = 0.05$, is the medication effective?

**Strategy.** $H_0: \mu \geq 12$, $H_1: \mu < 12$ (one-tailed to the left). t-statistic with 19 degrees of freedom.

**Solution.**

$$T = \frac{11.2 - 12}{2/\sqrt{20}} = \frac{-0.8}{0.4472} = -1.789$$

For $H_1: \mu < 12$, p-value $= P(t_{19} \leq -1.789)$. From t-table: $P(t_{19} \leq -1.729) = 0.05$ and $P(t_{19} \leq -2.093) = 0.025$. So $p \approx 0.045$.

Since $p = 0.045 < 0.05$, **we reject $H_0$**. Evidence that the medication reduces clotting time.

**Verification.** One-tailed critical value: $t_{0.05,\,19} = -1.729$. Since $T = -1.789 < -1.729$, rejection by the statistic agrees. Consistent.

**Source.** [OpenStax Statistics, §9.4, Example 9.8](https://openstax.org/books/statistics/pages/9-introduction) — CC-BY.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="103.5" titulo="Misinterpretation of p-value and correction (advanced)">

**Problem.** A researcher obtains $p = 0.03$ in a test of $H_0: \mu = 0$ and claims: "There is a 97% probability that the effect is real." Identify the error and formulate the correct interpretation.

**Strategy.** Apply the formal definition of p-value and distinguish probabilities on data from probabilities on hypotheses.

**Solution.**

The statement is **incorrect** for two reasons:

1. The p-value is a probability about the **data** (given $H_0$), not about the **hypotheses**. $P(\text{data} \mid H_0) \neq P(H_0 \mid \text{data})$ — confusing the two is the **fallacy of transposed conditionals** (base rate neglect).

2. $1 - p\text{-value} = 0.97$ has no interpretation as probability of $H_1$. To obtain $P(H_1 \mid \text{data})$, you would need Bayes's Theorem with a prior on the hypotheses.

**Correct interpretation**: "If $H_0$ were true, there would be only a 3% probability of observing an effect as large (or larger) as what was observed. The data are statistically incompatible with $H_0$ at the 5% level."

**Verification.** Two independent studies each with $p = 0.04$ do not imply a third study with $p = 0.04$ — combining evidence is done via meta-analysis, not by multiplying p-values.

**Source.** [OpenIntro Statistics, §5.1, Section "Interpreting p-values"](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/) — CC-BY-SA.

</Exemplo>
