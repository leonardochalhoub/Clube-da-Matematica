<Porta nivel="formal" titulo="Rigorous definition">

### The five elements of a hypothesis test

<Definicao titulo="Formal test structure">

1. **Hypotheses**: $H_0$ (null) — the claim to be tested — versus $H_1$ (alternative).
2. **Test statistic** $T = T(X_1, \ldots, X_n)$: calculated sample value with a known distribution under $H_0$.
3. **Significance level** $\alpha$: maximum tolerable probability of rejecting $H_0$ when it is true. Typical: $\alpha = 0.05$.
4. **Rejection region** $\mathcal{R}$: set of values of $T$ that lead to the rejection of $H_0$.
5. **Decision**: reject $H_0$ if $T \in \mathcal{R}$ (or equivalently, if $p\text{-value} \leq \alpha$).

</Definicao>

> "The null hypothesis $H_0$ represents a claim of skepticism. It is the status quo that would be maintained unless there is sufficient evidence against it." — [OpenIntro Statistics, §5.1](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/)

### Errors and test power

<Definicao titulo="Type I Error, Type II Error, and Power">

|  | $H_0$ true | $H_0$ false |
|---|---|---|
| **Reject $H_0$** | Type I Error ($\alpha$) | Correct decision (power $= 1-\beta$) |
| **Do not reject $H_0$** | Correct decision | Type II Error ($\beta$) |

- **Type I Error** (false positive): rejecting $H_0$ when it is true. Probability controlled by $\alpha$.
- **Type II Error** (false negative): failing to reject $H_0$ when it is false. Probability $\beta$ (depends on $H_1$, $\sigma$, $n$).
- **Power** $= 1 - \beta$: probability of detecting the real effect.

For a fixed sample size, decreasing $\alpha$ increases $\beta$ (trade-off). To increase power without sacrificing $\alpha$: increase $n$.

</Definicao>

### Formal definition of the p-value

<Definicao titulo="p-value">

The **p-value** is the probability that the test statistic assumes a value as extreme as, or more extreme than, the one observed, assuming $H_0$ is true:

<Equation explicacao="Two-tailed p-value: considers deviations in both directions from the null mean." latex="p = P(\lvert T \rvert \geq \lvert t_{\mathrm{obs}} \rvert \mid H_0) \quad \text{(two-tailed)}" />

<Equation explicacao="Right-tailed p-value: detects effects above mu0." latex="p = P(T \geq t_{\mathrm{obs}} \mid H_0) \quad \text{(one-tailed } H_1: \mu > \mu_0\text{)}" />

</Definicao>

> "The p-value measures how consistent the data are with $H_0$. A small p-value indicates that the data are incompatible with $H_0$ — not that $H_0$ is false with probability $1-p$." — [OpenIntro Statistics, §5.1](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/)

### Types of alternative hypothesis

<Definicao titulo="Two-tailed and one-tailed tests">

For $H_0: \mu = \mu_0$:

- $H_1: \mu \neq \mu_0$ — **two-tailed**. Rejection region on both sides: $\lvert T \rvert > t_{\alpha/2}$.
- $H_1: \mu > \mu_0$ — **right-tailed**. Rejection region: $T > t_\alpha$.
- $H_1: \mu < \mu_0$ — **left-tailed**. Rejection region: $T < -t_\alpha$.

The two-tailed test is more conservative (two-tailed p-value = $2 \times$ corresponding one-tailed p-value).

</Definicao>

<Insight>

The p-value is **not**: the probability that $H_0$ is true; the probability that the result is due to chance; the probability that the effect is real. It is merely a measure of compatibility between the data and $H_0$ under a specified model. The American Statistical Association (ASA) issued a formal statement in 2016 warning against the incorrect use of the p-value.

</Insight>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

Your friend says they have a **normal** die. You suspect it is loaded. How to prove it?

You assume: "okay, I'll pretend the die is normal." This is the **null hypothesis** — the status quo.

Then you roll it 60 times. If you get a 6 in 40 of those rolls (it should be about 10), something is very wrong! This is a **small p-value** — the results are very strange if the die were normal.

When the result is too strange to be a coincidence, you reject: "this die is loaded!"

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

Imagine you suspect a coin is loaded. You flip it 100 times and get 70 heads.

**Step 1**: Null hypothesis — "the coin is fair, it gets heads 50% of the time."

**Step 2**: Alternative hypothesis — "the coin is loaded."

**Step 3**: Key question — if the coin were fair, what is the probability of getting 70 or more heads in 100 flips?

**Step 4**: Calculate this number. It is the **p-value**. If it is very small (less than 5%), you conclude: it makes no sense to explain this by luck — the coin is loaded!

**Rule of thumb**: p-value less than 0.05 (5%) indicates that the data are hard to explain by the null hypothesis. We reject it.

</Porta>

### The logic of hypothesis testing

Hypothesis testing is a formal protocol for making decisions based on evidence, controlling the probability of error.

**The 5-step structure:**

1. **Formulate $H_0$ and $H_1$**: $H_0$ is the status quo (no effect, no difference), $H_1$ is what you want to demonstrate.

2. **Choose $\alpha$**: usually 0.05 (5%) for social sciences, 0.01 (1%) for medicine, $5 \times 10^{-7}$ for particle physics.

3. **Calculate the test statistic**: a number that summarizes how far the data are from $H_0$.

4. **Calculate the p-value**: probability of observing something as extreme as what was observed, if $H_0$ were true.

5. **Decide**: if $p \leq \alpha$, reject $H_0$. If $p > \alpha$, "do not reject" (caution: this does not mean $H_0$ is true!).

### Type I Error vs. Type II Error

You are never certain. The two possible errors:

| Error | When it occurs | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Type I (false positive) | Reject $H_0$ when it is true | Announce an effect that doesn't exist |
| Type II (false negative) | Do not reject $H_0$ when it is false | Miss a real effect |

$\alpha$ controls Type I. Increasing $n$ reduces Type II.

### Example

A factory claims that the average lifespan of its light bulbs is $\mu_0 = 1000$ h. A sample of $n = 25$ bulbs gives $\bar X = 980$ h with $s = 50$ h.

$H_0: \mu = 1000$ h versus $H_1: \mu < 1000$ h (manufacturer overestimates).

t-statistic: $T = (980 - 1000)/(50/\sqrt{25}) = -20/10 = -2.0$.

For one-tailed $t_{24}$ with $\alpha = 0.05$: critical value $\approx -1.711$. Since $-2.0 < -1.711$, we reject $H_0$.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

### The test as an optimal decision rule

The Neyman-Pearson test formalizes the problem: given the trade-off between Type I and Type II, what is the **optimal rejection region** for simple hypotheses $H_0: \theta = \theta_0$ vs $H_1: \theta = \theta_1$?

**Neyman-Pearson Lemma**: the most powerful rejection region for level $\alpha$ is:

$$\mathcal{R}_{NP} = \left\{\mathbf{x} : \frac{L(\theta_1 \mid \mathbf{x})}{L(\theta_0 \mid \mathbf{x})} > c_\alpha\right\}$$

where $c_\alpha$ is chosen so that $P(\mathbf{X} \in \mathcal{R}_{NP} \mid H_0) = \alpha$.

For composite hypotheses, the Generalized Likelihood Ratio Test (GLRT) extends this result.

### Test power and $n$ calculation

The **power** $1 - \beta = P(\text{reject } H_0 \mid H_1)$ depends on:

- Real distance $\delta = |\mu_1 - \mu_0|$ (effect size)
- $\sigma$ (variability)
- $n$ (sample size)
- $\alpha$ (significance level)

To detect difference $\delta$ with power $1-\beta$ and level $\alpha$ (two-tailed test for mean):

$$n = \frac{(z_{\alpha/2} + z_\beta)^2 \sigma^2}{\delta^2}$$

For $\alpha = 0.05$ and 80% power ($z_\beta = 0.842$): $n = (1.96 + 0.842)^2 \sigma^2/\delta^2 \approx 7.85\,\sigma^2/\delta^2$.

### p-value as a random statistic

Under $H_0$, the p-value has a $\mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)$ distribution. Therefore $P(p \leq \alpha \mid H_0) = \alpha$ exactly — confirming that $\alpha$ controls the Type I error. Under $H_1$, the p-value distribution is concentrated near 0.

### Multiple testing and FDR

When performing $m$ simultaneous tests, the probability of at least one false positive (FWER) is $1 - (1-\alpha)^m$. For $m = 20$ and $\alpha = 0.05$: FWER $= 1 - 0.95^{20} \approx 64\%$.

Bonferroni correction: use $\alpha/m$ per test. Alternative: FDR control (Benjamini-Hochberg, 1995) — more powerful when many tests are performed on high-dimensional data (genomics, neuroimaging).

### Statistical vs. practical significance

A result can be statistically significant but have an effect so small that it is clinically irrelevant. Cohen's standardized **effect size**:

$$d = \frac{|\bar X - \mu_0|}{s}$$

$d = 0.2$ (small), $d = 0.5$ (medium), $d = 0.8$ (large) — Cohen's conventions.

</Porta>

### The replicability crisis and the p-value debate

In 2016, the American Statistical Association published an unprecedented statement listing six principles on the use of the p-value, warning that:

1. The p-value is not the probability that $H_0$ is true.
2. A p-value above 0.05 does not mean there is no effect.
3. Scientific decisions should not be based solely on whether $p < 0.05$.

The replicability movement (Nosek et al., 2015: only 36% of 100 psychology studies replicated) revealed that the mechanical use of the p-value, combined with *p-hacking*, HARKing, and publication bias, produced a body of scientific literature that is systematically inflated.

### Alternatives to NHST

- **Equivalence and non-inferiority**: test if $|\mu - \mu_0| < \delta$ (effect is small enough to be ignored). Standard in clinical trials for generic pharmaceuticals.
- **Bayes Factor**: $\mathrm{BF}_{10} = P(\text{data} \mid H_1)/P(\text{data} \mid H_0)$ — directly compares hypotheses. Interpretable: $\mathrm{BF} > 10$ is strong evidence for $H_1$.
- **Bayesian analysis**: posterior $P(\theta \mid \text{data})$ gives direct probability about parameters. Avoids frequentist confusion.
- **e-values and anytime-valid inference**: extension that allows peeking at data without inflating $\alpha$, solving the problem of stopping the experiment early.

### Higgs Boson detection and $5\sigma$ criterion

The discovery of the Higgs boson at the LHC in 2012 used the criterion $p < 2.9 \times 10^{-7}$ (equivalent to $5\sigma$ in the standard Normal). The stricter criterion than the usual 0.05 reflects: (1) the irreversibility of announcing a new particle; (2) multiple tests over time; (3) cost of a false positive (construction of new experiments). François Englert and Peter Higgs received the [2013 Nobel Prize in Physics](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/2013/summary/) for the theoretical prediction confirmed.

### Multiple testing in a business context

A/B testing on e-commerce platforms often involves hundreds of simultaneous experiments. Controlling FDR instead of FWER allows 5% of significant tests to be false positives (expected), balancing the cost of false discoveries with the power to detect real effects. Shopify and Netflix have publicly reported using Bayesian frameworks to overcome NHST problems at scale.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practical application">

### Anvisa: efficacy of a new drug

A laboratory wants to register an analgesic. The protocol requires demonstrating that the average pain reduction is greater than placebo by at least 1 point on a 0-10 scale.

$H_0: \mu_{\text{treatment}} - \mu_{\text{placebo}} \leq 1$ (ineffective)
$H_1: \mu_{\text{treatment}} - \mu_{\text{placebo}} > 1$ (effective)

With $\alpha = 0.01$ (medical rigor) and 90% power, the lab calculates the necessary $n$ and plans the clinical trial. If $p < 0.01$, it requests registration.

### Quality control: filling process

A factory fills 1-liter bottles. The process is under control if $\mu = 1000$ mL. Every hour, $n = 9$ bottles are collected.

$H_0: \mu = 1000$ mL versus $H_1: \mu \neq 1000$ mL (two-tailed).

If the sample mean falls outside $1000 \pm 3\sigma/\sqrt{9} = 1000 \pm \sigma$, an alarm is triggered. This is the limit of the $\bar X$ control chart with $3\sigma$ (equivalent to $\alpha \approx 0.003$ per point).

### ENEM: performance comparison by school

INEP uses hypothesis tests to verify if a school's average differs significantly from the state average. With large $n$ (all schools), practically any difference becomes "statistically significant" — hence the importance of reporting effect size (difference in points on the scale), not just the p-value.

</Porta>

## Solved examples

<Exemplo numero="103.1" titulo="Two-tailed z-test for mean (basic)">

**Problem.** A company claims the average weight of its coffee bags is $\mu_0 = 500$ g. A sample of $n = 36$ bags gives $\bar X = 492$ g with $\sigma = 24$ g (known). At level $\alpha = 0.05$, do the data contradict the claim?

**Strategy.** $H_0: \mu = 500$, $H_1: \mu \neq 500$ (two-tailed). Use the z-statistic since $\sigma$ is known.

**Resolution.**

$$Z = \frac{\bar X - \mu_0}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}} = \frac{492 - 500}{24/\sqrt{36}} = \frac{-8}{4} = -2.00$$

Two-tailed p-value: $p = 2\,P(Z \leq -2.00) = 2 \times 0.0228 = 0.0456$.

Since $p = 0.0456 < \alpha = 0.05$, **we reject $H_0$**. The data contradict the company's claim at the 5% level.

**Verification.** The critical value for two-tailed with $\alpha = 0.05$ is $z_{0.025} = 1.960$. Since $|{-}2.00| = 2.00 > 1.960$, the rejection by the statistic agrees with the p-value. Consistent.

**Source.** [OpenStax Statistics, §9.2, Example 9.3](https://openstax.org/books/statistics/pages/9-2-outcomes-and-the-type-i-and-type-ii-errors) — CC-BY.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="103.2" titulo="Identifying Type I and II errors (conceptual)">

**Problem.** A quality test checks if a batch of medication has an average active ingredient concentration of 50 mg ($H_0$). At the 5% level, the batch is approved or rejected. (a) What constitutes a Type I Error in this context? (b) And a Type II Error? (c) Which is more serious?

**Strategy.** Map formal definitions to the specific context.

**Resolution.**

(a) **Type I Error**: rejecting $H_0$ when $\mu = 50$ mg — that is, rejecting a batch that is actually compliant. Consequence: waste of good product, re-work cost.

(b) **Type II Error**: failing to reject $H_0$ when $\mu \neq 50$ mg — that is, approving a batch out of specification. Consequence: underdosed or overdosed medication reaches the patient.

(c) In most pharmaceutical contexts, the **Type II Error is more serious**: medication out of specification can cause harm to the patient. Therefore, clinical trials use a small $\alpha$ but require high power (low $\beta$), increasing $n$.

**Verification.** The asymmetry between errors justifies calibrating $\alpha$ and $\beta$ differently by context — medicine uses $\alpha = 0.01$ and 80-90% power.

**Source.** [OpenIntro Statistics, §5.2, Example 5.4](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/) — CC-BY-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="103.3" titulo="Power and sample size calculation (intermediate)">

**Problem.** A researcher wants to detect that the average service time at a clinic changed from $\mu_0 = 30$ min to $\mu_1 = 27$ min ($\delta = 3$ min), with $\sigma = 10$ min, $\alpha = 0.05$ (two-tailed) and 80% power. What is the minimum $n$?

**Strategy.** Apply the sample size formula for power: $n = (z_{\alpha/2} + z_\beta)^2 \sigma^2/\delta^2$.

**Resolution.**

$z_{0.025} = 1.960$, $z_{0.20} = 0.842$ (80% power $\Rightarrow \beta = 0.20$).

$$n = \frac{(1.960 + 0.842)^2 \times 10^2}{3^2} = \frac{(2.802)^2 \times 100}{9} = \frac{7.851 \times 100}{9} \approx 87.2$$

Round up: $n = 88$ visits.

**Verification.** If $\delta = 6$ min (effect doubled): $n = 7.851 \times 100/36 \approx 22$. Larger effect requires smaller sample — coherent.

**Source.** [OpenIntro Statistics, §5.3, Example 5.7](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/) — CC-BY-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="103.4" titulo="One-tailed test — medication (intermediate)">

**Problem.** A new anticoagulant claims to reduce the average clotting time from $\mu_0 = 12$ s to below that. A sample of $n = 20$ patients gives $\bar X = 11.2$ s and $s = 2$ s. At level $\alpha = 0.05$, is the medication effective?

**Strategy.** $H_0: \mu \geq 12$, $H_1: \mu < 12$ (left-tailed). t-statistic with 19 degrees of freedom.

**Resolution.**

$$T = \frac{11.2 - 12}{2/\sqrt{20}} = \frac{-0.8}{0.4472} = -1.789$$

For $H_1: \mu < 12$, p-value $= P(t_{19} \leq -1.789)$. From t-table: $P(t_{19} \leq -1.729) = 0.05$ and $P(t_{19} \leq -2.093) = 0.025$. Thus $p \approx 0.045$.

Since $p = 0.045 < 0.05$, **we reject $H_0$**. Evidence that the medication reduces clotting time.

**Verification.** One-tailed critical value: $t_{0.05, 19} = -1.729$. Since $T = -1.789 < -1.729$, the rejection by the statistic agrees. Consistent.

**Source.** [OpenStax Statistics, §9.4, Example 9.8](https://openstax.org/books/statistics/pages/9-4-full-hypothesis-test-examples) — CC-BY.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="103.5" titulo="Misinterpretation of p-value and correction (advanced)">

**Problem.** A researcher obtains $p = 0.03$ in a test of $H_0: \mu = 0$ and states: "There is a 97% probability that the effect is real." Identify the error and formulate the correct interpretation.

**Strategy.** Apply the formal definition of p-value and distinguish probabilities about data from probabilities about hypotheses.

**Resolution.**

The statement is **incorrect** for two reasons:

1. The p-value is the probability about the **data** (given $H_0$), not about the **hypotheses**. $P(\text{data} \mid H_0) \neq P(H_0 \mid \text{data})$ — confusing the two is the **transposition of the conditional fallacy** (base rate neglect).

2. $1 - p\text{-value} = 0.97$ does not have the interpretation of the probability of $H_1$. To obtain $P(H_1 \mid \text{data})$, one would need Bayes' Theorem with a prior over the hypotheses.

**Correct interpretation**: "If $H_0$ were true, there would be only a 3% probability of observing an effect as large (or larger) than the one observed. The data are statistically incompatible with $H_0$ at the 5% level."

**Verification.** Two independent studies with $p = 0.04$ each do not imply a third study with $p = 0.04$ — evidence combination is done by meta-analysis, not by multiplying p-values.

**Source.** [OpenIntro Statistics, §5.1, Section "Interpreting p-values"](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/) — CC-BY-SA.

</Exemplo>
