<Porta nivel="formal" titulo="Rigorous definition">

### Structure: population, sample, and parameters

<Definicao titulo="Population, sample, and estimator">

- **Population** $\mathcal{P}$: the complete set of elements of interest. Its parameters ($\mu$, $\sigma^2$, $p$, …) are **fixed but unknown**.
- **Sample** $X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n$: the observed subset. They are **random variables** before being collected; **realizations** afterwards.
- **Estimator** $\hat\theta = T(X_1, \ldots, X_n)$: a statistic calculated from the sample to approximate $\theta$.

</Definicao>

> "A sample is a subset of the population. A statistic is a number calculated from a sample. Parameters are numbers that summarize data from an entire population." — [OpenStax Statistics, §1.1](https://openstax.org/books/statistics/pages/1-1-definitions-of-statistics-probability-and-key-terms)

### Sampling types

<Definicao titulo="Simple Random Sampling (SRS)">

Every subset of size $n$ from the population has the same probability of being selected. Formally, if the population has $N$ elements, each sample of size $n$ has probability $\binom{N}{n}^{-1}$.

<Equation explicacao="Inclusion probability in SRS: each element has the same chance." latex="\pi_i = \frac{n}{N} \quad \text{for all } i \in \mathcal{P}" />

</Definicao>

<Definicao titulo="Stratified sampling">

The population is divided into $K$ homogeneous strata. SRS is performed within each stratum with size $n_k$.

<Equation explicacao="Stratified mean estimator: weighted average of the means of each stratum." latex="\bar X_{\text{strat}} = \sum_{k=1}^K \frac{N_k}{N} \bar X_k" />

Advantage: reduces variance when strata are more homogeneous than the population.

</Definicao>

<Definicao titulo="Cluster sampling">

The population is grouped into clusters (e.g., neighborhoods, classrooms). A few entire clusters are randomly selected. Useful when the complete population list is unavailable or travel is expensive.

</Definicao>

> "In stratified sampling, the population is divided into groups called strata. A random sample is then selected from each stratum." — [OpenStax Statistics, §1.3](https://openstax.org/books/statistics/pages/1-3-frequency-frequency-tables-and-levels-of-measurement)

### Desirable properties of estimators

<Definicao titulo="Unbiasedness, consistency, and efficiency">

Let $\hat\theta$ be an estimator of $\theta$.

- **Unbiasedness**: $\mathrm{E}[\hat\theta] = \theta$. The estimator does not systematically overestimate or underestimate.
- **Consistency**: $\hat\theta \xrightarrow{P} \theta$ as $n \to \infty$. With more data, it converges to the true value.
- **Efficiency**: in the class of unbiased estimators, $\hat\theta$ has the lowest possible variance (reaching the Cramér-Rao bound).

</Definicao>

### Sampling distribution of the mean

<Teorema titulo="Central Limit Theorem (CLT)">

Let $X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n$ be iid with $\mathrm{E}[X_i] = \mu$ and $\mathrm{Var}(X_i) = \sigma^2 < \infty$. Then:

<Equation explicacao="Standardization of the sample mean converges in distribution to the standard Normal." latex="\frac{\bar X - \mu}{\sigma / \sqrt{n}} \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0,1) \quad \text{as } n \to \infty" />

Equivalently, for large $n$: $\bar X \overset{\text{approx.}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}\!\left(\mu,\, \sigma^2/n\right)$.

**Rule of thumb**: $n \geq 30$ is sufficient for approximately symmetric populations; highly skewed populations require larger $n$.

</Teorema>

<Insight>

The CLT is the central miracle of statistics: the shape of the population distribution (uniform, exponential, bimodal…) does not matter. For sufficiently large $n$, the sample mean is always approximately normally distributed. This is why election polls with 2,000 people can estimate the behavior of 200 million with a 2% error.

</Insight>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

You want to know the favorite ice cream flavor of all the children in school. But the school has **500 children** and you can't ask everyone.

So you **pick 40 children at random** and ask only them. If you pick them fairly—without leaving any group out—their answers will be **very similar to everyone's**.

The more children you ask, the more accurate the answer becomes. This is **sampling**: taking a small piece to understand the whole.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

When you want to know something about **many people** (all citizens, all children in a city), you can't ask everyone. So you use a **sample**—a small group chosen carefully.

**The secret is: how to choose?**

- **Simple random**: pick anyone, like a lottery. Everyone has the same chance.
- **Stratified**: divide into groups (boys/girls, for example) and pick from each group separately. It ensures no group is left out.
- **Cluster**: pick entire neighborhoods and interview everyone in the chosen neighborhood.

**And what happens when you calculate the sample mean?**

The sample mean $\bar X$ won't be **exactly** the mean of everyone, but it will be **close**. And if you repeat it with different samples, the means will be centered around the real mean $\mu$. This is the **Central Limit Theorem**—one of the most powerful ideas in mathematics.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old">

### Why sample?

A census (measuring the entire population) is expensive, time-consuming, and sometimes impossible. Well-done sampling gives you a precise estimate **with a fraction of the effort**.

Real examples:
- Government surveys: use a few hundred thousand households to estimate income, employment, and education for millions of people.
- Polling: 2,000 interviews estimate voting intention with an error of $\pm$ 2%.
- Industrial quality control: 100 tested parts estimate defects in a batch of 100,000.

### Sampling types

| Type | Idea | When to use |
|---|---|---|
| SRS | pick $n$ individuals with equal chance | standard theoretical basis |
| Stratified | pick within homogeneous groups | ensures subgroup representation |
| Systematic | every $k$-th element of the list | ordered list available |
| Cluster | pick entire groups | individual list unavailable or expensive |

### The sampling distribution of $\bar X$

$\bar X$ is a **random variable**: its value changes from sample to sample. But:

- $\mathrm{E}[\bar X] = \mu$ (unbiased: on average it's correct)
- $\mathrm{Var}(\bar X) = \sigma^2/n$ (more observations, more stable)
- Standard error: $\mathrm{SE}(\bar X) = \sigma/\sqrt{n}$

The standard error decreases with $\sqrt{n}$: to reduce the error by half, you need to **quadruple** $n$.

### Margin of error

For 95% confidence: $\mathrm{ME} = 1.96 \cdot \sigma/\sqrt{n}$.

Poll with $n = 2000$ and $\hat p = 0.45$: $\mathrm{ME} = 1.96\sqrt{0.45 \cdot 0.55/2000} \approx 0.022$. Result: 45% $\pm$ 2.2%.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

### Simple random sampling: the formal model

$X_1, \ldots, X_n \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} F$, where $F$ is the unknown population distribution, with $\mathrm{E}[X] = \mu$ and $\mathrm{Var}(X) = \sigma^2$.

### Law of Large Numbers

**Weak (Khinchin)**: $\bar X_n \xrightarrow{P} \mu$ for any $F$ with $\mathrm{E}[|X|] < \infty$.

**Strong (Kolmogorov)**: $\bar X_n \xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}} \mu$ — almost-sure convergence (path by path, except on a set of probability zero).

### Central Limit Theorem

$$\sqrt{n}\,\frac{\bar X_n - \mu}{\sigma} \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0,1)$$

Practical applicability: $n \geq 30$ for symmetric populations; populations with high kurtosis or skewness (like income or asset prices) may require much larger $n$.

### Estimators and their properties

| Property | Formal definition |
|---|---|
| Unbiasedness | $\mathrm{E}[\hat\theta] = \theta$ |
| Consistency | $\hat\theta \xrightarrow{P} \theta$ |
| Efficiency (UMVUE) | $\mathrm{Var}(\hat\theta) = [nI(\theta)]^{-1}$ |
| Sufficiency | $f(\mathbf{x} \mid \theta) = g(T(\mathbf{x}), \theta)\,h(\mathbf{x})$ |

### Cramér-Rao Bound

$$\mathrm{Var}(\hat\theta) \geq \frac{1}{nI(\theta)}, \quad I(\theta) = \mathrm{E}\!\left[\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\log f(X;\theta)\right)^2\right]$$

The maximum likelihood estimator (MLE) asymptotically reaches this bound.

### Sample size for precision $E$

$$n = \left\lceil\frac{z_{\alpha/2}^2\,\sigma^2}{E^2}\right\rceil$$

For proportion (worst case $p = 0.5$): $n = z_{\alpha/2}^2/(4E^2)$. With 95% and $E = 3\%$: $n \approx 1067$.

### Stratified sampling: efficiency gain

Variance of the stratified estimator versus SRS:

$$\mathrm{Var}(\bar X_{\text{strat}}) = \sum_{k=1}^K \left(\frac{N_k}{N}\right)^2 \frac{\sigma_k^2}{n_k} \leq \mathrm{Var}(\bar X_{\text{SRS}})$$

The gain is maximum when strata are internally homogeneous (low intra-stratum variance).

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

### Design-based versus model-based inference

The fundamental dichotomy of modern sampling theory:

- **Design-based** (Neyman, 1934): randomness comes from the selection mechanism. The Horvitz-Thompson estimator $\hat T_{HT} = \sum_{i \in S} Y_i/\pi_i$ is unbiased for any value of $Y$.
- **Model-based** (superpopulation framework): treats $Y_i$ as realizations of a stochastic model; sample randomness is secondary.

### Horvitz-Thompson Estimator

$$\hat T_{HT} = \sum_{i \in S} \frac{Y_i}{\pi_i}, \quad \widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\hat T_{HT}) = \sum_{i \in S}\sum_{j \in S} \frac{\pi_{ij} - \pi_i\pi_j}{\pi_{ij}} \frac{Y_i}{\pi_i}\frac{Y_j}{\pi_j}$$

Allows complex sampling (nested strata, unequal probabilities) while maintaining exact unbiasedness.

### Non-response and calibration

Post-stratification and raking adjust sample weights using auxiliary variables known in the population (census), reducing non-response bias without explicitly modeling the missingness mechanism.

### Concentration of measure

Non-asymptotic Hoeffding bound for $X_i \in [a, b]$:

$$P(\lvert\bar X - \mu\rvert > t) \leq 2\exp\!\left(-\frac{2n\,t^2}{(b-a)^2}\right)$$

Useful in machine learning (PAC learning) and A/B testing analysis with small sample sizes.

### Causal inference and sampling

Randomized Controlled Trials (RCTs) are sampling with random treatment assignment. Joshua Angrist and Guido Imbens received the [2021 Nobel Prize in Economics](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/2021/summary/) for developing quasi-experimental methods (instrumental variables, RDD, differences-in-differences) to extract causal inference when RCTs are unfeasible.

### Bootstrap and variants

- **Non-parametric**: resamples with replacement from $\{X_1, \ldots, X_n\}$, calculates $\hat\theta^*$ in each replicate, estimates variance and intervals.
- **Block bootstrap**: preserves dependency structure in time series (overlapping blocks, stationary bootstrap).
- **Bayesian bootstrap**: replaces resampling with Dirichlet weights, producing a posterior distribution over the functional.

### Sequential statistics

Wald's SPRT (Sequential Probability Ratio Test) stops collection as soon as evidence is sufficient, minimizing expected $n$. In modern A/B testing, *always-valid p-values* (e-values, anytime-valid inference) solve the *peeking* problem without inflating Type I error.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practical application">

### Continuous household surveys: two-stage stratified cluster sampling

Many national statistical offices use **two-stage stratified cluster sampling**:

1. **Stratum**: each administrative region is a primary stratum.
2. **First stage**: Primary Sampling Units (PSUs = census tracts) are selected.
3. **Second stage**: within each selected PSU, households are listed and a fixed fraction is selected.

Result: hundreds of thousands of households covered per quarter, representing millions of people, with a coefficient of variation below 5% for national unemployment rate estimates.

### Quality control: ISO 2859 standards

Industrial product batches are inspected by **attribute sampling**. For a batch of $N = 10,000$ units and an AQL (Acceptable Quality Level) of 1%, the table indicates $n = 200$ pieces; the batch is rejected if more than 5 defective pieces are found.

The standard error of the estimated proportion is:

$$\mathrm{SE}(\hat p) = \sqrt{\frac{\hat p(1-\hat p)}{n}} = \sqrt{\frac{0.025 \cdot 0.975}{200}} \approx 0.011$$

Margin of error of $\pm$ 1.1% at 95% confidence.

### Election polling

Pollsters use quotas (strata by region, gender, age group) plus random selection within each quota. With $n = 2000$, the margin of error for proportions is:

$$\mathrm{ME} = 1.96\sqrt{\frac{0.5 \cdot 0.5}{2000}} \approx 2.2\%$$

</Porta>

## Solved examples

<Exemplo numero="101.1" titulo="Standard error of the sample mean (basic)">

**Problem.** A survey on monthly income collects $n = 64$ workers. The population standard deviation is known: $\sigma = 800$. Calculate the standard error of the sample mean.

**Strategy.** Apply the formula $\mathrm{SE}(\bar X) = \sigma/\sqrt{n}$ directly.

**Resolution.**

$$\mathrm{SE}(\bar X) = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} = \frac{800}{\sqrt{64}} = \frac{800}{8} = 100$$

The standard error is 100.

**Verification.** For $n = 256$ (four times larger): $\mathrm{SE} = 800/16 = 50$. Quadrupling $n$ reduces the standard error by half. Consistent with the relationship $\mathrm{SE} \propto 1/\sqrt{n}$.

**Source.** [OpenStax Statistics, §7.1, Example 7.1](https://openstax.org/books/statistics/pages/7-1-the-central-limit-theorem-for-sample-means-averages) — CC-BY.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="101.2" titulo="Sample size for margin of error (intermediate)">

**Problem.** A researcher wants to estimate the proportion of high school students who work, with a maximum margin of error of 3% at 95% confidence. What is the minimum sample size?

**Strategy.** Use the conservative formula $n = z_{\alpha/2}^2/(4E^2)$ with $p = 0.5$ (worst-case scenario).

**Resolution.**

$$n = \frac{z_{\alpha/2}^2}{4E^2} = \frac{(1.960)^2}{4 \cdot (0.03)^2} = \frac{3.8416}{0.0036} \approx 1068$$

Round **up**: $n = 1068$.

**Verification.** With $n = 1068$ and $p = 0.5$: $\mathrm{ME} = 1.96\sqrt{0.25/1068} = 1.96 \cdot 0.01531 \approx 0.030$. Within the 3% limit.

**Source.** [OpenIntro Statistics, §5.2, Example 5.10](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/) — CC-BY-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="101.3" titulo="CLT application — probability of sample mean (intermediate)">

**Problem.** Service time at a clinic has mean $\mu = 18$ min and standard deviation $\sigma = 6$ min. A sample of $n = 36$ visits is collected. What is the probability that the sample mean is greater than 20 min?

**Strategy.** By the CLT, $\bar X \approx \mathcal{N}(18,\, 36/36) = \mathcal{N}(18, 1)$. Standardize and use the Z-table.

**Resolution.**

$$Z = \frac{\bar X - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}} = \frac{20 - 18}{6/\sqrt{36}} = \frac{2}{1} = 2.00$$

$$P(\bar X > 20) = P(Z > 2.00) = 1 - \Phi(2.00) = 1 - 0.9772 = 0.0228$$

Probability of approximately 2.3%.

**Verification.** The value $\bar X = 20$ is 2 standard deviations above the mean: by the 68-95-99.7 rule, only about 2.5% of sample means should fall above $\mu + 2\,\mathrm{SE}$. The result 2.28% is consistent.

**Source.** [OpenStax Statistics, §7.2, Example 7.3](https://openstax.org/books/statistics/pages/7-2-the-central-limit-theorem-for-sums) — CC-BY.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="101.4" titulo="Identifying sampling bias (conceptual)">

**Problem.** A business magazine conducts an online survey on tax satisfaction, obtaining 80% negative responses. The editor claims: "8 out of 10 business owners are dissatisfied with taxes." Identify the biases present.

**Strategy.** Check each step of the sampling process: who has access, who responds, who is represented.

**Resolution.**

Three biases accumulate:

1. **Selection bias**: online survey excludes business owners without digital access (small rural businesses, informal micro-entrepreneurs).
2. **Non-response bias**: tax issues mobilize those who are dissatisfied more (those who are satisfied feel no urgency to respond).
3. **Sampling frame bias**: magazine readers are larger business owners or those with specific management interests — they do not represent the universe of all "business owners."

**Conclusion**: the 80% estimate systematically overestimates dissatisfaction in the general population.

**Verification.** A probabilistic survey conducted by interviewers would produce a different — and methodologically defensible — result.

**Source.** [OpenIntro Statistics, §1.4, Section "Sampling Bias"](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/) — CC-BY-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="101.5" titulo="Stratified sampling: efficiency calculation (advanced)">

**Problem.** A company has 3 departments: A (200 emp., $\sigma_A = 4$k), B (500 emp., $\sigma_B = 2$k), C (300 emp., $\sigma_C = 6$k). Budget for $n = 100$ interviews. Compare the variance of SRS with proportional stratified sampling to estimate the mean salary.

**Strategy.** Calculate variance of SRS and stratified (proportional allocation).

**Resolution.**

Population variance: $\sigma^2 = \sum (N_k/N)\sigma_k^2$ (approximation — ignores variance between stratum means for simplicity).

Proportional allocation: $n_A = 20$, $n_B = 50$, $n_C = 30$.

$$\mathrm{Var}(\bar X_{\text{strat}}) = \sum_{k} \left(\frac{N_k}{N}\right)^2 \frac{\sigma_k^2}{n_k}$$

$$= \left(\frac{200}{1000}\right)^2\frac{16}{20} + \left(\frac{500}{1000}\right)^2\frac{4}{50} + \left(\frac{300}{1000}\right)^2\frac{36}{30}$$

$$= 0.04 \cdot 0.8 + 0.25 \cdot 0.08 + 0.09 \cdot 1.2 = 0.032 + 0.020 + 0.108 = 0.160$$

Stratified standard error: $\sqrt{0.160} \approx 0.40$k.

For comparison, SRS with $n = 100$ and total approximate variance $\sigma^2 \approx (200 \cdot 16 + 500 \cdot 4 + 300 \cdot 36)/1000 = (3200 + 2000 + 10800)/1000 = 16$ would give $\mathrm{Var}(\bar X_{\text{SRS}}) = 16/100 = 0.160$. In this case, proportional allocation coincides numerically with SRS — the efficiency gain comes when stratum means differ significantly.

**Verification.** The result confirms the theory: with proportional allocation and strata with different variances, stratified is at least as efficient as SRS.

**Source.** [OpenIntro Statistics, §1.4, Exercise 1.35](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/) — CC-BY-SA.

</Exemplo>
