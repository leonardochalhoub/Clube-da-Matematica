<Porta nivel="formal" titulo="Rigorous definition">

### z-test — sigma known

<Definicao titulo="z-test for a mean">

Assumptions: $X_1, \ldots, X_n$ iid, $\sigma$ known, population normal or $n \geq 30$ (CLT).

Under $H_0: \mu = \mu_0$:

<Equation explicacao="z-statistic: follows standard Normal distribution under H0 when sigma is known." latex="Z = \frac{\bar X - \mu_0}{\sigma / \sqrt{n}} \overset{H_0}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0, 1)" />

Practical use: rare, as $\sigma$ is usually unknown. Applied when $\sigma$ comes from long historical calibration.

</Definicao>

### t-test — one sample

<Definicao titulo="Student's t-test (one sample)">

Assumptions: $\sigma$ unknown; population normal or $n \geq 15$ (moderately robust).

<Equation explicacao="One-sample t-statistic: follows the t-distribution with n-1 degrees of freedom under H0 and normality." latex="T = \frac{\bar X - \mu_0}{s / \sqrt{n}} \overset{H_0}{\sim} t_{n-1}" />

For large $n$, $t_{n-1} \to \mathcal{N}(0,1)$.

</Definicao>

> "The Student's t-distribution is appropriate when we use the sample standard deviation $s$ in place of $\sigma$. The heavier tails reflect the additional uncertainty of estimating $\sigma$." — [OpenIntro Statistics, §5.3](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/)

### t-test — two independent samples

<Definicao titulo="Welch t (different variances)">

For $X_{1,i} \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} F_1(\mu_1, \sigma_1^2)$ and $X_{2,j} \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} F_2(\mu_2, \sigma_2^2)$, independent, testing $H_0: \mu_1 = \mu_2$:

<Equation explicacao="Welch statistic: uses the standard deviations of each group separately, without assuming equal variances." latex="T_W = \frac{\bar X_1 - \bar X_2}{\sqrt{s_1^2/n_1 + s_2^2/n_2}} \overset{H_0}{\approx} t_\nu" />

The degrees of freedom $\nu$ are approximated by the Welch-Satterthwaite formula:

<Equation explicacao="Welch-Satterthwaite effective degrees of freedom. Always between min(n1-1, n2-1) and n1+n2-2." latex="\nu = \frac{(s_1^2/n_1 + s_2^2/n_2)^2}{\frac{(s_1^2/n_1)^2}{n_1-1} + \frac{(s_2^2/n_2)^2}{n_2-1}}" />

</Definicao>

<Definicao titulo="Pooled t (equal variances)">

When $\sigma_1^2 = \sigma_2^2 = \sigma^2$ (variances assumed equal):

<Equation explicacao="Pooled variance: combined estimate of the common variance using both groups." latex="s_p^2 = \frac{(n_1 - 1)s_1^2 + (n_2 - 1)s_2^2}{n_1 + n_2 - 2}" />

<Equation explicacao="Pooled t-statistic: exact degrees of freedom n1+n2-2." latex="T_P = \frac{\bar X_1 - \bar X_2}{s_p\sqrt{1/n_1 + 1/n_2}} \overset{H_0}{\sim} t_{n_1 + n_2 - 2}" />

**Recommendation**: use Welch by default, as it is robust even when $\sigma_1 = \sigma_2$. The pooled t is valid only when equality of variances is reasonable.

</Definicao>

### Paired t-test

<Definicao titulo="Paired t-test">

Pairs $(X_{1,i}, X_{2,i})$ from the same individual (or experimental unit) at two moments or conditions. Define $D_i = X_{1,i} - X_{2,i}$.

<Equation explicacao="Reduces the problem to a one-sample t-test on the differences D i." latex="T = \frac{\bar D - \delta_0}{s_D / \sqrt{n}} \overset{H_0: \delta = \delta_0}{\sim} t_{n-1}" />

where $\bar D = \frac{1}{n}\sum D_i$ and $s_D^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum(D_i - \bar D)^2$.

**Advantage**: eliminates variability between individuals, increasing power to detect real effects.

</Definicao>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

You want to know if your class and the neighbor's class have different heights. You measure some children from each class and calculate the averages.

But the heights aren't exactly the same in each class — there is always a little variation. The **t-test** is a way to calculate if the difference between the averages is **big enough** not to be just by chance.

And if you measure the **same child before and after using orthopedic insoles**, you use the **paired** test — because it's the same person at two moments.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

### When to use z or t?

- **z-test**: you know exactly the population standard deviation (very rare!). Uses the standard normal curve.
- **t-test**: you only have the standard deviation calculated from the sample (the common case). The t-curve is a bit "fatter" than the normal — it recognizes that there is more uncertainty.

### One sample versus two samples

- **One sample**: "is my class's average grade different from the national average of 500?"
- **Two independent samples**: "is the average grade of School A different from School B?"
- **Paired**: "did the same 30 students improve after the tutoring?" (before vs. after)

The paired test is "fairer" because it removes the natural variation between people — each person serves as their own control.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old">

### z-test versus t-test — the practical difference

| | z-test | t-test |
|---|---|---|
| $\sigma$ | known | estimated by $s$ |
| Distribution | $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ | $t_{n-1}$ (heavier tails) |
| When to use | rare (calibrated equipment) | almost always |
| For large $n$ | indifferent — $t \to z$ | — |

### Golden rule for two samples

- **Independent groups and presumably different variances**: Welch.
- **Before-and-after in the same individual**: paired.
- **Known equal variances (ex: same production process)**: pooled t.

### Complete example: Welch t

Two Math classes:
- Class A: $n_1 = 15$, $\bar X_1 = 72$, $s_1 = 10$.
- Class B: $n_2 = 18$, $\bar X_2 = 68$, $s_2 = 15$.

$H_0: \mu_1 = \mu_2$, $H_1: \mu_1 \neq \mu_2$, $\alpha = 0.05$.

$$T_W = \frac{72 - 68}{\sqrt{100/15 + 225/18}} = \frac{4}{\sqrt{6.667 + 12.5}} = \frac{4}{\sqrt{19.167}} = \frac{4}{4.378} \approx 0.914$$

Welch degrees of freedom: $\nu \approx 28$ (calculated by formula). Two-tailed critical value: $t_{0.025,\,28} \approx 2.048$. Since $0.914 < 2.048$, we do not reject $H_0$.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

### Why Welch and not pooled by default?

The pooled test assumes $\sigma_1 = \sigma_2$. When this is false, the pooled t has a distorted size (actual Type I error probability) — it can be much larger or smaller than $\alpha$. The Welch test is robust: when variances are equal, it loses little power; when they are different, it is correct. That is why R, Python (scipy), SPSS, and OpenIntro itself recommend Welch as the default.

### Exact distribution vs. Welch-Satterthwaite approximation

The Welch test does not have an exact t-distribution under $H_0$ when $\sigma_1 \neq \sigma_2$ (Behrens-Fisher problem). The Welch-Satterthwaite approximation provides degrees of freedom $\nu$ that interpolate between $\min(n_1-1, n_2-1)$ and $n_1+n_2-2$, with good coverage in practice.

### Application conditions

| Condition | z-test | One-sample t-test | Welch t | Paired |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Minimum $n$ for CLT | 30 | 30 (or normal) | 30 per group (or normal) | 30 (or normal of diffs) |
| Severe outliers | sensitive | sensitive | sensitive | sensitive |
| Non-normal data small $n$ | bad | bad | bad | alternative: Wilcoxon |

### Power of paired vs. independent t-test

The standard error of the paired test is $s_D/\sqrt{n}$, where $s_D^2 = s_1^2 + s_2^2 - 2\rho\,s_1\,s_2$. For positive correlation $\rho > 0$ between pairs, $s_D < \sqrt{s_1^2 + s_2^2}$ — the paired test is more powerful. The gain is maximum when $\rho \to 1$ (highly correlated pairs).

### Non-parametric alternatives

When normality is violated and $n$ is small:

- **Wilcoxon signed-rank**: alternative to one-sample and paired t-test.
- **Mann-Whitney U**: alternative to Welch t for two independent samples.
- Robust to outliers; lose some power when normality is satisfied.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

### The Behrens-Fisher problem and its solutions

The Behrens-Fisher problem (1935/1939): testing $H_0: \mu_1 = \mu_2$ when $\sigma_1, \sigma_2$ are both unknown and possibly different. There is no exact frequentist solution (unlike the $\sigma_1 = \sigma_2$ case). The main approaches:

1. **Welch-Satterthwaite** (1949): approximation of df by a fractional value. Works well in practice; it is the standard in software.
2. **Scheffé's solution** (1943): construction by mixture of distributions. Exact but conservative.
3. **Bayesian approach**: with non-informative priors, produces credible intervals of exact coverage without depending on approximation.

### Analysis of variance as an extension

The two-sample independent t-test is a special case of ANOVA with two groups. For $k$ groups, the ANOVA F-test compares the variance between groups with the variance within groups:

$$F = \frac{\mathrm{SSTrat}/(k-1)}{\mathrm{SSRes}/(N-k)} \overset{H_0}{\sim} F_{k-1,\,N-k}$$

For $k = 2$: $F = T^2$ (pooled) — ANOVA and pooled t are equivalent.

### Tests for equality of variances

Before choosing pooled vs. Welch, one can test $H_0: \sigma_1 = \sigma_2$ with:
- **F-test**: $F = s_1^2/s_2^2 \sim F_{n_1-1, n_2-1}$. Very sensitive to non-normality.
- **Levene**: based on absolute deviations, more robust.
- **Bartlett**: more powerful but sensitive to non-normality.

In practice, this pre-test is not recommended because it has its own variance, creating global error control problems. Prefer Welch always.

### Meta-analysis and effect combination

When multiple studies estimate $\mu_1 - \mu_2$, they are combined by meta-analysis with fixed effect (all study the same $\delta$) or random effect (each study has $\delta_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\delta, \tau^2)$). The DerSimonian-Laird estimator is the standard for random effect. The $I^2$ index measures the proportion of total variance due to heterogeneity between studies.

### Application in finance: abnormal return t-test

In the event study methodology of Fama, Fisher, Jensen, and Roll (1969), one tests whether stock returns around an event are significantly different from zero. The paired test checks $D_i = R_{i,\text{event}} - R_{i,\text{expected}}$ for each company at the event. The distribution of returns is typically non-normal (heavy tails), but with large $n$, the CLT guarantees asymptotic validity.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practical application">

### Before and after: school dropout combat program

An education department implemented intensive attendance monitoring. It measured the attendance rate of 40 at-risk students **before** and **after** 60 days of monitoring.

$H_0: \mu_D = 0$ (no change), $H_1: \mu_D > 0$ (attendance increased).

Results of differences ($D_i = \text{after}_i - \text{before}_i$): $\bar D = +8.5$ pp, $s_D = 12$ pp.

$$T = \frac{8.5}{12/\sqrt{40}} = \frac{8.5}{1.897} = 4.48 \quad (df = 39)$$

$t_{0.05,\,39} = 1.685$ (one-tailed). Since $4.48 > 1.685$, we reject $H_0$: evidence of improvement in attendance.

### Yield comparison between regions: ENEM

INEP wants to verify if public school candidates from the Northeast and South regions have different average grades in Mathematics.

- Northeast: $n_1 = 500$, $\bar X_1 = 512$, $s_1 = 95$.
- South: $n_2 = 400$, $\bar X_2 = 541$, $s_2 = 88$.

Welch t:
$$T_W = \frac{512 - 541}{\sqrt{95^2/500 + 88^2/400}} = \frac{-29}{\sqrt{18.05 + 19.36}} = \frac{-29}{6.116} = -4.74$$

$\nu \approx 858$. $p\text{-value} \approx 0.000003$. Highly significant difference. However, the 29-point difference corresponds to an effect size $d \approx 0.32$ — small to medium by Cohen.

### Quality control: comparison of two suppliers

A pharmaceutical industry compares the purity variability of two excipient suppliers:

- Supplier A: $n_1 = 20$, $s_1 = 0.8$ mg/g.
- Supplier B: $n_2 = 15$, $s_2 = 1.5$ mg/g.

F-test for equality of variances: $F = 1.5^2/0.8^2 = 2.25/0.64 = 3.52$, $df = (14, 19)$. Critical value $F_{0.025,\,14,\,19} \approx 2.86$. Reject $H_0$: different variances. Therefore, Welch t is used to compare the means.

</Porta>

## Solved examples

<Exemplo numero="104.1" titulo="One-sample t-test (basic)">

**Problem.** The waiting time in an emergency room has historically been $\mu_0 = 45$ min. After implementing digital triage, a sample of $n = 25$ visits gave $\bar X = 40$ min and $s = 10$ min. At the 5% level, did the average time decrease?

**Strategy.** $H_0: \mu \geq 45$, $H_1: \mu < 45$ (left-tailed). t-statistic with $df = 24$.

**Resolution.**

$$T = \frac{\bar X - \mu_0}{s/\sqrt{n}} = \frac{40 - 45}{10/\sqrt{25}} = \frac{-5}{2} = -2.50$$

One-tailed critical value: $t_{0.05,\,24} = -1.711$. Since $-2.50 < -1.711$, **we reject $H_0$**.

p-value: $P(t_{24} \leq -2.50) \approx 0.010 < 0.05$. Confirmed.

**Verification.** 95% one-tailed CI: $\mu > 40 - 1.711 \times 2 = 36.58$ min. Since 45 is above the lower limit (but the test is about the superiority of 45 vs the new system, which was rejected), consistent.

**Source.** [OpenStax Statistics, §9.5, Example 9.9](https://openstax.org/books/statistics/pages/9-5-additional-information-and-full-hypothesis-test-examples) — CC-BY.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="104.2" titulo="Welch t — two independent samples (intermediate)">

**Problem.** Compare the systolic blood pressure of two groups of patients:
- Diet group: $n_1 = 12$, $\bar X_1 = 132$ mmHg, $s_1 = 18$ mmHg.
- Medication group: $n_2 = 15$, $\bar X_2 = 125$ mmHg, $s_2 = 10$ mmHg.

At the 5% level, do the means differ?

**Strategy.** $H_0: \mu_1 = \mu_2$, $H_1: \mu_1 \neq \mu_2$ (two-tailed). Use Welch because variances are apparently different ($18^2 = 324$ vs $10^2 = 100$, ratio 3.24).

**Resolution.**

$$\mathrm{SE} = \sqrt{\frac{18^2}{12} + \frac{10^2}{15}} = \sqrt{27 + 6.667} = \sqrt{33.667} = 5.803$$

$$T_W = \frac{132 - 125}{5.803} = \frac{7}{5.803} = 1.206$$

Welch degrees of freedom (approximation): $\nu \approx 17$ (calculated by full formula). Two-tailed critical value: $t_{0.025,\,17} = 2.110$. Since $1.206 < 2.110$, **we do not reject $H_0$**.

**Verification.** p-value $\approx 0.244 > 0.05$. With $n_1 = 12$ and $n_2 = 15$, power is limited to detect 7 mmHg differences. A larger study would be recommended.

**Source.** [OpenIntro Statistics, §5.4, Example 5.12](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/) — CC-BY-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="104.3" titulo="Paired t-test — before and after (intermediate)">

**Problem.** Ten runners had their VO2max measured before and after 8 weeks of interval training. The differences ($D_i = \text{after} - \text{before}$) in mL/kg/min were: 3, 1, 4, 2, 5, 0, 3, 2, 4, 2. At the 5% level, did the training improve VO2max?

**Strategy.** $H_0: \mu_D = 0$, $H_1: \mu_D > 0$ (one-tailed). Calculate $\bar D$ and $s_D$ of the differences.

**Resolution.**

$\sum D_i = 3+1+4+2+5+0+3+2+4+2 = 26$. $\bar D = 26/10 = 2.6$ mL/kg/min.

$\sum D_i^2 = 9+1+16+4+25+0+9+4+16+4 = 88$. $s_D^2 = (88 - 10 \times 2.6^2)/(10-1) = (88 - 67.6)/9 = 20.4/9 = 2.267$. $s_D = 1.506$.

$$T = \frac{\bar D}{s_D/\sqrt{n}} = \frac{2.6}{1.506/\sqrt{10}} = \frac{2.6}{0.4763} = 5.46$$

Critical value $t_{0.05,\,9} = 1.833$. Since $5.46 > 1.833$, **we reject $H_0$**. p-value $< 0.001$.

**Verification.** 95% CI for $\mu_D$: $2.6 \pm 2.262 \times 0.476 = 2.6 \pm 1.077$, i.e., $[1.52; 3.68]$ mL/kg/min. Since the CI does not include 0, consistent with rejecting $H_0$.

**Source.** [OpenIntro Statistics, §5.4, Example 5.14](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/) — CC-BY-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="104.4" titulo="Pooled t versus Welch — when to use each (advanced)">

**Problem.** Two machining processes produce metal parts. Process A: $n_1 = 20$, $\bar X_1 = 50.2$ mm, $s_1 = 0.8$ mm. Process B: $n_2 = 25$, $\bar X_2 = 50.0$ mm, $s_2 = 0.9$ mm. Calculate the pooled and Welch tests. Compare.

**Strategy.** Both test $H_0: \mu_1 = \mu_2$, $H_1: \mu_1 \neq \mu_2$ with $\alpha = 0.05$.

**Resolution.**

**Pooled:**
$s_p^2 = (19 \times 0.64 + 24 \times 0.81)/(20+25-2) = (12.16 + 19.44)/43 = 31.60/43 = 0.735$. $s_p = 0.857$.

$T_P = (50.2 - 50.0)/(0.857\sqrt{1/20+1/25}) = 0.2/(0.857 \times 0.3) = 0.2/0.257 = 0.778$. $df = 43$.

**Welch:**
$\mathrm{SE} = \sqrt{0.64/20 + 0.81/25} = \sqrt{0.032 + 0.0324} = \sqrt{0.0644} = 0.254$.
$T_W = 0.2/0.254 = 0.787$. $\nu \approx 43$ (similar variances).

Both: $T \approx 0.78$, $p \approx 0.44$. We do not reject $H_0$. Both tests agree when variances are similar.

**Verification.** The ratio of variances is $0.81/0.64 = 1.27$ — close to 1. When $s_1 \approx s_2$, pooled and Welch produce practically identical results.

**Source.** [OpenStax Statistics, §10.1, Example 10.1](https://openstax.org/books/statistics/pages/10-1-two-population-means-with-unknown-standard-deviations) — CC-BY.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="104.5" titulo="Choosing the correct test — complete case (advanced)">

**Problem.** A researcher wants to compare the sleep time of adolescents in full-time school versus regular school. She collected 15 adolescents of each school type, independently. Summary data: full-time: $\bar X_1 = 7.2$ h, $s_1 = 1.4$ h; regular: $\bar X_2 = 8.1$ h, $s_2 = 0.8$ h. At the 5% level, does sleep differ? Use the appropriate test.

**Strategy.** Independent groups, apparently different variances ($s_1/s_2 = 1.75$). Use Welch t.

**Resolution.**

$H_0: \mu_1 = \mu_2$, $H_1: \mu_1 \neq \mu_2$ (two-tailed).

$\mathrm{SE} = \sqrt{1.4^2/15 + 0.8^2/15} = \sqrt{1.96/15 + 0.64/15} = \sqrt{0.1307 + 0.04267} = \sqrt{0.1733} = 0.4163$.

$T_W = (7.2 - 8.1)/0.4163 = -0.9/0.4163 = -2.162$.

Welch degrees of freedom (approximation): $\nu \approx 22$. Critical value: $t_{0.025,\,22} = 2.074$.

Since $\lvert -2.162 \rvert = 2.162 > 2.074$, **we reject $H_0$** at the 5% level. p-value $\approx 0.042$.

Cohen's effect size: $d = 0.9/\sqrt{(1.4^2 + 0.8^2)/2} = 0.9/\sqrt{1.3} = 0.9/1.14 = 0.79$ — large effect.

**Verification.** 95% CI for $\mu_1 - \mu_2$: $-0.9 \pm 2.074 \times 0.4163 = -0.9 \pm 0.863$, i.e., $[-1.763; -0.037]$. Since it does not include 0, consistent with rejecting $H_0$.

**Source.** [OpenIntro Statistics, §5.4, Exercise 5.17](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/) — CC-BY-SA.

</Exemplo>
