<Porta nivel="formal" titulo="Rigorous definition">

### z test — sigma known

<Definicao titulo="z test for one mean">

Assumptions: $X_1, \ldots, X_n$ iid, $\sigma$ known, population normal or $n \geq 30$ (CLT).

Under $H_0: \mu = \mu_0$:

<Equation explicacao="z statistic: follows standard Normal distribution under H0 when sigma is known." latex="Z = \frac{\bar X - \mu_0}{\sigma / \sqrt{n}} \overset{H_0}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0, 1)" />

Practical use: rare, since $\sigma$ is generally unknown. Applied when $\sigma$ comes from long historical calibration.

</Definicao>

### t test — one sample

<Definicao titulo="Student's t test (one sample)">

Assumptions: $\sigma$ unknown; population normal or $n \geq 15$ (moderately robust).

<Equation explicacao="One-sample t statistic: follows t distribution with n-1 degrees of freedom under H0 and normality." latex="T = \frac{\bar X - \mu_0}{s / \sqrt{n}} \overset{H_0}{\sim} t_{n-1}" />

For large $n$, $t_{n-1} \to \mathcal{N}(0,1)$.

</Definicao>

> "Student's t distribution is appropriate when we use the sample standard deviation $s$ instead of $\sigma$. The heavier tails reflect the additional uncertainty of estimating $\sigma$." — [OpenIntro Statistics, §5.3](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/)

### t test — two independent samples

<Definicao titulo="Welch t (unequal variances)">

For $X_{1,i} \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} F_1(\mu_1, \sigma_1^2)$ and $X_{2,j} \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} F_2(\mu_2, \sigma_2^2)$, independent, testing $H_0: \mu_1 = \mu_2$:

<Equation explicacao="Welch statistic: uses deviations from each group separately, without assuming equal variances." latex="T_W = \frac{\bar X_1 - \bar X_2}{\sqrt{s_1^2/n_1 + s_2^2/n_2}} \overset{H_0}{\approx} t_\nu" />

The degrees of freedom $\nu$ are approximated by the Welch-Satterthwaite formula:

<Equation explicacao="Effective Welch-Satterthwaite degrees of freedom. Always between min(n1-1, n2-1) and n1+n2-2." latex="\nu = \frac{(s_1^2/n_1 + s_2^2/n_2)^2}{\frac{(s_1^2/n_1)^2}{n_1-1} + \frac{(s_2^2/n_2)^2}{n_2-1}}" />

</Definicao>

<Definicao titulo="Pooled t (equal variances)">

When $\sigma_1^2 = \sigma_2^2 = \sigma^2$ (variances assumed equal):

<Equation explicacao="Pooled variance: combined estimate of common variance using both groups." latex="s_p^2 = \frac{(n_1 - 1)s_1^2 + (n_2 - 1)s_2^2}{n_1 + n_2 - 2}" />

<Equation explicacao="Pooled t statistic: exact degrees of freedom n1+n2-2." latex="T_P = \frac{\bar X_1 - \bar X_2}{s_p\sqrt{1/n_1 + 1/n_2}} \overset{H_0}{\sim} t_{n_1 + n_2 - 2}" />

**Recommendation**: use Welch by default, as it is robust even when $\sigma_1 = \sigma_2$. Pooled t is valid only when equality of variances is reasonable.

</Definicao>

### Paired t test

<Definicao titulo="Paired t test">

Pairs $(X_{1,i}, X_{2,i})$ from the same individual (or experimental unit) at two times or under two conditions. Define $D_i = X_{1,i} - X_{2,i}$.

<Equation explicacao="Reduces the problem to a one-sample t test on the differences Di." latex="T = \frac{\bar D - \delta_0}{s_D / \sqrt{n}} \overset{H_0: \delta = \delta_0}{\sim} t_{n-1}" />

where $\bar D = \frac{1}{n}\sum D_i$ and $s_D^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum(D_i - \bar D)^2$.

**Advantage**: eliminates variability between individuals, increasing power to detect real effects.

</Definicao>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

You want to know if your class and your neighbor's class have different heights. You measure a few children from each class and calculate the averages.

But heights are not exactly the same in each class — there is always some variation. The **t test** is a way to calculate whether the difference between the averages is **large enough** to not just be by chance.

And if you measure the **same child before and after using orthopaedic insoles**, you use the **paired** test — because it is the same person at both times.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

### When to use z or t?

- **z test**: you know exactly the standard deviation of the population (very rare!). Uses the standard normal curve.
- **t test**: you only have the calculated standard deviation from the sample (the common case). The t curve is a bit "fatter" than normal — it recognizes that there is more uncertainty.

### One sample versus two samples

- **One sample**: "is the average grade of my class different from the national average of 500?"
- **Two independent samples**: "is the average grade at School A different from School B?"
- **Paired**: "did the same 30 students improve after tutoring?" (before vs. after)

The paired test is more "fair" because it removes natural variation between people — each person serves as their own control.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old teenager">

### z test versus t test — the practical difference

| | z test | t test |
|---|---|---|
| $\sigma$ | known | estimated by $s$ |
| Distribution | $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ | $t_{n-1}$ (heavier tails) |
| When to use | rare (calibrated equipment) | almost always |
| For large $n$ | indifferent — $t \to z$ | — |

### Golden rule for two samples

- **Independent groups with presumably different variances**: Welch.
- **Before-and-after on the same individual**: paired.
- **Variances known to be equal (e.g. same production process)**: pooled t.

### Complete example: Welch t

Two math classes:
- Class A: $n_1 = 15$, $\bar X_1 = 72$, $s_1 = 10$.
- Class B: $n_2 = 18$, $\bar X_2 = 68$, $s_2 = 15$.

$H_0: \mu_1 = \mu_2$, $H_1: \mu_1 \neq \mu_2$, $\alpha = 0.05$.

$$T_W = \frac{72 - 68}{\sqrt{100/15 + 225/18}} = \frac{4}{\sqrt{6.667 + 12.5}} = \frac{4}{\sqrt{19.167}} = \frac{4}{4.378} \approx 0.914$$

Welch degrees of freedom: $\nu \approx 28$ (computed by formula). Two-sided critical value: $t_{0.025,\,28} \approx 2.048$. Since $0.914 < 2.048$, we fail to reject $H_0$.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

### Why Welch and not pooled by default?

The pooled test assumes $\sigma_1 = \sigma_2$. When this is false, the pooled t has distorted size (real Type I error probability) — it can be much larger or smaller than $\alpha$. Welch's test is robust: when variances are equal, it loses little power; when they differ, it is correct. This is why R, Python (scipy), SPSS, and OpenIntro itself recommend Welch as the default.

### Exact distribution vs. Welch-Satterthwaite approximation

Welch's test does not have exact $t$ distribution under $H_0$ when $\sigma_1 \neq \sigma_2$ (the Behrens-Fisher problem). The Welch-Satterthwaite approximation provides degrees of freedom $\nu$ that interpolate between $\min(n_1-1, n_2-1)$ and $n_1+n_2-2$, with good empirical coverage.

### Application conditions

| Condition | z test | One-sample t | Welch t | Paired |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Minimum $n$ for CLT | 30 | 30 (or normal) | 30 per group (or normal) | 30 (or normal of diffs) |
| Severe outliers | sensitive | sensitive | sensitive | sensitive |
| Non-normal data, small $n$ | poor | poor | poor | alternative: Wilcoxon |

### Power of paired t versus independent t

The standard error of the paired test is $s_D/\sqrt{n}$, where $s_D^2 = s_1^2 + s_2^2 - 2\rho\,s_1\,s_2$. For positive correlation $\rho > 0$ between pairs, $s_D < \sqrt{s_1^2 + s_2^2}$ — the paired test is more powerful. The gain is maximum when $\rho \to 1$ (highly correlated pairs).

### Non-parametric alternatives

When normality is violated and $n$ is small:

- **Wilcoxon signed-rank**: alternative to one-sample t and paired t.
- **Mann-Whitney U**: alternative to Welch t for two independent samples.
- Robust to outliers; lose some power when normality is satisfied.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

### The Behrens-Fisher problem and its solutions

The Behrens-Fisher problem (1935/1939): test $H_0: \mu_1 = \mu_2$ when $\sigma_1, \sigma_2$ are both unknown and possibly different. There is no exact frequentist solution (unlike the $\sigma_1 = \sigma_2$ case). The main approaches:

1. **Welch-Satterthwaite** (1949): approximation of df by a fractional value. Works well in practice; is the standard in software.
2. **Scheffé's solution** (1943): construction via mixture of distributions. Exact but conservative.
3. **Bayesian approach**: with non-informative priors, produces credible intervals with exact coverage without depending on approximation.

### Analysis of variance as extension

The two-sample t test is a special case of ANOVA with two groups. For $k$ groups, the ANOVA F-test compares the variance between groups with variance within groups:

$$F = \frac{\mathrm{SSTreatment}/(k-1)}{\mathrm{SSResidual}/(N-k)} \overset{H_0}{\sim} F_{k-1,\,N-k}$$

For $k = 2$: $F = T^2$ (pooled) — ANOVA and pooled t are equivalent.

### Tests for equality of variances

Before choosing pooled vs. Welch, one can test $H_0: \sigma_1 = \sigma_2$ with:
- **F test**: $F = s_1^2/s_2^2 \sim F_{n_1-1, n_2-1}$. Very sensitive to non-normality.
- **Levene**: based on absolute deviations, more robust.
- **Bartlett**: more powerful but sensitive to non-normality.

In practice, this pre-test is not recommended because it itself has variance, creating problems in controlling overall error. Prefer Welch always.

### Meta-analysis and combining effects

When multiple studies estimate $\mu_1 - \mu_2$, combine via meta-analysis with fixed effect (all studies the same $\delta$) or random effect (each study has $\delta_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\delta, \tau^2)$). DerSimonian-Laird estimator is standard for random effects. The $I^2$ index measures the proportion of total variance due to between-study heterogeneity.

### Application in finance: t test of abnormal returns

In the event study methodology of Fama, Fisher, Jensen, and Roll (1969), one tests whether returns of a stock around an event are significantly different from zero. The paired test evaluates $D_i = R_{i,\text{event}} - R_{i,\text{expected}}$ for each firm during the event. Return distributions are typically non-normal (heavy tails), but with large $n$ the CLT guarantees asymptotic validity.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Brazilian practical application">

### Before and after: school dropout prevention program

An education authority implemented intensive attendance monitoring. It measured attendance rates of 40 at-risk students **before** and **after** 60 days of monitoring.

$H_0: \mu_D = 0$ (no change), $H_1: \mu_D > 0$ (attendance increased).

Results of differences ($D_i = \text{after}_i - \text{before}_i$): $\bar D = +8.5$ pp, $s_D = 12$ pp.

$$T = \frac{8.5}{12/\sqrt{40}} = \frac{8.5}{1.897} = 4.48 \quad (df = 39)$$

$t_{0.05,\,39} = 1.685$ (one-sided). Since $4.48 > 1.685$, we reject $H_0$: evidence of attendance improvement.

### Comparing achievement across regions: ENEM

The INEP wants to verify whether students from public schools in the Northeast and South regions have different average math scores.

- Northeast: $n_1 = 500$, $\bar X_1 = 512$, $s_1 = 95$.
- South: $n_2 = 400$, $\bar X_2 = 541$, $s_2 = 88$.

Welch t:
$$T_W = \frac{512 - 541}{\sqrt{95^2/500 + 88^2/400}} = \frac{-29}{\sqrt{18.05 + 19.36}} = \frac{-29}{6.116} = -4.74$$

$\nu \approx 858$. $p\text{-value} \approx 0.000003$. Highly significant difference. However, the difference of 29 points corresponds to effect size $d \approx 0.32$ — small to medium by Cohen's standards.

### Quality control: comparing two suppliers

A pharmaceutical company compares the variability in purity of an excipient from two suppliers:

- Supplier A: $n_1 = 20$, $s_1 = 0.8$ mg/g.
- Supplier B: $n_2 = 15$, $s_2 = 1.5$ mg/g.

F test for equality of variances: $F = 1.5^2/0.8^2 = 2.25/0.64 = 3.52$, $df = (14, 19)$. Critical value $F_{0.025,\,14,\,19} \approx 2.86$. Reject $H_0$: variances are different. Therefore, use Welch t to compare the means.

</Porta>

## Worked examples

<Exemplo numero="104.1" titulo="One-sample t test (basic)">

**Problem.** Wait time at an emergency room was historically $\mu_0 = 45$ min. After implementing digital triage, a sample of $n = 25$ visits gave $\bar X = 40$ min and $s = 10$ min. At the 5% level, has the mean time decreased?

**Strategy.** $H_0: \mu \geq 45$, $H_1: \mu < 45$ (left-tailed). t statistic with $df = 24$.

**Solution.**

$$T = \frac{\bar X - \mu_0}{s/\sqrt{n}} = \frac{40 - 45}{10/\sqrt{25}} = \frac{-5}{2} = -2.50$$

Left-tailed critical value: $t_{0.05,\,24} = -1.711$. Since $-2.50 < -1.711$, **we reject $H_0$**.

p-value: $P(t_{24} \leq -2.50) \approx 0.010 < 0.05$. Confirmed.

**Verification.** 95% one-sided CI: $\mu > 40 - 1.711 \times 2 = 36.58$ min. Since 45 is above the lower bound (but the test is about the superiority of 45 vs. the new system, which was rejected), consistent.

**Source.** [OpenStax Statistics, §9.5, Example 9.9](https://openstax.org/books/statistics/pages/9-5-additional-information-and-full-hypothesis-test-examples) — CC-BY.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="104.2" titulo="Welch t — two independent samples (intermediate)">

**Problem.** Compare systolic blood pressure between two patient groups:
- Diet group: $n_1 = 12$, $\bar X_1 = 132$ mmHg, $s_1 = 18$ mmHg.
- Medication group: $n_2 = 15$, $\bar X_2 = 125$ mmHg, $s_2 = 10$ mmHg.

At the 5% level, do the means differ?

**Strategy.** $H_0: \mu_1 = \mu_2$, $H_1: \mu_1 \neq \mu_2$ (two-sided). Use Welch because variances appear different ($18^2 = 324$ vs. $10^2 = 100$, ratio 3.24).

**Solution.**

$$\mathrm{SE} = \sqrt{\frac{18^2}{12} + \frac{10^2}{15}} = \sqrt{27 + 6.667} = \sqrt{33.667} = 5.803$$

$$T_W = \frac{132 - 125}{5.803} = \frac{7}{5.803} = 1.206$$

Welch degrees of freedom (approximation): $\nu \approx 17$ (by formula). Two-sided critical value: $t_{0.025,\,17} = 2.110$. Since $1.206 < 2.110$, **we fail to reject $H_0$**.

**Verification.** p-value $\approx 0.244 > 0.05$. With $n_1 = 12$ and $n_2 = 15$, power is limited to detect 7 mmHg differences. A larger study would be recommended.

**Source.** [OpenIntro Statistics, §5.4, Example 5.12](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/) — CC-BY-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="104.3" titulo="Paired t test — before and after (intermediate)">

**Problem.** Ten runners had VO2max measured before and after 8 weeks of interval training. The differences ($D_i = \text{after} - \text{before}$) in mL/kg/min were: 3, 1, 4, 2, 5, 0, 3, 2, 4, 2. At the 5% level, did training improve VO2max?

**Strategy.** $H_0: \mu_D = 0$, $H_1: \mu_D > 0$ (right-tailed). Calculate $\bar D$ and $s_D$ from the differences.

**Solution.**

$\sum D_i = 3+1+4+2+5+0+3+2+4+2 = 26$. $\bar D = 26/10 = 2.6$ mL/kg/min.

$\sum D_i^2 = 9+1+16+4+25+0+9+4+16+4 = 88$. $s_D^2 = (88 - 10 \times 2.6^2)/(10-1) = (88 - 67.6)/9 = 20.4/9 = 2.267$. $s_D = 1.506$.

$$T = \frac{\bar D}{s_D/\sqrt{n}} = \frac{2.6}{1.506/\sqrt{10}} = \frac{2.6}{0.4763} = 5.46$$

Critical value $t_{0.05,\,9} = 1.833$. Since $5.46 > 1.833$, **we reject $H_0$**. p-value $< 0.001$.

**Verification.** 95% CI for $\mu_D$: $2.6 \pm 2.262 \times 0.476 = 2.6 \pm 1.077$, or $[1.52;\; 3.68]$ mL/kg/min. Since the CI does not include 0, consistent with rejection of $H_0$.

**Source.** [OpenIntro Statistics, §5.4, Example 5.14](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/) — CC-BY-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="104.4" titulo="Pooled t versus Welch — when to use each (advanced)">

**Problem.** Two machining processes produce metal parts. Process A: $n_1 = 20$, $\bar X_1 = 50.2$ mm, $s_1 = 0.8$ mm. Process B: $n_2 = 25$, $\bar X_2 = 50.0$ mm, $s_2 = 0.9$ mm. Compute the pooled and Welch tests. Compare.

**Strategy.** Both test $H_0: \mu_1 = \mu_2$, $H_1: \mu_1 \neq \mu_2$ with $\alpha = 0.05$.

**Solution.**

**Pooled:**
$s_p^2 = (19 \times 0.64 + 24 \times 0.81)/(20+25-2) = (12.16 + 19.44)/43 = 31.60/43 = 0.735$. $s_p = 0.857$.

$T_P = (50.2 - 50.0)/(0.857\sqrt{1/20+1/25}) = 0.2/(0.857 \times 0.3) = 0.2/0.257 = 0.778$. $df = 43$.

**Welch:**
$\mathrm{SE} = \sqrt{0.64/20 + 0.81/25} = \sqrt{0.032 + 0.0324} = \sqrt{0.0644} = 0.254$.
$T_W = 0.2/0.254 = 0.787$. $\nu \approx 43$ (similar variances).

Both: $T \approx 0.78$, $p \approx 0.44$. We fail to reject $H_0$. The two tests agree when variances are similar.

**Verification.** The variance ratio is $0.81/0.64 = 1.27$ — close to 1. When $s_1 \approx s_2$, pooled and Welch produce practically identical results.

**Source.** [OpenStax Statistics, §10.1, Example 10.1](https://openstax.org/books/statistics/pages/10-1-two-population-means-with-unknown-standard-deviations) — CC-BY.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="104.5" titulo="Choosing the right test — complete case (advanced)">

**Problem.** A researcher wants to compare sleep time of adolescents in full-time versus regular school. She collected 15 adolescents from each school type, independently. Summary data: full-time: $\bar X_1 = 7.2$ h, $s_1 = 1.4$ h; regular: $\bar X_2 = 8.1$ h, $s_2 = 0.8$ h. At the 5% level, does sleep differ? Use the appropriate test.

**Strategy.** Independent groups, variances apparently different ($s_1/s_2 = 1.75$). Use Welch t.

**Solution.**

$H_0: \mu_1 = \mu_2$, $H_1: \mu_1 \neq \mu_2$ (two-sided).

$\mathrm{SE} = \sqrt{1.4^2/15 + 0.8^2/15} = \sqrt{1.96/15 + 0.64/15} = \sqrt{0.1307 + 0.04267} = \sqrt{0.1733} = 0.4163$.

$T_W = (7.2 - 8.1)/0.4163 = -0.9/0.4163 = -2.162$.

Welch degrees of freedom (approximation): $\nu \approx 22$. Critical value: $t_{0.025,\,22} = 2.074$.

Since $\lvert -2.162 \rvert = 2.162 > 2.074$, **we reject $H_0$** at the 5% level. p-value $\approx 0.042$.

Cohen's effect size: $d = 0.9/\sqrt{(1.4^2 + 0.8^2)/2} = 0.9/\sqrt{1.3} = 0.9/1.14 = 0.79$ — large effect.

**Verification.** 95% CI for $\mu_1 - \mu_2$: $-0.9 \pm 2.074 \times 0.4163 = -0.9 \pm 0.863$, or $[-1.763;\; -0.037]$. Since it does not include 0, consistent with rejection of $H_0$.

**Source.** [OpenIntro Statistics, §5.4, Exercise 5.17](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/) — CC-BY-SA.

</Exemplo>
