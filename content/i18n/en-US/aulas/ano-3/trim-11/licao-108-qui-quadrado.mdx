### Chi-squared distribution

<Definicao titulo="Chi-squared distribution with k degrees of freedom">

Let $Z_1, Z_2, \ldots, Z_k$ be independent random variables with distribution $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$. Define:

$$X = Z_1^2 + Z_2^2 + \cdots + Z_k^2$$

The variable $X$ has **chi-squared distribution with $k$ degrees of freedom**, written $X \sim \chi^2_k$. Properties:

- $\mathbb{E}[X] = k$ and $\text{Var}[X] = 2k$.
- The density is $f(x) = \frac{x^{k/2-1}e^{-x/2}}{2^{k/2}\Gamma(k/2)}$ for $x > 0$.
- For $k$ large, $\chi^2_k \approx \mathcal{N}(k, 2k)$ (CLT).

</Definicao>

> "Chi-squared distributions have an additivity property: if $X_1 \sim \chi^2_{n_1}$ and $X_2 \sim \chi^2_{n_2}$ are independent, then $X_1 + X_2 \sim \chi^2_{n_1 + n_2}$." — [OpenStax Statistics, §11.1](https://openstax.org/books/statistics/pages/11-1-facts-about-the-chi-square-distribution)

### Goodness-of-fit test

<Definicao titulo="Hypotheses and goodness-of-fit statistic">

Given a sample of $n$ observations categorized into $k$ classes:

**Hypotheses:**
$$H_0: p_i = p_i^0 \quad (i = 1, \ldots, k) \qquad \text{vs.} \qquad H_1: \text{at least one } p_i \neq p_i^0$$

**Expected frequencies:** $E_i = n \, p_i^0$.

**Statistic:**
$$\chi^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \frac{(O_i - E_i)^2}{E_i}$$

**Degrees of freedom:** $df = k - 1 - r$, where $r$ is the number of parameters estimated from the data (typically $r = 0$ for fully specified distributions).

Under $H_0$, $\chi^2 \xrightarrow{d} \chi^2_{df}$ as $n \to \infty$ (Pearson's asymptotic result, 1900).

</Definicao>

### Independence test in $r \times c$ table

<Definicao titulo="Expected frequencies and statistic in contingency table">

For a table with $r$ rows and $c$ columns, total $n$, row $i$ total equal to $R_i$, column $j$ total equal to $C_j$:

**Hypotheses:**
$$H_0: P(A_i \cap B_j) = P(A_i)\,P(B_j) \quad \forall\, i, j \qquad \text{(independence)}$$

**Expected frequencies under $H_0$:**
$$E_{ij} = \frac{R_i \cdot C_j}{n}$$

**Statistic:**
$$\chi^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{r} \sum_{j=1}^{c} \frac{(O_{ij} - E_{ij})^2}{E_{ij}}$$

**Degrees of freedom:** $df = (r-1)(c-1)$.

</Definicao>

> "Expected frequencies for an independence test are calculated assuming that the row proportions are equal across all columns. If the null hypothesis is true (variables independent), this assumption is satisfied." — [OpenIntro Statistics, §6.4](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/)

### Assumptions for validity (Cochran's rule)

<Definicao titulo="Conditions for applicability">

The chi-squared test (goodness of fit or independence) is valid when:

1. **Independence** of observations (simple random sampling or experiment with independent units).
2. **Minimum cell size:** $E_i \geq 5$ in all cells — or, in Cochran's relaxed version, at least 80% of cells with $E_i \geq 5$ and none with $E_i < 1$.
3. **Mutually exclusive and exhaustive categories** (each observation belongs to exactly one cell).

When condition 2 fails in $2 \times 2$ tables: use the **Fisher's Exact Test**.

</Definicao>

### Yates correction ($2 \times 2$ table)

<Teorema titulo="Yates continuity correction">

For $2 \times 2$ tables with $E_{ij}$ close to 5, the discrete–continuous approximation can be improved with:

$$\chi^2_Y = \sum_{i,j} \frac{\bigl(\lvert O_{ij} - E_{ij} \rvert - 0,5\bigr)^2}{E_{ij}}$$

The correction is conservative: it slightly reduces $\chi^2$, increasing the p-value. Its adoption is debated — many statisticians prefer Fisher's Exact Test for small samples.

</Teorema>

### Effect size: Cramér's V

<Definicao titulo="Cramer's V">

Cramér's V normalizes $\chi^2$ to a scale $[0, 1]$ independent of $n$:

$$V = \sqrt{\frac{\chi^2}{n \cdot \min(r-1,\, c-1)}}$$

Conventional interpretation (Cohen):

- $V \approx 0,1$: small effect
- $V \approx 0,3$: medium effect
- $V \geq 0,5$: large effect

</Definicao>

<Insight>

**Why does the statistic have this form?** Each term $(O_i - E_i)^2 / E_i$ is proportional to $Z_i^2$ where $Z_i = (O_i - E_i)/\sqrt{E_i}$ is a standardization of the discrepancy in category $i$. The sum of squares of standard normals converges to $\chi^2$ — the constraint "sum of $O_i$ = $n$" reduces the degrees of freedom by 1, hence $df = k - 1$.

</Insight>

Imagine you have a bag with **six colored balls** — two red, two blue, and two yellow — and your brother says that all colors come out with equal chance.

You draw 60 balls and count: 25 red, 10 blue, and 25 yellow.

That's very different from what you expected (20 of each)! The chi-squared test is like a **toy judge**: it looks at the difference between what you expected and what actually came out and says: "Was that just bad luck, or is something wrong with the bag?"

If the difference is too big to be just luck, the judge bangs the gavel: **the bag is broken**.

### Two uses of the chi-squared test

**1. Goodness of fit** — do the data match the theory?

You roll a die 60 times and count how many times each face comes up. If the die is fair, you'd expect 10 of each. If face 6 came up 15 times and face 1 only 5 times, is the die loaded?

The test sums $(O - E)^2 / E$ for each face. If the sum is too large, we conclude the die is probably loaded.

**2. Independence** — are two characteristics related?

I survey 200 people about team preference (A, B, C) and region (North, South). Are sports preferences proportional in each region, or does region influence the team?

The test makes a table with observed counts and calculates what we'd expect if there were no relationship. The difference becomes the chi-squared statistic.

**Degrees of freedom:** for $k$ categories, $df = k - 1$. For table $r \times c$, $df = (r-1)(c-1)$.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old teen">

### The central idea

When we observe $k$ categories in a sample of size $n$, each frequency $O_i$ oscillates around $E_i = n p_i$. The expected oscillation of $O_i$ is on the order of $\sqrt{E_i}$ (standard deviation of a binomial). Therefore $(O_i - E_i)/\sqrt{E_i} \approx Z_i$ is approximately standard normal. The sum $\sum Z_i^2$ approximates $\chi^2$ — and that is precisely the test statistic.

### Execution flowchart

1. **Define $H_0$** and expected proportions $p_i^0$ (goodness of fit) or assume independence (table).
2. **Calculate $E_i$**: for goodness of fit, $E_i = n p_i^0$; for independence, $E_{ij} = R_i C_j / n$.
3. **Check** $E_i \geq 5$ in all cells.
4. **Compute** $\chi^2 = \sum (O_i - E_i)^2 / E_i$.
5. **Determine $df$**: $k - 1$ (goodness of fit without estimated parameters) or $(r-1)(c-1)$ (independence).
6. **Compare** with critical value $\chi^2_{\alpha; df}$ or calculate the p-value.
7. If $\chi^2 > \chi^2_{\alpha; df}$, reject $H_0$.

### Quick example: loaded die?

Roll a die 120 times. Observe: 15, 22, 17, 25, 19, 22.

$E_i = 120/6 = 20$ each. $df = 6 - 1 = 5$.

$$\chi^2 = \frac{(15-20)^2}{20} + \frac{(22-20)^2}{20} + \frac{(17-20)^2}{20} + \frac{(25-20)^2}{20} + \frac{(19-20)^2}{20} + \frac{(22-20)^2}{20}$$

$$= \frac{25 + 4 + 9 + 25 + 1 + 4}{20} = \frac{68}{20} = 3,40$$

Critical value at 5%: $\chi^2_{0,05;\,5} = 11,07$. Since $3,40 < 11,07$, **we do not reject** $H_0$ — no evidence of loading.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

### Asymptotic derivation

Under $H_0$, the vector of counts $(O_1, \ldots, O_k)$ follows Multinomial$(n, p^0)$. The covariance matrix of the vector $\mathbf{O}/n$ is $\Sigma = \text{diag}(p^0) - p^0 (p^0)^\top$. After standardization, the multivariate CLT guarantees that $\sqrt{n}(O/n - p^0) \to \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \Sigma)$. Pearson's statistic converges to the quadratic form $\chi^2_{k-1}$ by the theory of quadratic forms in normals with linear constraint (Cochran's Theorem).

### Degrees of freedom — linear intuition

The constraint $\sum O_i = n$ eliminates 1 degree of freedom: the residuals vector $(O_i - E_i)$ lives in a subspace of dimension $k - 1$. For goodness of fit with $r$ parameters estimated via MLE: each estimated parameter reduces $df$ by 1 additionally, so $df = k - 1 - r$.

For $r \times c$ table: the $rc$ values $O_{ij}$ have $r + c - 1$ constraints (fixed margins), so $df = rc - (r + c - 1) = (r-1)(c-1)$.

### $G^2$ statistic (likelihood ratio)

Asymptotically equivalent alternative:

$$G^2 = 2 \sum_{i} O_i \ln\!\left(\frac{O_i}{E_i}\right)$$

For $n$ large, $G^2 \approx \chi^2$ with the same $df$. In log-linear models (Poisson GLM with log link), $G^2$ is the residual deviance, preferred for its KL divergence interpretation.

### Standardized residuals

To diagnose which cells contribute most to rejection:

$$r_{ij} = \frac{O_{ij} - E_{ij}}{\sqrt{E_{ij}\,(1 - R_i/n)(1 - C_j/n)}}$$

Cells with $\lvert r_{ij} \rvert > 1,96$ are suspect at the 5% level (apply Bonferroni correction if many cells).

### Python implementation

```python
from scipy.stats import chisquare, chi2_contingency
import numpy as np

# Goodness of fit
chi2, p = chisquare(f_obs=[15, 22, 17, 25, 19, 22])

# Independence
table = [[80, 20], [60, 40]]
chi2, p, dof, expected = chi2_contingency(table, correction=False)

# Cramér's V
n = np.array(table).sum()
v = np.sqrt(chi2 / (n * (min(np.array(table).shape) - 1)))
```

### Historical context

Karl Pearson (1900) proposed the $\chi^2$ statistic as a solution to the problem of measuring discrepancies between observed and theoretical frequencies, establishing the foundation of modern hypothesis testing. The asymptotic result is valid for $n \to \infty$; for finite samples, the question of how good the approximation is was debated for decades — Fisher (1922) corrected Pearson's degrees of freedom for the estimated parameters case, generating one of the most celebrated conflicts in 20th-century statistics.

### Log-linear models

The contingency table is a special case of **Poisson GLM** with logarithmic link. For $r \times c$ table, the independence model is:

$$\log E_{ij} = \mu + \alpha_i + \beta_j$$

The saturated model adds interaction terms $(\alpha\beta)_{ij}$. The deviance between independence and saturated model is the independence test. The extension to multidimensional tables (hierarchical log-linear models) allows testing partial independence, conditional independence, and higher-order interaction effects.

### Power and sample size issues

To detect $V = 0,3$ in a $3 \times 3$ table with power $1 - \beta = 0,80$ and $\alpha = 0,05$, the calculation via Cohen (1988) uses:

$$n = \frac{\lambda^*}{\lambda_{\text{nc}}} \quad \text{where } \lambda_{\text{nc}} = n V^2 \min(r-1, c-1)$$

The non-centrality parameter $\lambda_{\text{nc}}$ is proportional to $n$. R package `pwr::pwr.chisq.test` automates this.

### Simpson's Paradox and Cochran-Mantel-Haenszel

In observational studies, the association seen in a $2 \times 2$ table may reverse sign when stratified by a confounding variable (Simpson's Paradox). The Cochran-Mantel-Haenszel test combines $k$ tables $2 \times 2$:

$$\chi^2_{CMH} = \frac{\left[\sum_s (O_{11s} - E_{11s})\right]^2}{\sum_s \text{Var}(O_{11s})}$$

with $df = 1$, where $s$ is the stratum index. Yields adjusted common odds ratio.

### Bayesian alternatives

The Bayes factor for contingency tables uses the Dirichlet-Multinomial model. R package `BayesFactor::contingencyTableBF`. For large sparse tables, hierarchical Dirichlet priors model cell heterogeneity without the asymptotic issues of classical $\chi^2$.

### Limits of $\chi^2$ with large $n$

For very large $n$, any real discrepancy — even tiny — becomes statistically significant. A $V = 0,03$ with $n = 10.000$ generates $\chi^2 = 9$ and p-value $< 0,05$ in a $2 \times 2$ table. The professional must report and interpret $V$ (or $\phi$), not just the p-value.

### Electoral survey in Brazil

The TSE releases ballot results by state and age group. An analyst wants to know whether candidate preference is independent of age group in a sample of 1,200 voters from Rio de Janeiro state.

**Observed table** (simplified, 3 candidates × 3 age groups):

| | 18–34 | 35–54 | 55+ | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cand. A | 120 | 150 | 80 | 350 |
| Cand. B | 100 | 180 | 120 | 400 |
| Cand. C | 80 | 70 | 100 | 250 |
| **Total** | 300 | 400 | 300 | 1000 |

(Sample adjusted to $n = 1000$ for calculation ease.)

**Step 1 — Expected:** $E_{ij} = R_i C_j / n$.

$E_{A,18-34} = 350 \times 300 / 1000 = 105$; $E_{A,35-54} = 350 \times 400/1000 = 140$; $E_{A,55+} = 350 \times 300/1000 = 105$. Others analogously.

**Step 2 — Check $E_{ij} \geq 5$:** all above 75. OK.

**Step 3 — Calculate $\chi^2$:**

For Cand. A: $(120-105)^2/105 + (150-140)^2/140 + (80-105)^2/105 \approx 2,14 + 0,71 + 5,95 = 8,80$.

Summing all 9 cells: $\chi^2 \approx 26,4$ (approximate value).

**Step 4 — $df$:** $(3-1)(3-1) = 4$.

**Step 5 — Decision:** Critical value $\chi^2_{0,05;\,4} = 9,49$. Since $26,4 \gg 9,49$, **we reject $H_0$** — there is significant association between preferred candidate and age group.

**Step 6 — Effect size:** $V = \sqrt{26,4/(1000 \times 2)} \approx 0,115$ (small effect despite significance — large $n$ amplifies everything).

**Common errors in Brazilian context**

- Use $\chi^2$ on panel data (before/after campaign) without adapting to McNemar.
- Ignore the TSE's complex sampling design — $\chi^2$ estimators must be corrected by sampling weights (R package `survey`).
- Report only p-value without Cramér's V in a large $n$ context.

</Porta>

## Worked examples

<Exemplo numero="1" titulo="Goodness of fit: fair die?" dificuldade="basico">

**Problem.** A die is rolled 120 times. Counts per face: 15, 22, 17, 25, 19, 22. At the 5% level, is the die fair?

**Strategy.** Goodness-of-fit test: $H_0$ is that each face has probability 1/6. Expected: $E_i = 120/6 = 20$ for each face. $df = 6 - 1 = 5$.

**Solution.**

$$\chi^2 = \frac{(15-20)^2}{20} + \frac{(22-20)^2}{20} + \frac{(17-20)^2}{20} + \frac{(25-20)^2}{20} + \frac{(19-20)^2}{20} + \frac{(22-20)^2}{20}$$

$$= \frac{25 + 4 + 9 + 25 + 1 + 4}{20} = \frac{68}{20} = 3,40$$

Critical value: $\chi^2_{0,05;\,5} = 11,07$. Since $3,40 < 11,07$, **we do not reject $H_0$**.

**Verification.** The p-value associated with $\chi^2 = 3,40$ with $df = 5$ is approximately 0.64 — well above 0.05. The largest discrepancy was on face 4 (+5), but within what is expected from sampling variation.

**Source.** [OpenStax Statistics, §11.3, Example 11.3](https://openstax.org/books/statistics/pages/11-3-goodness-of-fit-test) — CC-BY.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="2" titulo="Independence: gender and opinion on affirmative action" dificuldade="intermediario">

**Problem.** Survey of 400 Brazilian voters records gender (Male/Female) and opinion on racial quotas (Favorable/Neutral/Against). Observed table:

| | Favorable | Neutral | Against | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Male | 80 | 40 | 80 | 200 |
| Female | 120 | 50 | 30 | 200 |
| **Total** | 200 | 90 | 110 | 400 |

Test independence at $\alpha = 5\%$.

**Strategy.** Table $2 \times 3$: $df = (2-1)(3-1) = 2$.

**Solution.**

$E_{M,Fav} = 200 \times 200 / 400 = 100$; $E_{M,Neu} = 200 \times 90/400 = 45$; $E_{M,Con} = 200 \times 110/400 = 55$.

$E_{F,Fav} = 100$; $E_{F,Neu} = 45$; $E_{F,Con} = 55$.

$$\chi^2 = \frac{(80-100)^2}{100} + \frac{(40-45)^2}{45} + \frac{(80-55)^2}{55} + \frac{(120-100)^2}{100} + \frac{(50-45)^2}{45} + \frac{(30-55)^2}{55}$$

$$= 4,00 + 0,56 + 11,36 + 4,00 + 0,56 + 11,36 = 31,84$$

Critical value: $\chi^2_{0,05;\,2} = 5,99$. Since $31,84 \gg 5,99$, **we reject $H_0$** — there is significant association.

**Verification.** $V = \sqrt{31,84/(400 \times 1)} \approx 0,282$ — medium effect. The largest contribution comes from the "Against" category (25 points out of 31.84).

**Source.** [OpenIntro Statistics, §6.4, Exercise 6.29](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/) — CC-BY-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="3" titulo="Goodness of fit to Poisson distribution" dificuldade="intermediario">

**Problem.** A call center records the number of calls per hour over 200 hours: 0 calls (30 hours), 1 call (60 hours), 2 calls (54 hours), 3 calls (36 hours), 4 or more (20 hours). Test whether the data follow Poisson at 5%.

**Strategy.** Estimate $\hat{\lambda} = (0 \cdot 30 + 1 \cdot 60 + 2 \cdot 54 + 3 \cdot 36 + 4 \cdot 20)/200 = 328/200 = 1,64$. Since we estimated 1 parameter, $df = 5 - 1 - 1 = 3$.

**Solution.**

Poisson probabilities ($\hat{\lambda} = 1,64$): $p_0 = e^{-1,64} \approx 0,1942$; $p_1 \approx 0,3185$; $p_2 \approx 0,2612$; $p_3 \approx 0,1427$; $p_{4+} = 1 - \sum_{0}^{3} p_i \approx 0,0834$.

Expected: $E_0 = 38,8$; $E_1 = 63,7$; $E_2 = 52,2$; $E_3 = 28,5$; $E_{4+} = 16,7$. All $\geq 5$: OK.

$$\chi^2 = \frac{(30-38,8)^2}{38,8} + \frac{(60-63,7)^2}{63,7} + \frac{(54-52,2)^2}{52,2} + \frac{(36-28,5)^2}{28,5} + \frac{(20-16,7)^2}{16,7}$$

$$\approx 2,00 + 0,21 + 0,06 + 1,97 + 0,65 = 4,89$$

Critical value: $\chi^2_{0,05;\,3} = 7,81$. Since $4,89 < 7,81$, **we do not reject $H_0$** — the data are compatible with Poisson.

**Verification.** The $df = 3$ (not 4) because we estimated $\lambda$ from the data itself; using the wrong $df$ is the most common pitfall in this type of problem.

**Source.** [OpenStax Statistics, §11.3, Example 11.5 (adapted)](https://openstax.org/books/statistics/pages/11-3-goodness-of-fit-test) — CC-BY.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="4" titulo="$2 \\times 2$ table with Yates correction" dificuldade="avancado">

**Problem.** A clinical trial compares vaccine vs. placebo in 60 volunteers (30 in each group). Results:

| | Protected | Unprotected | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vaccine | 22 | 8 | 30 |
| Placebo | 15 | 15 | 30 |
| **Total** | 37 | 23 | 60 |

With Yates correction, test independence at 5%.

**Strategy.** Table $2 \times 2$, $df = 1$. Expected: $E_{V,P} = 37 \times 30/60 = 18,5$; $E_{V,NP} = 23 \times 30/60 = 11,5$; others equal by symmetry.

**Solution.**

$$\chi^2_Y = \frac{(\lvert 22 - 18,5\rvert - 0,5)^2}{18,5} + \frac{(\lvert 8 - 11,5\rvert - 0,5)^2}{11,5} + \frac{(\lvert 15 - 18,5\rvert - 0,5)^2}{18,5} + \frac{(\lvert 15 - 11,5\rvert - 0,5)^2}{11,5}$$

$$= \frac{(3,0)^2}{18,5} + \frac{(3,0)^2}{11,5} + \frac{(3,0)^2}{18,5} + \frac{(3,0)^2}{11,5} = \frac{9}{18,5} + \frac{9}{11,5} + \frac{9}{18,5} + \frac{9}{11,5}$$

$$= 0,486 + 0,783 + 0,486 + 0,783 = 2,538$$

Critical value: $\chi^2_{0,05;\,1} = 3,84$. Since $2,538 < 3,84$, **we do not reject $H_0$** at the 5% level.

**Verification.** Without Yates correction: $\chi^2 = (3,5)^2 \times (2/18,5 + 2/11,5) = 12,25 \times (0,108 + 0,174) = 3,45$. Still would not reject, but the correction was conservative as expected.

<Exemplo numero="5" titulo="Mendelian genetics: 9:3:3:1 ratio" dificuldade="desafio">

**Problem.** A dihybrid cross in peas (Mendel) predicts phenotypes in the ratio 9:3:3:1 (smooth-yellow : smooth-green : wrinkled-yellow : wrinkled-green). Observed in 160 offspring: 90, 30, 27, 13. Test at 5% whether the data follow Mendel.

**Strategy.** Expected proportions: $p_1 = 9/16$, $p_2 = 3/16$, $p_3 = 3/16$, $p_4 = 1/16$. $df = 4 - 1 = 3$ (fully specified distribution).

**Solution.**

$E_1 = 160 \times 9/16 = 90$; $E_2 = 160 \times 3/16 = 30$; $E_3 = 30$; $E_4 = 160 \times 1/16 = 10$.

$$\chi^2 = \frac{(90-90)^2}{90} + \frac{(30-30)^2}{30} + \frac{(27-30)^2}{30} + \frac{(13-10)^2}{10}$$

$$= 0 + 0 + \frac{9}{30} + \frac{9}{10} = 0,30 + 0,90 = 1,20$$

Critical value: $\chi^2_{0,05;\,3} = 7,81$. Since $1,20 \ll 7,81$, **we do not reject $H_0$** — the data are consistent with Mendel's laws.

**Verification.** The p-value is approximately 0.75 — the data fit the 9:3:3:1 ratio almost perfectly. The wrinkled-green category ($E_4 = 10$) is exactly at the limit of Cochran's rule; for smaller samples, category merging or Fisher–Freeman–Halton would be recommended.

**Source.** [OpenStax Statistics, §11.3, Example 11.7 (adapted)](https://openstax.org/books/statistics/pages/11-3-goodness-of-fit-test) — CC-BY.

</Exemplo>
