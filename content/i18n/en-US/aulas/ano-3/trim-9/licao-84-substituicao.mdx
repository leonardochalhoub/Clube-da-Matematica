<Porta nivel="formal" titulo="Theorem and procedure">

### Change-of-variable theorem

<Teorema titulo="Substitution (change of variable)">

Let $g: [a, b] \to \mathbb{R}$ be differentiable with $g'$ continuous, and $f$ continuous on the image of $g$. Then:

$$\int_a^b f(g(x))\, g'(x)\, dx = \int_{g(a)}^{g(b)} f(u)\, du.$$

**Indefinite version:** if $F' = f$, then $\int f(g(x))\, g'(x)\, dx = F(g(x)) + C$.

</Teorema>

> "The substitution rule is the equivalent for integration of the chain rule for differentiation." — [OpenStax Calculus Vol. 1, §5.5](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/5-5-substitution)

**Proof.** If $F' = f$, by the chain rule: $(F \circ g)' = f(g(x)) \cdot g'(x)$. Thus $F \circ g$ is an antiderivative of $f(g(x)) g'(x)$. By the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus:

$$\int_a^b f(g(x)) g'(x)\, dx = F(g(b)) - F(g(a)) = \int_{g(a)}^{g(b)} f(u)\, du. \quad \square$$

### Mechanical procedure

<Definicao titulo="Steps for substitution">

1. **Identify** $u = g(x)$ — typically the "inner" expression of a composite function.
2. **Compute** $du = g'(x)\, dx$ and, if needed, solve for $dx$.
3. **Rewrite** the entire integral in terms of $u$: both the integrand and the $dx$ must disappear.
4. **Definite integral:** also substitute the limits of integration: $a \to g(a)$, $b \to g(b)$.
5. **Compute** the integral in $u$ (should be simpler than the original).
6. **Indefinite:** substitute back $u = g(x)$ to express the result in terms of $x$.

</Definicao>

### Sign that substitution will work

The integrand must contain $f(\text{something})$ multiplied by the **derivative of the "something"** (or a constant multiple of that derivative).

Examples of the pattern:

| Integrand | $u$ | $du$ |
|---|---|---|
| $2x\, e^{x^2}$ | $x^2$ | $2x\, dx$ |
| $\cos x \cdot e^{\sin x}$ | $\sin x$ | $\cos x\, dx$ |
| $\frac{x}{x^2 + 1}$ | $x^2 + 1$ | $2x\, dx$ (needs adjustment $1/2$) |
| $x^2(x^3 + 1)^5$ | $x^3 + 1$ | $3x^2\, dx$ (adjustment $1/3$) |

<Insight>

**Constant adjustment:** if $g'(x)$ appears multiplied by a constant $k$ in the integrand (but not by the exact value of $g'$), simply divide and multiply by $k$:

$$\int x\, e^{x^2}\, dx = \frac{1}{2}\int 2x\, e^{x^2}\, dx = \frac{1}{2} e^{x^2} + C.$$

This works with constants but **not** with variables or functions of $x$.

</Insight>

Imagine you need to add up hard-to-count pieces. Then you discover you can swap each piece for a coin, and counting coins is easy. Later you swap the coins back for what you needed.

Substitution is that: swap a hard form for an easy one to calculate, then convert back.

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

Remember the **chain rule** in derivatives? $(\sin(x^2))' = \cos(x^2) \cdot 2x$.

The chain rule "creates" a product. Substitution is the **way back**: when you see $\cos(x^2) \cdot 2x$, you recognize it as a "child" of the chain rule and reverse it to $\sin(x^2)$.

**How to do it:** call the "inner thing" $u$. For example, $u = x^2$. Then $du = 2x\, dx$. The integral $\int \cos(x^2) \cdot 2x\, dx$ becomes $\int \cos u\, du = \sin u + C = \sin(x^2) + C$.

Substitution works when you **recognize the pattern**. That comes with practice.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old">

Substitution is the most important integration technique. Whenever the chain rule produced a product when differentiating, substitution undoes that product when integrating.

**Step-by-step for $\int 2x(x^2+1)^3\, dx$:**

1. Choose $u = x^2 + 1$ (the "thing inside" the power).
2. Compute: $du = 2x\, dx$ — exactly what's left!
3. Rewrite: $\int u^3\, du$.
4. Integrate: $u^4/4 + C$.
5. Substitute back: $\frac{(x^2+1)^4}{4} + C$.

**Check:** differentiate the result by the chain rule:
$\left(\frac{(x^2+1)^4}{4}\right)' = \frac{4(x^2+1)^3}{4} \cdot 2x = 2x(x^2+1)^3$. Correct.

**Definite integral with substitution:**

In addition to substituting the variable, transform the limits too:

$$\int_0^1 2x(x^2+1)^3\, dx: \quad u(0) = 1, \quad u(1) = 2.$$

$$\int_1^2 u^3\, du = \left[\frac{u^4}{4}\right]_1^2 = 4 - \frac{1}{4} = \frac{15}{4}.$$

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

Substitution is the inverse of the chain rule. In formal terms:

### Hypotheses for the definite integral

For the version with limit substitution, $g$ must be **continuous and differentiable** on $[a, b]$. For the indefinite version, it is not necessary that $g$ be injective, but in the definite version with limit substitution, if $g$ is not monotone, it may be necessary to split the interval at points where $g'$ changes sign.

### Special substitutions

**Radicals:** $\sqrt{ax + b}$ — take $u = ax + b$.

**Trigonometric functions:** $\int \tan x\, dx = \int \frac{\sin x}{\cos x}\, dx$. Take $u = \cos x$, $du = -\sin x\, dx$:

$$\int \tan x\, dx = -\int \frac{du}{u} = -\ln|u| + C = -\ln|\cos x| + C = \ln|\sec x| + C.$$

**Composite exponential:** $\int e^{ax}\, dx = \frac{1}{a}e^{ax} + C$ (constant adjustment: $u = ax$, $du = a\, dx$).

> "One of the keys to recognizing which substitution to use is identifying the 'inner' function — the one that, if differentiated, would yield a multiplicative factor present in the integrand." — [APEX Calculus, §6.1](https://www.apexcalculus.com/)

### Substitution fails

Substitution fails when $g'(x)$ **does not appear** in the integrand — or appears multiplied by a function of $x$ that is not constant. For example, $\int x^2 e^{x^3 + x}\, dx$ has no simple substitution because the derivative of $x^3 + x$ is $3x^2 + 1$, not $x^2$.

When direct substitution fails: try integration by parts (Lesson 85).

### In production (SymPy)

```python
import sympy as sp

print(sp.integrate(2*x * (x**2+1)**3, x))
# => (x**2 + 1)**4/4
```

### Change of variable in analysis

The change-of-variable theorem in $\mathbb{R}^n$ (for multiple integrals) is:

$$\int_{\phi(\Omega)} f(y)\, dy = \int_\Omega f(\phi(x)) |\det J_\phi(x)|\, dx,$$

where $J_\phi$ is the Jacobian matrix of $\phi$. In 1D, $|\det J_\phi| = |g'(x)|$, recovering the usual substitution. In 2D and 3D, the Jacobian measures the "distortion of area/volume" of the coordinate change — it is the foundation of integrals in polar, cylindrical, and spherical coordinates.

### Substitution in probability

If $X$ is a random variable with density $f_X$, and $Y = g(X)$ with $g$ monotone and differentiable, the density of $Y$ is:

$$f_Y(y) = f_X(g^{-1}(y)) \cdot \left|\frac{d}{dy} g^{-1}(y)\right|.$$

This is exactly the change-of-variable theorem applied to the density. Fundamental for Monte Carlo simulations (inverse-transform method).

### Substitution integration in ODEs

The method of **separation of variables** for $\frac{dy}{dx} = h(x)k(y)$ is a disguised substitution: $\int \frac{dy}{k(y)} = \int h(x)\, dx$. The technique reduces the ODE to two independent antiderivations.

### Trigonometric substitution (preview of Lesson 87)

For integrands with $\sqrt{a^2 - x^2}$, $\sqrt{a^2 + x^2}$, $\sqrt{x^2 - a^2}$, one uses trigonometric substitution ($x = a\sin\theta$, $a\tan\theta$, $a\sec\theta$) to eliminate radicals via Pythagorean identities. Generalization of the same principle.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practical application — Brazilian data">

### Compound interest with variable contributions

The growth rate of an investment with increasing contributions is $r(t) = 1000 e^{0.06t}$ reals per month. Value accumulated in 12 months:

$$V = \int_0^{12} 1000 e^{0.06t}\, dt.$$

Substitution: $u = 0.06t$, $du = 0.06\, dt$, so $dt = du/0.06$.

$$V = \frac{1000}{0.06}\int_0^{0.72} e^u\, du = \frac{1000}{0.06}[e^{0.72} - 1] \approx 16.667 \times 1.054 \approx 17.567.$$

### Damped velocity

A vehicle has acceleration $a(t) = 5e^{-0.2t}$ m/s². Velocity after 10 s (starting from rest):

$$v(10) = \int_0^{10} 5e^{-0.2t}\, dt.$$

$u = -0.2t$, $du = -0.2\, dt$:

$$= \frac{5}{-0.2}[e^{-0.2t}]_0^{10} = -25(e^{-2} - 1) = 25(1 - e^{-2}) \approx 25 \times 0.865 \approx 21.6\text{ m/s}.$$

### SymPy for verification

```python
import sympy as sp
x = sp.Symbol('x')
# Substitution: integral of x*sqrt(x^2 + 1)
print(sp.integrate(x * sp.sqrt(x**2 + 1), x))
# => (x**2 + 1)**(3/2)/3
```

## Worked examples

<Exemplo numero="1" titulo="Direct substitution with exponential (basic)">

**Problem.** Compute $\displaystyle\int 3x^2 e^{x^3}\, dx$.

**Strategy.** The integrand has the form $f(g(x)) \cdot g'(x)$ with $g(x) = x^3$ and $g'(x) = 3x^2$.

**Solution.**
$u = x^3$, $du = 3x^2\, dx$.

$$\int 3x^2 e^{x^3}\, dx = \int e^u\, du = e^u + C = e^{x^3} + C.$$

**Check.** $(e^{x^3})' = e^{x^3} \cdot 3x^2 = 3x^2 e^{x^3}$. Correct.

**Source.** [APEX Calculus, §6.1, Example 6.1.2](https://www.apexcalculus.com/) — CC-BY-NC.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="2" titulo="Constant adjustment (basic)">

**Problem.** Compute $\displaystyle\int x(x^2 + 5)^4\, dx$.

**Strategy.** $g(x) = x^2 + 5$, $g'(x) = 2x$. The integrand has $x$ (half of $g'$), so a $1/2$ adjustment is needed.

**Solution.**
$u = x^2 + 5$, $du = 2x\, dx$, so $x\, dx = du/2$.

$$\int x(x^2+5)^4\, dx = \int u^4 \cdot \frac{du}{2} = \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{u^5}{5} + C = \frac{(x^2+5)^5}{10} + C.$$

**Check.** $\left(\frac{(x^2+5)^5}{10}\right)' = \frac{5(x^2+5)^4 \cdot 2x}{10} = x(x^2+5)^4$. Correct.

**Source.** [APEX Calculus, §6.1, Example 6.1.4](https://www.apexcalculus.com/) — CC-BY-NC.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="3" titulo="Substitution in definite integral with limit change (intermediate)">

**Problem.** Compute $\displaystyle\int_0^1 2x(x^2 + 1)^3\, dx$.

**Strategy.** $u = x^2 + 1$, $du = 2x\, dx$. Change the limits: $u(0) = 1$, $u(1) = 2$.

**Solution.**
$$\int_0^1 2x(x^2+1)^3\, dx = \int_1^2 u^3\, du = \left[\frac{u^4}{4}\right]_1^2 = \frac{16}{4} - \frac{1}{4} = \frac{15}{4}.$$

**Check.** Without limit change: antiderivative $(x^2+1)^4/4$. Evaluate at $x=1$ and $x=0$: $2^4/4 - 1^4/4 = 4 - 1/4 = 15/4$. Correct.

**Source.** [OpenStax Calculus Vol. 1, §5.5, Example 5.38](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/5-5-substitution) — CC-BY-NC-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="4" titulo="Substitution with trigonometric function (intermediate)">

**Problem.** Compute $\displaystyle\int \sin^3 x \cos x\, dx$.

**Strategy.** $g(x) = \sin x$, $g'(x) = \cos x$ — appears explicitly in the integrand.

**Solution.**
$u = \sin x$, $du = \cos x\, dx$.

$$\int \sin^3 x \cos x\, dx = \int u^3\, du = \frac{u^4}{4} + C = \frac{\sin^4 x}{4} + C.$$

**Check.** $\left(\frac{\sin^4 x}{4}\right)' = \frac{4\sin^3 x \cos x}{4} = \sin^3 x \cos x$. Correct.

**Source.** [OpenStax Calculus Vol. 1, §5.5, Example 5.36](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/5-5-substitution) — CC-BY-NC-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="5" titulo="Integral of tangent by substitution (challenge)">

**Problem.** Compute $\displaystyle\int \tan x\, dx$.

**Strategy.** Write $\tan x = \sin x / \cos x$, then take $u = \cos x$.

**Solution.**
$u = \cos x$, $du = -\sin x\, dx$, so $\sin x\, dx = -du$.

$$\int \frac{\sin x}{\cos x}\, dx = \int \frac{-du}{u} = -\ln|u| + C = -\ln|\cos x| + C = \ln|\sec x| + C.$$

**Check.** $(\ln|\sec x|)' = \frac{\sec x \tan x}{\sec x} = \tan x$. Correct.

**Source.** [APEX Calculus, §6.1, Example 6.1.8](https://www.apexcalculus.com/) — CC-BY-NC.

</Exemplo>
