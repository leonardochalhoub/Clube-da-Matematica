### Riemann sum

<Definicao titulo="Riemann integral">

Let $f: [a,b] \to \mathbb{R}$ be **bounded**. A **partition** $P$ of $[a,b]$ is a set $\{a = x_0 < x_1 < \cdots < x_n = b\}$. The **norm** of the partition is $\|P\| = \max_i \Delta x_i$ where $\Delta x_i = x_i - x_{i-1}$.

Choosing **sample points** $x_i^* \in [x_{i-1}, x_i]$, the **Riemann sum** is:

$$S(f, P) = \sum_{i=1}^n f(x_i^*)\, \Delta x_i.$$

$f$ is **integrable on $[a,b]$** if there exists a limit $L$ such that for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists $\delta > 0$ with $|S(f,P) - L| < \varepsilon$ whenever $\|P\| < \delta$, regardless of the choice of $x_i^*$. We write $\int_a^b f(x)\, dx = L$.

</Definicao>

> "The definite integral is formally the limit of Riemann sums when the norm of the partition tends to zero." — [OpenStax Calculus Vol. 1, §5.2](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/5-2-the-definite-integral)

### Darboux sums

Equivalent definition via lower and upper sums:

$$L(f, P) = \sum_{i=1}^n \Bigl(\inf_{[x_{i-1}, x_i]} f\Bigr) \Delta x_i, \qquad U(f, P) = \sum_{i=1}^n \Bigl(\sup_{[x_{i-1}, x_i]} f\Bigr) \Delta x_i.$$

$f$ is integrable $\iff \sup_P L(f,P) = \inf_P U(f,P)$.

### Integrability criterion

<Teorema titulo="Integrability of continuous functions">

If $f$ is **continuous** on $[a, b]$, then $f$ is integrable on $[a, b]$.

More generally: $f$ bounded whose **set of discontinuities has Lebesgue measure zero** is integrable (Riemann-Lebesgue criterion).

</Teorema>

### Properties

<Definicao titulo="Properties of the definite integral">

For $f$, $g$ integrable on $[a, b]$ and $\alpha, \beta, c \in \mathbb{R}$ with $a \leq c \leq b$:

- **Linearity**: $\int_a^b (\alpha f + \beta g) = \alpha \int_a^b f + \beta \int_a^b g$
- **Additivity**: $\int_a^b f = \int_a^c f + \int_c^b f$
- **Reversal of limits**: $\int_b^a f = -\int_a^b f$
- **Zero integral**: $\int_a^a f = 0$
- **Monotonicity**: $f \leq g$ on $[a,b] \Rightarrow \int_a^b f \leq \int_a^b g$
- **Triangle inequality**: $\left|\int_a^b f\right| \leq \int_a^b |f|$

</Definicao>

### Mean Value Theorem for Integrals

<Teorema titulo="Mean Value Theorem for Integrals">

If $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$, there exists $c \in (a, b)$ such that:

$$\int_a^b f(x)\, dx = f(c)(b - a).$$

The number $\frac{1}{b-a}\int_a^b f$ is the **mean value** of $f$ on $[a,b]$.

</Teorema>

<Insight>

The **oriented area** is the key: when $f < 0$, the contribution of the integral is negative — the rectangles lie "below the axis". To compute the total geometric area (always positive), integrate $|f(x)|$ or split the interval at the zeros of $f$.

</Insight>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

Imagine a drawing of a mountain. You want to paint everything **under the mountain** and above the ground. How much paint do you need?

You take many **tiny squares**, fit each one under the mountain, and add up the paint from all of them. The finer the squares, the closer to reality.

That is the integral: **add up squares to measure an area**.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

Area of a rectangle = base × height. But what if the height changes all the time, like on a mountain?

**Idea:** split the base into small pieces, make one rectangle for each piece (with the height "in the middle"), add them all up.

The more pieces (the finer), the closer the sum gets to the real area. When the pieces disappear, the sum converges to an exact number.

That number is the **definite integral**: $\int_a^b f(x)\, dx$. The symbol $\int$ is a stretched "S" — it comes from "Sum".

The "S" was invented by Leibniz in 1675. Newton called the same thing a "fluent". The two of them fought over credit — one of the biggest disputes in the history of mathematics.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old teen">

The integral $\int_a^b f(x)\, dx$ is the **oriented area** between the graph of $f$ and the $x$-axis, on the interval $[a, b]$.

**How to approximate:** divide $[a, b]$ into $n$ equal pieces of width $\Delta x = (b-a)/n$. In each piece, pick a point $x_i^*$ and build the rectangle of height $f(x_i^*)$.

$$\int_a^b f(x)\, dx \approx \sum_{i=1}^n f(x_i^*)\, \Delta x.$$

The larger $n$, the better the approximation. In the limit: exact value.

**Oriented area:** if $f(x) > 0$, the rectangle contributes positively. If $f(x) < 0$, it contributes negatively. So a function that crosses the $x$-axis can have integral zero even though it has nonzero "geometric area".

**Example:** $\int_0^\pi \sin x\, dx = 2$ (area above the axis), but $\int_0^{2\pi} \sin x\, dx = 0$ (the second half cancels the first).

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

The Riemann integral is a construction from real analysis; the formal definition via $\varepsilon$-$\delta$ appears in texts you will see in Calculus II/III. What matters for practical use:

### Types of Riemann sums

- **Left**: $x_i^* = x_{i-1}$ — use the left endpoint of each subinterval.
- **Right**: $x_i^* = x_i$ — right endpoint.
- **Midpoint**: $x_i^* = (x_{i-1} + x_i)/2$ — better accuracy per rectangle (error $O(\Delta x^2)$ vs. $O(\Delta x)$).
- **Trapezoidal**: use trapezoids, not rectangles — error $O(\Delta x^2)$.

> "The rules for numerical approximation of the integral — trapezoidal, Simpson — are sophisticated versions of the Riemann sum, with optimized weight choices." — [APEX Calculus, §5.3](https://www.apexcalculus.com/)

### Applications of properties

**Additivity** lets you break up complicated domains: $\int_{-2}^3 f = \int_{-2}^0 f + \int_0^3 f$.

**Monotonicity** gives quick estimates without computing the integral: if $m \leq f \leq M$ on $[a,b]$, then $m(b-a) \leq \int_a^b f \leq M(b-a)$.

**Mean Value Theorem for Integrals** guarantees the existence of a "mean value" for any continuous function — fundamental for fixed-point theorems and ODEs.

### Numerical quadrature

In engineering applications: **no closed formula exists** for many relevant integrals (e.g., normal distribution, Bessel functions). Use numerical quadrature: Gauss-Legendre, Clenshaw-Curtis, Gauss-Kronrod (with error estimates). Implemented in `scipy.integrate.quad`.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

### Riemann vs. Lebesgue integrals

The Riemann integral requires the partition to refine the domain. The **Lebesgue** integral (1902) partitions the range: $\int f\, d\mu = \int_0^\infty \mu(\{f > t\})\, dt$. For continuous functions (the case of this lesson), the two coincide. But Lebesgue integrates more general functions ($L^1$ and $L^2$), has dominated convergence, and is the foundation of modern probability (expectation of a random variable is a Lebesgue integral).

### Henstock-Kurzweil integral

The gauge integral (Henstock, 1955; Kurzweil, 1957) generalizes Riemann and integrates every derivative — something Lebesgue does not do in general. Rarely used in applications, but technically elegant.

### Financial applications

The **Black-Scholes price** is an integral of the payoff against the normal (log-normal) density. The **expectation of a continuous random variable** is $E[X] = \int x\, f_X(x)\, dx$ — Riemann integral if $f_X$ is continuous, Lebesgue in the general case. **Value at Risk** is a quantile, found by inverting the integral of the density.

### Parallel computing

Riemann sums with $n = 10^9$ in Monte Carlo (integral by random sampling) are the standard method in high dimensions ($d > 10$), where deterministic quadrature fails due to the "curse of dimensionality". Monte Carlo convergence: $O(n^{-1/2})$ independent of $d$.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practical application — Brazilian data">

### Distance traveled by an urban bus

A bus has velocity $v(t) = 30 + 10\sin(t)$ km/h (typical traffic oscillation). Distance from $t = 0$ to $t = \pi$ hours:

$$d = \int_0^\pi (30 + 10\sin t)\, dt.$$

Since $\int_0^\pi 30\, dt = 30\pi$ and $\int_0^\pi 10\sin t\, dt = 20$, we get $d = 30\pi + 20 \approx 94{,}2 + 20 = 114{,}2\text{ km}$.

### Residential energy consumption

A residence has power $P(t) = 1 + 0{,}5\cos(\pi t / 12)$ kW (24-hour cycle). Energy consumed in 12 hours:

$$E = \int_0^{12} P(t)\, dt = \int_0^{12} \left(1 + 0{,}5\cos\frac{\pi t}{12}\right) dt.$$

Result: $E = 12 + \frac{0{,}5 \cdot 12}{\pi}[\sin(\pi)] = 12$ kWh (the cosine part integrates to zero).

## Solved examples

<Exemplo numero="1" titulo="Riemann sum with n = 4 (basic)">

**Problem.** Estimate $\int_0^2 x^2\, dx$ using the right Riemann sum with $n = 4$ subintervals.

**Strategy.** Divide $[0, 2]$ into 4 equal parts, take the right point of each part, calculate the sum.

**Solution.**
$\Delta x = 2/4 = 0{,}5$. Right points: $x_1^* = 0{,}5$, $x_2^* = 1$, $x_3^* = 1{,}5$, $x_4^* = 2$.

$$S_4 = f(0{,}5) + f(1) + f(1{,}5) + f(2)) \cdot 0{,}5 = (0{,}25 + 1 + 2{,}25 + 4) \cdot 0{,}5 = 7{,}5 \cdot 0{,}5 = 3{,}75.$$

(The exact value by the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus is $\int_0^2 x^2\, dx = 8/3 \approx 2{,}67$. The right sum overestimated because $x^2$ is increasing.)

**Verification.** The sum with large $n$ converges to $8/3$: confirms that $3{,}75$ is an overestimate.

**Source.** [APEX Calculus, §5.2, Example 5.2.4](https://www.apexcalculus.com/) — CC-BY-NC.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="2" titulo="Geometric interpretation of integral with negative function (basic)">

**Problem.** Interpret geometrically $\int_0^\pi \sin x\, dx$ and $\int_\pi^{2\pi} \sin x\, dx$.

**Strategy.** $\sin x \geq 0$ on $[0, \pi]$ and $\sin x \leq 0$ on $[\pi, 2\pi]$. The definite integral measures oriented area.

**Solution.**
- $\int_0^\pi \sin x\, dx = [-\cos x]_0^\pi = -\cos\pi + \cos 0 = 1 + 1 = 2$ (area above the axis: $+2$).
- $\int_\pi^{2\pi} \sin x\, dx = [-\cos x]_\pi^{2\pi} = -\cos 2\pi + \cos\pi = -1 - 1 = -2$ (area below the axis: contribution $-2$).

So $\int_0^{2\pi} \sin x\, dx = 2 + (-2) = 0$. The **geometric** area total is $2 + 2 = 4$, but the **integral** is zero.

**Verification.** Additivity: $\int_0^\pi + \int_\pi^{2\pi} = 2 - 2 = 0 = \int_0^{2\pi} \sin x\, dx$. Check.

**Source.** [OpenStax Calculus Vol. 1, §5.2, Example 5.18](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/5-2-the-definite-integral) — CC-BY-NC-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="3" titulo="Use of properties without calculating (intermediate)">

**Problem.** Given that $\int_0^4 f(x)\, dx = 7$ and $\int_0^4 g(x)\, dx = 3$, calculate $\int_0^4 (2f(x) - 5g(x))\, dx$.

**Strategy.** Use the linearity property of the definite integral.

**Solution.**
$$\int_0^4 (2f - 5g)\, dx = 2\int_0^4 f\, dx - 5\int_0^4 g\, dx = 2(7) - 5(3) = 14 - 15 = -1.$$

**Verification.** Linearity is exact — no approximation involved.

**Source.** [OpenStax Calculus Vol. 1, §5.2, Example 5.15](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/5-2-the-definite-integral) — CC-BY-NC-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="4" titulo="Estimation by bound (intermediate)">

**Problem.** Show that $2 \leq \int_0^2 \sqrt{x+1}\, dx \leq 2\sqrt{3}$.

**Strategy.** Use monotonicity: bound $\sqrt{x+1}$ on $[0, 2]$ from below and above.

**Solution.**
On $[0, 2]$: minimum of $\sqrt{x+1}$ at $x = 0$ is $\sqrt{1} = 1$; maximum at $x = 2$ is $\sqrt{3}$.

By monotonicity: $\int_0^2 1\, dx \leq \int_0^2 \sqrt{x+1}\, dx \leq \int_0^2 \sqrt{3}\, dx$, i.e. $2 \leq \int \leq 2\sqrt{3}$.

**Verification.** The exact value by the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus: $\int_0^2 \sqrt{x+1}\, dx = \left[\frac{2}{3}(x+1)^{3/2}\right]_0^2 = \frac{2}{3}(3^{3/2} - 1) = \frac{2}{3}(3\sqrt{3} - 1) \approx 2{,}80$, which is between 2 and $2\sqrt{3} \approx 3{,}46$. Check.

**Source.** [Active Calculus, §4.2, Exercise 4.2.3](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-4-2-Riemann.html) — CC-BY-NC-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="5" titulo="Mean value of continuous function (challenge)">

**Problem.** Calculate the mean value of $f(x) = x^2$ on the interval $[1, 4]$.

**Strategy.** Use the mean value formula: $f_{\text{mean}} = \frac{1}{b-a}\int_a^b f(x)\, dx$.

**Solution.**
$$f_{\text{mean}} = \frac{1}{4-1}\int_1^4 x^2\, dx = \frac{1}{3}\left[\frac{x^3}{3}\right]_1^4 = \frac{1}{3}\left(\frac{64}{3} - \frac{1}{3}\right) = \frac{1}{3} \cdot 21 = 7.$$

By the Mean Value Theorem for Integrals, there exists $c \in (1, 4)$ such that $f(c) = 7$, i.e. $c^2 = 7$, so $c = \sqrt{7} \approx 2{,}65 \in (1, 4)$. Check.

**Verification.** $\sqrt{7} \approx 2{,}65$ is within the interval $(1, 4)$, as guaranteed by the MVTI. Check.

**Source.** [APEX Calculus, §5.3, Example 5.3.4](https://www.apexcalculus.com/) — CC-BY-NC.

</Exemplo>
