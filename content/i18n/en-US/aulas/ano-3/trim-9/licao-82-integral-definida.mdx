### Riemann Sum

<Definicao titulo="Riemann Integral">

Let $f: [a,b] \to \mathbb{R}$ be **bounded**. A **partition** $P$ of $[a,b]$ is a set $\{a = x_0 < x_1 < \cdots < x_n = b\}$. The **mesh** of the partition is $\|P\| = \max_i \Delta x_i$ where $\Delta x_i = x_i - x_{i-1}$.

Choosing **sample points** $x_i^* \in [x_{i-1}, x_i]$, the **Riemann sum** is:

$$S(f, P) = \sum_{i=1}^n f(x_i^*)\, \Delta x_i.$$

$f$ is **integrable on $[a,b]$** if there exists a limit $L$ such that for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists $\delta > 0$ with $|S(f,P) - L| < \varepsilon$ whenever $\|P\| < \delta$, regardless of the choice of $x_i^*$. We write $\int_a^b f(x)\, dx = L$.

</Definicao>

> "The definite integral is formally the limit of Riemann sums as the mesh of the partition tends to zero." — [OpenStax Calculus Vol. 1, §5.2](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/5-2-the-definite-integral)

### Darboux Sums

Equivalent definition via lower and upper sums:

$$L(f, P) = \sum_{i=1}^n \Bigl(\inf_{[x_{i-1}, x_i]} f\Bigr) \Delta x_i, \qquad U(f, P) = \sum_{i=1}^n \Bigl(\sup_{[x_{i-1}, x_i]} f\Bigr) \Delta x_i.$$

$f$ is integrable $\iff \sup_P L(f,P) = \inf_P U(f,P)$.

### Integrability criterion

<Teorema titulo="Integrability of continuous functions">

If $f$ is **continuous** on $[a, b]$, then $f$ is integrable on $[a, b]$.

More generally: a bounded $f$ whose **set of discontinuities has Lebesgue measure zero** is integrable (Riemann–Lebesgue criterion).

</Teorema>

### Properties

<Definicao titulo="Properties of the definite integral">

For $f$, $g$ integrable on $[a, b]$ and $\alpha, \beta, c \in \mathbb{R}$ with $a \leq c \leq b$:

- **Linearity**: $\int_a^b (\alpha f + \beta g) = \alpha \int_a^b f + \beta \int_a^b g$
- **Additivity**: $\int_a^b f = \int_a^c f + \int_c^b f$
- **Reversal of limits**: $\int_b^a f = -\int_a^b f$
- **Zero integral**: $\int_a^a f = 0$
- **Monotonicity**: $f \leq g$ on $[a,b] \Rightarrow \int_a^b f \leq \int_a^b g$
- **Triangle inequality**: $\left|\int_a^b f\right| \leq \int_a^b |f|$

</Definicao>

### Mean Value Theorem for Integrals

<Teorema titulo="Mean Value Theorem for Integrals">

If $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$, there exists $c \in (a, b)$ such that:

$$\int_a^b f(x)\, dx = f(c)(b - a).$$

The number $\frac{1}{b-a}\int_a^b f$ is the **average value** of $f$ on $[a,b]$.

</Teorema>

<Insight>

**Signed area** is the key concept: when $f < 0$, the integral's contribution is negative — the rectangles lie "below the axis". To compute the total geometric area (always positive), integrate $|f(x)|$ or split the interval at the zeros of $f$.

</Insight>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

Imagine a drawing of a mountain. You want to paint everything **under the mountain** and above the ground. How much paint do you need?

You grab lots of very thin **little squares**, fit each one under the mountain, and add up all the paint. The thinner the squares, the closer you get to the real answer.

That is the integral: **adding up little squares to measure an area**.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

Area of a rectangle = base × height. But what if the height keeps changing, like on a mountain?

**Idea:** divide the base into small pieces, build a rectangle for each piece (using the height "in the middle"), then add them all up.

The more pieces (the thinner they are), the closer the sum gets to the real area. When the pieces shrink to nothing, the sum converges to an exact number.

That number is the **definite integral**: $\int_a^b f(x)\, dx$. The symbol $\int$ is a stretched "S" — it stands for "Sum".

The "S" was invented by Leibniz in 1675. Newton called the same thing a "fluent". The two men argued over credit — one of the greatest disputes in the history of mathematics.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old">

The integral $\int_a^b f(x)\, dx$ is the **signed area** between the graph of $f$ and the $x$-axis on the interval $[a, b]$.

**How to approximate:** divide $[a, b]$ into $n$ equal pieces of width $\Delta x = (b-a)/n$. In each piece, choose a sample point $x_i^*$ and build the rectangle of height $f(x_i^*)$.

$$\int_a^b f(x)\, dx \approx \sum_{i=1}^n f(x_i^*)\, \Delta x.$$

The larger $n$, the better the approximation. In the limit: the exact value.

**Signed area:** if $f(x) > 0$, the rectangle contributes positively. If $f(x) < 0$, it contributes negatively. As a result, a function that crosses the $x$-axis can have a zero integral even though its "geometric area" is non-zero.

**Example:** $\int_0^\pi \sin x\, dx = 2$ (area above the axis), but $\int_0^{2\pi} \sin x\, dx = 0$ (the second half cancels the first).

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

The Riemann integral is a construction from real analysis; the formal $\varepsilon$-$\delta$ definition appears in the texts you will see in Calculus II/III. What matters for practical use:

### Types of Riemann sums

- **Left**: $x_i^* = x_{i-1}$ — uses the left endpoint of each subinterval.
- **Right**: $x_i^* = x_i$ — right endpoint.
- **Midpoint**: $x_i^* = (x_{i-1} + x_i)/2$ — best accuracy per rectangle (error $O(\Delta x^2)$ vs. $O(\Delta x)$).
- **Trapezoidal**: uses trapezoids instead of rectangles — error $O(\Delta x^2)$.

> "Numerical integration rules — trapezoidal, Simpson — are sophisticated versions of Riemann sums, with optimized weight choices." — [APEX Calculus, §5.3](https://www.apexcalculus.com/)

### Applying the properties

**Additivity** lets you break complicated domains: $\int_{-2}^3 f = \int_{-2}^0 f + \int_0^3 f$.

**Monotonicity** gives quick estimates without computing the integral: if $m \leq f \leq M$ on $[a,b]$, then $m(b-a) \leq \int_a^b f \leq M(b-a)$.

**The Mean Value Theorem for Integrals** guarantees the existence of an "average value" for any continuous function — fundamental for fixed-point theorems and ODEs.

### Numerical quadrature

In engineering applications: **no closed form exists** for many relevant integrals (e.g., the normal distribution, Bessel functions). Numerical quadrature is used instead: Gauss-Legendre, Clenshaw-Curtis, Gauss-Kronrod (with error estimates). Implemented in `scipy.integrate.quad`.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

### Riemann vs. Lebesgue integrals

The Riemann integral partitions the domain. The **Lebesgue** integral (1902) partitions the codomain: $\int f\, d\mu = \int_0^\infty \mu(\{f > t\})\, dt$. For continuous functions (the case in this lesson) the two coincide. But Lebesgue integrates more general functions ($L^1$ and $L^2$), has dominated convergence, and is the foundation of modern probability (the expected value of a random variable is a Lebesgue integral).

### Henstock–Kurzweil integral

The gauge integral (Henstock, 1955; Kurzweil, 1957) generalizes Riemann and integrates every differentiable function — something Lebesgue cannot do in general. Rarely used in applications, but technically elegant.

### Financial applications

The **Black–Scholes price** is an integral of the payoff against the (log-)normal density. The **expected value of a continuous random variable** is $E[X] = \int x\, f_X(x)\, dx$ — a Riemann integral when $f_X$ is continuous, a Lebesgue integral in general. **Value at Risk** is a quantile found by inverting the integral of the density.

### Parallel computation

Riemann sums with $n = 10^9$ in Monte Carlo (integration by random sampling) are the standard method in high dimensions ($d > 10$), where deterministic quadrature fails due to the "curse of dimensionality". Monte Carlo convergence: $O(n^{-1/2})$ regardless of $d$.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practical application">

### Distance traveled by a city bus

A bus has velocity $v(t) = 30 + 10\sin(t)$ km/h (typical traffic oscillation). Distance between $t = 0$ and $t = \pi$ hours:

$$d = \int_0^\pi (30 + 10\sin t)\, dt.$$

Since $\int_0^\pi 30\, dt = 30\pi$ and $\int_0^\pi 10\sin t\, dt = 20$, we get $d = 30\pi + 20 \approx 94.2 + 20 = 114.2\text{ km}$.

### Household energy consumption

A household has power draw $P(t) = 1 + 0.5\cos(\pi t / 12)$ kW (24 h cycle). Energy consumed in 12 hours:

$$E = \int_0^{12} P(t)\, dt = \int_0^{12} \left(1 + 0.5\cos\frac{\pi t}{12}\right) dt.$$

Result: $E = 12 + \frac{0.5 \cdot 12}{\pi}[\sin(\pi)] = 12$ kWh (the cosine term integrates to zero).

## Worked examples

<Exemplo numero="1" titulo="Riemann sum with n = 4 (basic)">

**Problem.** Estimate $\int_0^2 x^2\, dx$ using the right Riemann sum with $n = 4$ subintervals.

**Strategy.** Divide $[0, 2]$ into 4 equal parts, take the right endpoint of each part, compute the sum.

**Solution.**
$\Delta x = 2/4 = 0.5$. Right endpoints: $x_1^* = 0.5$, $x_2^* = 1$, $x_3^* = 1.5$, $x_4^* = 2$.

$$S_4 = (f(0.5) + f(1) + f(1.5) + f(2)) \cdot 0.5 = (0.25 + 1 + 2.25 + 4) \cdot 0.5 = 7.5 \cdot 0.5 = 3.75.$$

(The exact value by the FTC is $\int_0^2 x^2\, dx = 8/3 \approx 2.67$. The right sum overestimated because $x^2$ is increasing.)

**Check.** The sum with large $n$ converges to $8/3$: this confirms that $3.75$ is an overestimate.

**Source.** [APEX Calculus, §5.2, Example 5.2.4](https://www.apexcalculus.com/) — CC-BY-NC.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="2" titulo="Geometric interpretation of an integral with a negative function (basic)">

**Problem.** Interpret geometrically $\int_0^\pi \sin x\, dx$ and $\int_\pi^{2\pi} \sin x\, dx$.

**Strategy.** $\sin x \geq 0$ on $[0, \pi]$ and $\sin x \leq 0$ on $[\pi, 2\pi]$. The definite integral measures signed area.

**Solution.**
- $\int_0^\pi \sin x\, dx = [-\cos x]_0^\pi = -\cos\pi + \cos 0 = 1 + 1 = 2$ (area above the axis: $+2$).
- $\int_\pi^{2\pi} \sin x\, dx = [-\cos x]_\pi^{2\pi} = -\cos 2\pi + \cos\pi = -1 - 1 = -2$ (area below the axis: contribution $-2$).

Therefore $\int_0^{2\pi} \sin x\, dx = 2 + (-2) = 0$. The total **geometric** area is $2 + 2 = 4$, but the **integral** is zero.

**Check.** Additivity: $\int_0^\pi + \int_\pi^{2\pi} = 2 - 2 = 0 = \int_0^{2\pi} \sin x\, dx$. Confirmed.

**Source.** [OpenStax Calculus Vol. 1, §5.2, Example 5.18](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/5-2-the-definite-integral) — CC-BY-NC-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="3" titulo="Using properties without computing (intermediate)">

**Problem.** Given that $\int_0^4 f(x)\, dx = 7$ and $\int_0^4 g(x)\, dx = 3$, compute $\int_0^4 (2f(x) - 5g(x))\, dx$.

**Strategy.** Apply the linearity of the definite integral.

**Solution.**
$$\int_0^4 (2f - 5g)\, dx = 2\int_0^4 f\, dx - 5\int_0^4 g\, dx = 2(7) - 5(3) = 14 - 15 = -1.$$

**Check.** Linearity is exact — no approximation involved.

**Source.** [OpenStax Calculus Vol. 1, §5.2, Example 5.15](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/5-2-the-definite-integral) — CC-BY-NC-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="4" titulo="Bound estimate (intermediate)">

**Problem.** Show that $2 \leq \int_0^2 \sqrt{x+1}\, dx \leq 2\sqrt{3}$.

**Strategy.** Use monotonicity: bound $\sqrt{x+1}$ on $[0, 2]$ from below and above.

**Solution.**
On $[0, 2]$: minimum of $\sqrt{x+1}$ at $x = 0$ is $\sqrt{1} = 1$; maximum at $x = 2$ is $\sqrt{3}$.

By monotonicity: $\int_0^2 1\, dx \leq \int_0^2 \sqrt{x+1}\, dx \leq \int_0^2 \sqrt{3}\, dx$, i.e., $2 \leq \int \leq 2\sqrt{3}$.

**Check.** Exact value by the FTC: $\int_0^2 \sqrt{x+1}\, dx = \left[\frac{2}{3}(x+1)^{3/2}\right]_0^2 = \frac{2}{3}(3^{3/2} - 1) = \frac{2}{3}(3\sqrt{3} - 1) \approx 2.80$, which lies between 2 and $2\sqrt{3} \approx 3.46$. Confirmed.

**Source.** [Active Calculus, §4.2, Exercise 4.2.3](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-4-2-Riemann.html) — CC-BY-NC-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="5" titulo="Average value of a continuous function (challenge)">

**Problem.** Compute the average value of $f(x) = x^2$ on the interval $[1, 4]$.

**Strategy.** Use the average value formula: $f_{\text{avg}} = \frac{1}{b-a}\int_a^b f(x)\, dx$.

**Solution.**
$$f_{\text{avg}} = \frac{1}{4-1}\int_1^4 x^2\, dx = \frac{1}{3}\left[\frac{x^3}{3}\right]_1^4 = \frac{1}{3}\left(\frac{64}{3} - \frac{1}{3}\right) = \frac{1}{3} \cdot 21 = 7.$$

By the Mean Value Theorem for Integrals, there exists $c \in (1, 4)$ such that $f(c) = 7$, i.e., $c^2 = 7$, so $c = \sqrt{7} \approx 2.65 \in (1, 4)$. Confirmed.

**Check.** $\sqrt{7} \approx 2.65$ lies inside the interval $(1, 4)$, as guaranteed by the MVT. Confirmed.

**Source.** [APEX Calculus, §5.3, Example 5.3.4](https://www.apexcalculus.com/) — CC-BY-NC.

</Exemplo>
