### FTC — Part 1: differentiate the integral

<Teorema titulo="FTC Part 1 (derivative of the integral function)">

Let $f: [a, b] \to \mathbb{R}$ be **continuous**. Define the **integral function**:

$$G(x) = \int_a^x f(t)\, dt, \quad x \in [a, b].$$

Then $G$ is differentiable on $(a, b)$ and $G'(x) = f(x)$.

</Teorema>

> "FTC Part 1 states that the derivative of the function defined by an integral with a variable upper limit is equal to the integrand evaluated at the upper limit." — [OpenStax Calculus Vol. 1, §5.3](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/5-3-the-fundamental-theorem-of-calculus)

**Proof of FTC Part 1.** By definition of derivative:

$$G'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{G(x+h) - G(x)}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{1}{h} \int_x^{x+h} f(t)\, dt.$$

By the Mean Value Theorem for Integrals, there exists $c_h$ between $x$ and $x+h$ such that $\int_x^{x+h} f(t)\, dt = f(c_h) \cdot h$. Therefore:

$$G'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} f(c_h).$$

Since $c_h \to x$ as $h \to 0$ and $f$ is continuous, $f(c_h) \to f(x)$. Therefore $G'(x) = f(x)$. $\square$

### FTC — Part 2: calculate the integral

<Teorema titulo="FTC Part 2 (evaluation formula)">

Let $f: [a, b] \to \mathbb{R}$ be **continuous** and $F$ any antiderivative of $f$. Then:

$$\int_a^b f(x)\, dx = F(b) - F(a).$$

Notation: $F(x)\big|_a^b := F(b) - F(a)$.

</Teorema>

**Proof of FTC Part 2.** By FTC Part 1, $G(x) = \int_a^x f(t)\, dt$ satisfies $G' = f$. Since $F' = f$ as well, $F - G$ has zero derivative on $(a, b)$, so $F(x) = G(x) + C$ for some constant $C$. Then:

$$F(b) - F(a) = [G(b) + C] - [G(a) + C] = G(b) - G(a) = \int_a^b f - 0 = \int_a^b f. \quad \square$$

### Leibniz rule (variable limits)

<Definicao titulo="Differentiation under the integral sign">

If $f$ is continuous and $u(x)$, $v(x)$ are differentiable, then:

$$\frac{d}{dx} \int_{u(x)}^{v(x)} f(t)\, dt = f(v(x))\, v'(x) - f(u(x))\, u'(x).$$

(Consequence of FTC Part 1 applied via the chain rule.)

</Definicao>

<Insight>

**Why is the FTC "fundamental"?** Before the FTC, computing $\int_a^b f$ required passing through the limit of Riemann sums — tedious for any function that is not constant. After the FTC, the problem reduces to: **find antiderivative + two evaluations**. This transformed calculus from an analysis tool into an engineering tool.

</Insight>

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

Imagine a staircase. Each step has a height. You want to know **how much you went up in total**. You don't need to measure each step — just look at **where you are now** and where you started, and find the difference.

The FTC says: to calculate the total accumulated change, you only need to know **where you arrived** and **where you departed**.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

The integral adds up little pieces to calculate area. Derivative measures the speed of change. They seem like very different things!

The FTC shows that **they are inverse operations**, just like addition and subtraction.

**Magic recipe:** to compute $\int_a^b f$, find a function $F$ whose derivative is $f$. The integral is just $F(b) - F(a)$.

Example: $\int_0^3 x^2\, dx$. Since $(x^3/3)' = x^2$, the integral is $3^3/3 - 0 = 9$.

Without FTC: you'd have to add infinitely many rectangles. With FTC: three calculations. That's why it's the most important theorem in calculus.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old teen">

The FTC has **two parts**:

**Part 1 (FTC1):** if you integrate a function and then differentiate the result, you get the original function back. Mathematically: $\frac{d}{dx}\int_a^x f(t)\, dt = f(x)$.

**Part 2 (FTC2):** to compute $\int_a^b f$, just find an antiderivative $F$ and compute $F(b) - F(a)$.

**How to apply FTC2 in practice:**

1. Identify the integrand $f(x)$.
2. Find $F$ such that $F'(x) = f(x)$ (use the antiderivative table).
3. Compute $F(b) - F(a)$.

**Complete example:**

$\int_1^4 (3x^2 + 2x)\, dx$.
- $F(x) = x^3 + x^2$.
- $F(4) - F(1) = (64 + 16) - (1 + 1) = 80 - 2 = 78$.

**Verification:** differentiate $F(x) = x^3 + x^2$. You get $3x^2 + 2x$. Check.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

The FTC is the glue that joins the two halves of calculus. In practical terms:

### FTC1 — differentiate integrals with variable limits

Frequent situation in ODEs and integral equations:

$$\frac{d}{dx}\int_0^{x^2} \sin(t)\, dt = \sin(x^2) \cdot 2x.$$

General rule (Leibniz): if the upper limit is $v(x)$ and the lower is $u(x)$:

$$\frac{d}{dx}\int_{u(x)}^{v(x)} f(t)\, dt = f(v(x)) v'(x) - f(u(x)) u'(x).$$

> "FTC Part 1 is the theorem that lets you differentiate integrals — fundamental in physics when a quantity is defined as an integral of another." — [APEX Calculus, §5.4](https://www.apexcalculus.com/)

### FTC2 — reduction to algebra

FTC Part 2 reduces the computation of $\int_a^b f$ to the algebraic problem of finding $F$ such that $F' = f$. The entire catalogue of integration techniques (substitution, parts, partial fractions) exists **solely** to solve this algebraic problem.

### Error analysis

The most common error is forgetting the sign of the difference: $F(b) - F(a)$, not $F(a) - F(b)$. Always write $\big|_a^b$ explicitly during the calculation.

### FTC in numerical analysis

When $f$ has no elementary antiderivative (e.g., $e^{-x^2}$), numerical methods are used:
- **Simpson's method**: accuracy $O(\Delta x^4)$, suitable for smooth functions.
- **Gauss-Kronrod quadrature**: adaptive, standard in `scipy.integrate.quad`.
- **Monte Carlo**: viable in high dimension where other methods scale poorly.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

### The FTC in historical context

Newton formulated the FTC around 1666 in the context of "methods of fluxions" (derivatives were "fluxions", integrals were "fluents"). Leibniz developed it independently in 1675-1676, with notation $\int$ and $dx$ that persists to this day.

The famous priority dispute (18th century) was largely fueled by nationalism — the Royal Society of London accused Leibniz of plagiarism; the court was presided over by Newton. Modern historiography recognizes independent parallel development.

### FTC in Lebesgue calculus

In Lebesgue theory, the FTC requires more careful hypotheses:

- **FTC1 (Lebesgue):** If $f \in L^1([a,b])$, then $G(x) = \int_a^x f$ is absolutely continuous and $G' = f$ a.e.
- **FTC2 (Lebesgue):** If $F$ is absolutely continuous, $F' = f$ a.e. and $\int_a^b f = F(b) - F(a)$.

Pathological example: the **Cantor function** (devil's staircase) is continuous, has derivative zero a.e., but $F(1) - F(0) = 1 \neq 0 = \int_0^1 F'$. This is because the Cantor function is NOT absolutely continuous — violating the hypothesis of Lebesgue's FTC Part 2.

### FTC in quantitative finance

In the Black-Scholes formula, the price derivation uses Itô's lemma — a stochastic version of the FTC. Formally: $dF(W_t) = F'(W_t)\, dW_t + \frac{1}{2}F''(W_t)\, dt$, where $W_t$ is Brownian motion. The extra $\frac{1}{2}F''$ is the "second-order correction" from the quadratic variation of Brownian motion — the FTC analogue in stochastic space.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practical application — Brazilian data">

### Fleet fuel consumption

A fleet of trucks has consumption rate $c(t) = 200 + 30t$ liters per hour (linear growth due to load accumulation). Total consumption in the first 8 hours:

$$C = \int_0^8 (200 + 30t)\, dt = \left[200t + 15t^2\right]_0^8 = 1600 + 960 = 2560\text{ liters.}$$

At R\$ 6.50/liter: total fuel cost = R\$ 16,640.

### Derivative of integral with variable limit

The amount of CO2 accumulated in an environment is $A(x) = \int_0^x e^{-t/10}\, dt$. The accumulation rate at instant $x$ is, by FTC Part 1:

$$A'(x) = e^{-x/10}.$$

There is no need to compute the integral — FTC Part 1 gives the derivative directly.

### Computational verification

```python
import sympy as sp
x = sp.Symbol('x')
F = sp.integrate(x**2 + 3*x, x)        # antiderivative
result = F.subs(x, 4) - F.subs(x, 1)  # FTC2
print(result)  # => 33
```

## Solved examples

<Exemplo numero="1" titulo="FTC2 applied to polynomial (basic)">

**Problem.** Calculate $\displaystyle\int_1^3 (x^2 + 2x - 1)\, dx$.

**Strategy.** Find antiderivative by FTC Part 2: $F(x)$ with $F'(x) = x^2 + 2x - 1$. Then $F(3) - F(1)$.

**Solution.**
$$F(x) = \frac{x^3}{3} + x^2 - x.$$

$$\int_1^3 (x^2 + 2x - 1)\, dx = F(3) - F(1) = \left(9 + 9 - 3\right) - \left(\frac{1}{3} + 1 - 1\right) = 15 - \frac{1}{3} = \frac{44}{3}.$$

**Verification.** $F'(x) = x^2 + 2x - 1$. Check.

**Source.** [APEX Calculus, §5.4, Example 5.4.2](https://www.apexcalculus.com/) — CC-BY-NC.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="2" titulo="FTC2 with trigonometric functions (basic)">

**Problem.** Calculate $\displaystyle\int_0^{\pi/2} (\cos x + 2\sin x)\, dx$.

**Strategy.** Antiderivatives of $\cos x$ and $\sin x$ from the table; evaluate at the endpoints.

**Solution.**
$$F(x) = \sin x - 2\cos x.$$

$$\int_0^{\pi/2} (\cos x + 2\sin x)\, dx = F(\pi/2) - F(0) = (1 - 0) - (0 - 2) = 1 + 2 = 3.$$

**Verification.** $F'(x) = \cos x + 2\sin x$. Check.

**Source.** [OpenStax Calculus Vol. 1, §5.3, Example 5.22](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/5-3-the-fundamental-theorem-of-calculus) — CC-BY-NC-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="3" titulo="FTC1 to differentiate integral (intermediate)">

**Problem.** Calculate $\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}\int_1^{x^3} \cos(t^2)\, dt$.

**Strategy.** FTC Part 1 combined with the chain rule: the upper limit is $v(x) = x^3$.

**Solution.**
$$\frac{d}{dx}\int_1^{x^3} \cos(t^2)\, dt = \cos((x^3)^2) \cdot (x^3)' = \cos(x^6) \cdot 3x^2.$$

**Verification.** The general Leibniz rule: $\frac{d}{dx}\int_a^{v(x)} f(t)\, dt = f(v(x)) \cdot v'(x)$. Applied with $f(t) = \cos(t^2)$, $v(x) = x^3$: $\cos(x^6) \cdot 3x^2$. Check.

**Source.** [APEX Calculus, §5.4, Example 5.4.5](https://www.apexcalculus.com/) — CC-BY-NC.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="4" titulo="Leibniz rule with both limits variable (intermediate)">

**Problem.** Calculate $\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}\int_x^{x^2} \frac{1}{t}\, dt$ for $x > 0$.

**Strategy.** General Leibniz rule: $f(v(x))v'(x) - f(u(x))u'(x)$ with $v(x) = x^2$, $u(x) = x$, $f(t) = 1/t$.

**Solution.**
$$\frac{d}{dx}\int_x^{x^2} \frac{1}{t}\, dt = \frac{1}{x^2} \cdot 2x - \frac{1}{x} \cdot 1 = \frac{2}{x} - \frac{1}{x} = \frac{1}{x}.$$

**Verification.** Alternatively: $\int_x^{x^2} \frac{1}{t}\, dt = \ln(x^2) - \ln x = 2\ln x - \ln x = \ln x$. Differentiating: $(\ln x)' = 1/x$. Check.

**Source.** [OpenStax Calculus Vol. 1, §5.3, Exercise 5.132](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/5-3-the-fundamental-theorem-of-calculus) — CC-BY-NC-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo numero="5" titulo="Area between curve and axis with sign change (challenge)">

**Problem.** Calculate the total geometric area between $y = x^2 - 4$ and the $x$-axis on $[-3, 3]$.

**Strategy.** The function $f(x) = x^2 - 4$ is negative on $(-2, 2)$ and positive elsewhere. Split the interval at zeros to sum areas with correct signs.

**Solution.**
Zeros: $x^2 = 4 \Rightarrow x = \pm 2$.

On $[-3, -2]$ and $[2, 3]$: $f \geq 0$. On $[-2, 2]$: $f \leq 0$.

$$A = \int_{-3}^{-2}(x^2-4)\, dx + \left|\int_{-2}^{2}(x^2-4)\, dx\right| + \int_2^3(x^2-4)\, dx.$$

$F(x) = x^3/3 - 4x$.

- $F(-2) - F(-3) = (-8/3 + 8) - (-9 + 12) = 16/3 - 3 = 7/3$.
- $F(2) - F(-2) = (8/3 - 8) - (-8/3 + 8) = -32/3$. Absolute value: $32/3$.
- $F(3) - F(2) = (9 - 12) - (8/3 - 8) = -3 + 16/3 = 7/3$.

Total area: $7/3 + 32/3 + 7/3 = 46/3$.

**Verification.** The integral $\int_{-3}^3 (x^2 - 4)\, dx = F(3) - F(-3) = (9-12) - (-9+12) = -3 - 3 = -6 \neq 46/3$. The definite integral differs from geometric area when $f$ changes sign.

**Source.** [Active Calculus, §4.4, Exercise 4.4.5](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-4-4-FTC.html) — CC-BY-NC-SA.

</Exemplo>
