### Fundamental identities

<Definicao titulo="Identities used in trigonometric integrals">

The following identities are the central tools:

- **Pythagorean:** $\sin^2 x + \cos^2 x = 1$; $1 + \tan^2 x = \sec^2 x$; $1 + \cot^2 x = \csc^2 x$.
- **Even power (half-angle):**

<Equation explicacao="Reduces sin² and cos² to double-angle functions — essential for even exponents." latex="\sin^2 x = \frac{1 - \cos 2x}{2}, \qquad \cos^2 x = \frac{1 + \cos 2x}{2}." />

- **Product-to-sum:** $\sin A \cos B = \frac{1}{2}[\sin(A+B) + \sin(A-B)]$.

</Definicao>

### Patterns for $\int \sin^n x \cos^m x\, dx$

<Definicao titulo="Strategy according to exponent parity">

| Situation | Strategy |
|---|---|
| $n$ odd | Reserve $\sin x\, dx$; convert $\sin^{n-1}$ via $\sin^2 = 1 - \cos^2$; substitute $u = \cos x$ |
| $m$ odd | Reserve $\cos x\, dx$; convert $\cos^{m-1}$ via $\cos^2 = 1 - \sin^2$; substitute $u = \sin x$ |
| Both even | Use half-angle repeatedly |

For $\int \tan^n x \sec^m x\, dx$:

| Situation | Strategy |
|---|---|
| $m$ even | Reserve $\sec^2 x\, dx$; convert via $\sec^2 = 1 + \tan^2$; substitute $u = \tan x$ |
| $n$ odd | Reserve $\sec x \tan x\, dx$; convert via $\tan^2 = \sec^2 - 1$; substitute $u = \sec x$ |

</Definicao>

> "The strategy for integrating a product of powers of sine and cosine depends on the parities of the exponents involved. When one of the exponents is odd, we 'peel off' one factor and use the Pythagorean identity to convert the remaining even power." — [OpenStax Calculus Vol. 2, §3.2](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-2/pages/3-2-trigonometric-integrals)

### Reduction formulas

<Teorema titulo="Reduction formula for sinⁿ">

$$\int \sin^n x\, dx = -\frac{\sin^{n-1}x \cos x}{n} + \frac{n-1}{n}\int \sin^{n-2}x\, dx.$$

Analogous formulas exist for $\cos^n$, $\tan^n$, $\sec^n$, $\csc^n$.

**Proof:** integration by parts with $u = \sin^{n-1}x$, $dv = \sin x\, dx$, plus the identity $\sin^2 = 1 - \cos^2$.

</Teorema>

<Insight>

**Weierstrass substitution** (universal): $t = \tan(x/2)$ converts any rational integral in $\sin x$, $\cos x$ into a rational integral in $t$. Via partial fractions, everything becomes elementary — but the result can be complicated. Use when other strategies fail.

</Insight>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

You know when you wear a ghost costume so nobody recognizes you, and then you take it off? Trig substitution is like that.

You have $\sqrt{1 - x^2}$ — hard to work with. You swap $x$ for $\sin\theta$ — disguise! The radical disappears and becomes $\cos\theta$. After computing with $\theta$, you remove the disguise and return to $x$.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

There's an identity you know: $\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1$.

This means that $\sqrt{1 - \sin^2\theta} = \cos\theta$ (assuming $\cos\theta \geq 0$).

So if you have $\sqrt{1 - x^2}$ and set $x = \sin\theta$, the radical disappears and becomes $\cos\theta$.

After integrating with respect to $\theta$, you use a triangle: if $\sin\theta = x$ and the hypotenuse is 1, the adjacent side is $\sqrt{1 - x^2}$. This gives you $\cos\theta$, $\tan\theta$, etc. in terms of $x$ — and you "undo" the substitution.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old teen">

### Powers of sine and cosine

**Case 1 (odd exponent):** $\int \sin^3 x\, dx$.

Reserve a $\sin x\, dx$, convert the rest: $\sin^2 x = 1 - \cos^2 x$.

$\int (1 - \cos^2 x)\sin x\, dx$. Substitute $u = \cos x$: $-\int(1 - u^2)\, du = -u + u^3/3 + C = -\cos x + \cos^3 x/3 + C$.

**Case 2 (even exponents):** $\int \sin^2 x\, dx$.

Use half-angle: $\int \frac{1 - \cos 2x}{2}\, dx = \frac{x}{2} - \frac{\sin 2x}{4} + C$.

### Trigonometric substitution

**Example:** $\int \sqrt{1 - x^2}\, dx$.

Substitute $x = \sin\theta$, $dx = \cos\theta\, d\theta$, $\sqrt{1-x^2} = \cos\theta$.

The integral becomes $\int \cos^2\theta\, d\theta = \theta/2 + \sin(2\theta)/4 + C$.

Return: $\theta = \arcsin x$, $\sin(2\theta) = 2\sin\theta\cos\theta = 2x\sqrt{1-x^2}$.

Result: $\dfrac{\arcsin x}{2} + \dfrac{x\sqrt{1-x^2}}{2} + C$.

**Reference triangle:** always draw one — it is the key to converting trig functions of $\theta$ back to $x$.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

### Mental table of basic integrals

- $\int \sin^2 x\, dx = (x - \sin x\cos x)/2 + C$.
- $\int \cos^2 x\, dx = (x + \sin x\cos x)/2 + C$.
- $\int \tan x\, dx = -\ln\lvert\cos x\rvert + C = \ln\lvert\sec x\rvert + C$.
- $\int \cot x\, dx = \ln\lvert\sin x\rvert + C$.
- $\int \sec x\, dx = \ln\lvert\sec x + \tan x\rvert + C$.
- $\int \csc x\, dx = -\ln\lvert\csc x + \cot x\rvert + C$.
- $\int \sec^2 x\, dx = \tan x + C$; $\int \csc^2 x\, dx = -\cot x + C$.
- $\int \sec x\tan x\, dx = \sec x + C$.

### Engineering connections

- **RMS of sinusoid:** $V_{rms} = V_0/\sqrt{2}$ — via $\int_0^T \sin^2(\omega t)\, dt = T/2$.
- **Cauchy distribution:** $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} 1/(\pi(1+x^2))\, dx = 1$ — substitute $x = \tan\theta$.
- **Arc length of ellipse:** $\int \sqrt{1 - k^2\sin^2\theta}\, d\theta$ — elliptic integral, no closed form.
- **Fourier coefficients:** $a_n = (1/\pi)\int_{-\pi}^\pi f(x)\cos(nx)\, dx$ — for $f$ trig, direct computation via identities.

### Euler and complex identity

Powers of $\sin x$ and $\cos x$ are computable via Euler: $\sin x = (e^{ix} - e^{-ix})/(2i)$, $\cos x = (e^{ix} + e^{-ix})/2$. Binomial expansion plus integration of complex exponentials, then real/imaginary part.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="40" titulo="For a senior professional">

### Weierstrass substitution (universal)

$t = \tan(x/2)$ transforms any rational function in $\sin x$ and $\cos x$ into rational in $t$:

$$\sin x = \frac{2t}{1+t^2}, \quad \cos x = \frac{1-t^2}{1+t^2}, \quad dx = \frac{2\,dt}{1+t^2}.$$

The resulting integral solves via partial fractions. However, the result may be more complicated than direct methods — use when other strategies fail or in CAS.

### Elliptic integrals

$\int \sqrt{1 - k^2\sin^2\theta}\, d\theta = E(k)$ and $\int d\theta/\sqrt{1 - k^2\sin^2\theta} = K(k)$ — Legendre's elliptic integrals. Not elementary for $k \neq 0$. Appear in arc length of ellipse, exact pendulum, gravitation.

### Fourier analysis

The coefficients $a_n, b_n$ of a generalized Fourier series are computed via trig integrals. The smoothness of $f$ controls the decay of $|a_n|$, $|b_n|$ — Fourier analysis connects trig integrals to approximation theory.

### Differential geometry

Arc length $L = \int \sqrt{1 + (f')^2}\, dx$ involves trig substitution when $f' = \tan\theta$. Curvature $\kappa = |f''|/(1 + (f')^2)^{3/2}$ appears integrated when computing arc length in natural coordinates. Trig substitution in multiple variables: spherical and cylindrical coordinates are trig Jacobians.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practical application">

**Recipe for trig integrals:**

1. Look at the exponents of $\sin$ and $\cos$.
2. Any odd? Reserve one copy of that factor with its $dx$, convert the rest.
3. Both even? Use $\sin^2 = (1 - \cos 2x)/2$ and $\cos^2 = (1 + \cos 2x)/2$.
4. Radical $\sqrt{a^2 \pm x^2}$ or $\sqrt{x^2 - a^2}$? Apply trig substitution from the table.
5. After trig substitution, draw the reference triangle to return to $x$.
6. Check by differentiating.

### Area of a circle — canonical validation

$A = 4\int_0^r \sqrt{r^2 - x^2}\, dx$. Substitute $x = r\sin\theta$: integral becomes $4r^2\int_0^{\pi/2}\cos^2\theta\, d\theta = 4r^2\cdot\pi/4 = \pi r^2$.

### RMS of sinusoid — circuits

Voltage $v(t) = V_0\sin(\omega t)$ over one period $T = 2\pi/\omega$:

$$V_{rms} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{T}\int_0^T V_0^2\sin^2(\omega t)\, dt} = V_0\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}} = \frac{V_0}{\sqrt{2}} \approx 0.707\, V_0.$$

### Common errors

- Forget the Jacobian $dx \to a\cos\theta\, d\theta$ (or $a\sec^2\theta\, d\theta$) — causes missing factor $a$.
- Fail to return from $\theta$ to $x$ using the triangle.
- Confuse $\sin^2 x$ (power) with $\sin(2x)$ (double angle).
- In $x = a\sec\theta$, forget the correct branch of $\theta$.

</Porta>

## Worked examples

<Exemplo titulo="Odd power of sine (basic)" numero="1">

**Problem.** Compute $\int \sin^3 x\, dx$.

**Strategy.** Odd exponent: reserve $\sin x\, dx$, convert $\sin^2 = 1 - \cos^2$, substitute $u = \cos x$.

**Solution.**

$\int \sin^3 x\, dx = \int \sin^2 x \cdot \sin x\, dx = \int (1 - \cos^2 x)\sin x\, dx$.

Substitute $u = \cos x$, $du = -\sin x\, dx$:

$-\int (1 - u^2)\, du = -u + u^3/3 + C = -\cos x + \frac{\cos^3 x}{3} + C$.

**Check.** Differentiate: $\sin x - \cos^2 x\sin x = \sin x(1 - \cos^2 x) = \sin^3 x$. Correct.

**Source.** [APEX Calculus §6.3, Example 6.3.1](https://www.apexcalculus.com/apex-calculus-v5.pdf) — CC-BY-NC.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Even exponents — half-angle (basic)" numero="2">

**Problem.** Compute $\int \sin^2 x \cos^2 x\, dx$.

**Strategy.** Both even: use $\sin x\cos x = \sin(2x)/2$ and then half-angle.

**Solution.**

$\sin^2 x\cos^2 x = (\sin x\cos x)^2 = \frac{\sin^2(2x)}{4} = \frac{1}{4}\cdot\frac{1 - \cos 4x}{2} = \frac{1 - \cos 4x}{8}$.

$$\int \sin^2 x\cos^2 x\, dx = \frac{x}{8} - \frac{\sin 4x}{32} + C.$$

**Check.** Differentiate: $1/8 - \cos(4x)/8 = (1 - \cos 4x)/8 = \sin^2(2x)/4 = \sin^2 x\cos^2 x$. Correct.

**Source.** [OpenStax Calculus Vol. 2, §3.2, Example 3.12](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-2/pages/3-2-trigonometric-integrals) — CC-BY-NC-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Substitution x = a sin θ for √(a² − x²) (intermediate)" numero="3">

**Problem.** Compute $\int \sqrt{4 - x^2}\, dx$.

**Strategy.** Radical $\sqrt{a^2 - x^2}$ with $a = 2$: substitute $x = 2\sin\theta$.

**Solution.**

$dx = 2\cos\theta\, d\theta$; $\sqrt{4 - x^2} = \sqrt{4 - 4\sin^2\theta} = 2\cos\theta$ (with $\theta \in [-\pi/2, \pi/2]$).

$\int 2\cos\theta \cdot 2\cos\theta\, d\theta = 4\int\cos^2\theta\, d\theta = 4\cdot\frac{\theta + \sin\theta\cos\theta}{2} = 2\theta + 2\sin\theta\cos\theta + C$.

Reference triangle ($\sin\theta = x/2$, hypotenuse 2, adjacent side $\sqrt{4-x^2}$):

$\theta = \arcsin(x/2)$; $\cos\theta = \sqrt{4-x^2}/2$.

Result: $2\arcsin(x/2) + 2\cdot\frac{x}{2}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{4-x^2}}{2} + C = 2\arcsin(x/2) + \frac{x\sqrt{4-x^2}}{2} + C$.

**Check.** Differentiate (chain rule): $\frac{2}{\sqrt{4-x^2}} + \frac{\sqrt{4-x^2} - x^2/\sqrt{4-x^2}}{2} = \frac{2}{\sqrt{4-x^2}} + \frac{4 - 2x^2}{2\sqrt{4-x^2}} = \sqrt{4-x^2}$. Correct.

**Source.** [APEX Calculus §6.4, Example 6.4.2](https://www.apexcalculus.com/apex-calculus-v5.pdf) — CC-BY-NC.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Substitution x = a tan θ for √(a² + x²) (intermediate)" numero="4">

**Problem.** Compute $\int \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1 + x^2}}\, dx$.

**Strategy.** Radical $\sqrt{a^2 + x^2}$ with $a = 1$: substitute $x = \tan\theta$.

**Solution.**

$dx = \sec^2\theta\, d\theta$; $\sqrt{1+x^2} = \sec\theta$.

$\int \frac{\sec^2\theta}{\sec\theta}\, d\theta = \int \sec\theta\, d\theta = \ln\lvert\sec\theta + \tan\theta\rvert + C$.

Triangle ($\tan\theta = x$, hypotenuse $\sqrt{1+x^2}$): $\sec\theta = \sqrt{1+x^2}$, $\tan\theta = x$.

Result: $\ln\lvert x + \sqrt{1+x^2}\rvert + C$ (equivalent to $\text{arcsinh}(x) + C$).

**Check.** Differentiate $\ln(x + \sqrt{1+x^2})$: $\frac{1 + x/\sqrt{1+x^2}}{x + \sqrt{1+x^2}} = \frac{(\sqrt{1+x^2}+x)/\sqrt{1+x^2}}{x+\sqrt{1+x^2}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1+x^2}}$. Correct.

**Source.** [OpenStax Calculus Vol. 2, §3.3, Example 3.15](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-2/pages/3-3-trigonometric-substitution) — CC-BY-NC-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Reduction formula and application (challenge)" numero="5">

**Problem.** Derive the reduction formula $\int \sin^n x\, dx = -\frac{\sin^{n-1}x\cos x}{n} + \frac{n-1}{n}\int\sin^{n-2}x\, dx$ and use it to compute $\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^4 x\, dx$.

**Strategy.** Derive via integration by parts; apply for $n = 4$ and $n = 2$.

**Solution.**

**Derivation:** $u = \sin^{n-1}x$, $dv = \sin x\, dx$. $du = (n-1)\sin^{n-2}x\cos x\, dx$, $v = -\cos x$.

$\int\sin^n x\, dx = -\sin^{n-1}x\cos x + (n-1)\int\cos^2 x\sin^{n-2}x\, dx$.

Substitute $\cos^2 x = 1 - \sin^2 x$:

$(n-1)\int(1 - \sin^2 x)\sin^{n-2}x\, dx = (n-1)\int\sin^{n-2}x\, dx - (n-1)\int\sin^n x\, dx$.

Rearrange: $n\int\sin^n x\, dx = -\sin^{n-1}x\cos x + (n-1)\int\sin^{n-2}x\, dx$. Divide by $n$.

**Application:** $n = 4$: $\int\sin^4 x\, dx = -\frac{\sin^3 x\cos x}{4} + \frac{3}{4}\int\sin^2 x\, dx$.

$\int\sin^2 x\, dx = x/2 - \sin(2x)/4$.

Evaluating on $[0, \pi/2]$: $\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^4 x\, dx = 0 + \frac{3}{4}\left[\frac{\pi}{4} - 0\right] = \frac{3\pi}{16}$.

**Check.** Wallis formula: $\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^4 x\, dx = \frac{3\cdot 1}{4\cdot 2}\cdot\frac{\pi}{2} = \frac{3\pi}{16}$. Confirms.

**Source.** [APEX Calculus §6.3, Theorem 6.3.1](https://www.apexcalculus.com/apex-calculus-v5.pdf) — CC-BY-NC.

</Exemplo>
