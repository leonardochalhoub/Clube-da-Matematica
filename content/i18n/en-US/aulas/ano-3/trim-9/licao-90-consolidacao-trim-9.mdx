### Conceptual map of integral calculus

<Definicao titulo="Antiderivative and indefinite integral">

Let $f : I \to \mathbb{R}$ be continuous on an interval $I$. A function $F : I \to \mathbb{R}$ is an **antiderivative** of $f$ on $I$ if $F'(x) = f(x)$ for all $x \in I$.

> "If $F$ is an antiderivative of $f$, then the complete family of antiderivatives is $F(x) + C$, where $C$ is an arbitrary constant. We write $\int f(x)\,dx = F(x) + C$." — [Active Calculus §4.1, p. 215](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-4-4-FTC.html)

The **indefinite integral** denotes this family: $\int f(x)\,dx = F(x) + C$.

</Definicao>

<Definicao titulo="Definite integral and Riemann sum">

For $f$ bounded on $[a, b]$, partition $a = x_0 < x_1 < \cdots < x_n = b$ and sample points $x_i^* \in [x_{i-1}, x_i]$:

$$S_n = \sum_{i=1}^n f(x_i^*)\,\Delta x_i$$

> "The definite integral of $f(x)$ from $a$ to $b$ is the limit of Riemann sums as the partition norm tends to zero, provided that limit exists and is the same for any choice of sample points." — [OpenStax Calculus Vol. 2 §1.2](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-2/pages/1-2-the-definite-integral)

</Definicao>

<Teorema titulo="Fundamental Theorem of Calculus (FTC1 and FTC2)">

Let $f$ be continuous on $[a, b]$.

**FTC1.** The function $G(x) = \displaystyle\int_a^x f(t)\,dt$ is differentiable on $(a, b)$ and $G'(x) = f(x)$.

**FTC2.** If $F$ is any antiderivative of $f$ on $[a, b]$, then

$$\int_a^b f(x)\,dx = F(b) - F(a).$$

**Proof of FTC2 from FTC1.** Since $G'(x) = f(x) = F'(x)$, we have $G(x) = F(x) + C$ for some constant $C$. Evaluating at $x = a$: $G(a) = 0$ implies $C = -F(a)$. Thus $G(b) = F(b) - F(a)$.

</Teorema>

### Quick table of fundamental antiderivatives

<Definicao titulo="Table of antiderivatives">

| $f(x)$ | $\int f(x)\,dx$ |
|---|---|
| $x^n$ ($n \neq -1$) | $\dfrac{x^{n+1}}{n+1} + C$ |
| $x^{-1}$ | $\ln\lvert x\rvert + C$ |
| $e^x$ | $e^x + C$ |
| $\sin x$ | $-\cos x + C$ |
| $\cos x$ | $\sin x + C$ |
| $\sec^2 x$ | $\tan x + C$ |
| $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}$ | $\arcsin x + C$ |
| $\dfrac{1}{1+x^2}$ | $\arctan x + C$ |

</Definicao>

<Insight>

**Why does the constant $+C$ matter?** In the indefinite integral, $C$ represents an entire family of functions — vertical parallel curves. In initial-value problems, $C$ is determined by problem data. In the definite integral ($F(b) - F(a)$), the constants cancel, so $C$ disappears. Understanding this prevents the classic error of "forgetting $+C$" in definite integrals.

</Insight>

### Canonical applications

<Definicao titulo="Area and volume by integration">

**Area between curves.** If $f(x) \geq g(x)$ on $[a, b]$:

$$A = \int_a^b [f(x) - g(x)]\,dx$$

**Volume by disks/washers.** Revolution of $y = f(x)$ about the $x$-axis:

$$V = \pi \int_a^b [f(x)]^2\,dx$$

For washer (region between $f$ and $g$, $f \geq g \geq 0$):

$$V = \pi \int_a^b \bigl([f(x)]^2 - [g(x)]^2\bigr)\,dx$$

**Volume by cylindrical shells.** Revolution of $y = f(x) \geq 0$ about the $y$-axis:

$$V = 2\pi \int_a^b x f(x)\,dx$$

> "The method of cylindrical shells is preferable when the region rotates about an axis parallel to the axis of integration." — [APEX Calculus §7.3, p. 359](https://www.apexcalculus.com/)

</Definicao>

Imagine you want to know **how much paint** colored a drawing. You look line by line: how much did you color in this line? How much in that one? You add up all the lines, one by one.

That's what the **integral** does: it sums infinitely many tiny pieces to get the total. And the **Fundamental Theorem** is the shortcut that avoids summing infinity: you just look at the beginning and the end.

You learned to do this with **different types** of paint (different functions) and found that the same trick always works.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

This term you learned to **undo the derivative**. The derivative "sped up" the function; the integral "slows back down."

But there are tricks for when the function is complicated:

- **Substitution**: you swap the variable for a simpler one, like changing clothes to go to the beach.
- **By parts**: when two types of function multiply, you separate the work.
- **Partial fractions**: break a big fraction into smaller pieces.
- **Trig sub**: when you have a strange square root, swap for an angle.

And in the end you learned to **measure areas** and **calculate volumes** of solids of revolution with all of this.

</Porta>

### How to tackle a new integral — the method

1. **Look at the integrand** carefully.
2. **Recognize the pattern** using the decision tree from the formal door.
3. **Apply the corresponding technique**.
4. If stuck, try algebraic manipulation (complete the square, trig identity).
5. **Verify** by differentiating the result.

### Examples of quick pattern recognition

- $\int x e^{x^2}\,dx$: the derivative of $x^2$ is $2x$ — sub $u = x^2$.
- $\int x e^x\,dx$: product polynomial $\times$ exponential without chain — by parts.
- $\int \dfrac{1}{x^2 - 1}\,dx$: rational with linear factors — partial fractions.
- $\int \sin^3 x\,dx$: odd power of sine — sub $u = \cos x$.
- $\int \sqrt{1 - x^2}\,dx$: radical of type $\sqrt{a^2 - x^2}$ — trig sub $x = \sin\theta$.

### What will be tested

- **Indefinite integral**: technique + constant $+C$ + verification.
- **Definite integral**: FTC2 with correct limits; if using sub, swap limits too.
- **Area between curves**: find intersection, determine which curve is "on top".
- **Volume**: choose the right method (disks vs. shells) based on axis of revolution.

<Insight>

**When substituting in a definite integral, swap the limits in $u$, not in $x$.** Classic error: compute $u$ at $a$ and $b$ but forget to rewrite the limits in terms of $u$. If $u = g(x)$, the new limits are $g(a)$ and $g(b)$ — you never need to go back to $x$.

</Insight>

### Why manual techniques matter in the 21st century

99% of integrals in industry are solved by CAS (SymPy, Mathematica, MATLAB). But the engineer needs to be able to do it by hand because:

1. **Sanity check**: when the CAS returns an unexpected result, you need to know if it's correct.
2. **Weak formulation in FEM**: integration by parts appears *explicitly by hand* when deriving finite element equations.
3. **Transforms**: Laplace $\mathcal{L}\{f\} = \int_0^\infty f(t)e^{-st}\,dt$ and Fourier are defined as integrals — understanding analytic structure matters.
4. **Taylor series with error**: the Lagrange remainder formula is an integral.

### Connections to the next module

- **ODEs**: every separable ODE is solved by integrating both sides. First-order linear ODE requires integrating factor + integral.
- **Laplace transform**: it is by parts and partials encapsulated in notation.
- **Continuous probability**: $\mathbb{E}[X] = \int x f(x)\,dx$, variance, distribution function — all are integrals.
- **Quantitative finance**: present value of continuous flow $= \int_0^T C(t)e^{-rt}\,dt$; Black-Scholes derives from a Gaussian integral.

### Production best practices

```python
import sympy as sp
x = sp.Symbol('x')

# Symbolic — checks your manual answer
F = sp.integrate(x * sp.exp(x), x)
print(F)  # (x - 1)*exp(x)

# Numeric — when there is no closed form
from scipy.integrate import quad
val, err = quad(lambda t: sp.exp(-t**2), 0, 1)
```

### The hierarchy of integrals you didn't see in high school

The **Riemann integral** you learned is sufficient for piecewise-continuous functions. But mathematics moved beyond:

- **Lebesgue (1902)**: integrates much more discontinuous functions; the Dirichlet function ($\mathbf{1}_\mathbb{Q}$) is Lebesgue-integrable (value 0) but not Riemann. Every Riemann-integrable is Lebesgue-integrable, but not vice versa.
- **Henstock-Kurzweil**: generalizes both Riemann and Lebesgue to certain unbounded-variation functions.
- **Stochastic integration (Itô)**: $\int_0^T f(t)\,dW_t$ where $W_t$ is Brownian motion — the integrand doesn't have bounded variation; Riemann's definition collapses.

### Feynman and differentiation under the integral sign

> "I had tried for several days to compute a certain integral ... None of the standard methods worked. Then I remembered a method I'd learned from an old calculus book — differentiating under the integral sign." — R. Feynman

The technique: if $I(\alpha) = \int_a^b f(x, \alpha)\,dx$, then $I'(\alpha) = \int_a^b \partial_\alpha f\,dx$ (under convergence hypotheses). Powerful for integrals like $\int_0^\infty e^{-x^2}\cos(2\alpha x)\,dx$.

### Gabriel's trumpet paradox

The surface generated by $y = 1/x$, $x \in [1, \infty)$, rotated about the $x$-axis has:
- **Volume** finite: $V = \pi \int_1^\infty x^{-2}\,dx = \pi$.
- **Surface area** infinite: $A = 2\pi \int_1^\infty \frac{1}{x}\sqrt{1 + x^{-4}}\,dx = \infty$.

Philosophical conclusion: you can *fill* the trumpet with $\pi$ liters of paint, but cannot *paint* it — infinite area. The apparent contradiction resolves by noting that the paint layer has thickness, and minimum thickness eventually covers the surface.

### In quantitative finance

Option pricing via Black-Scholes (Lesson 119) reduces to a Gaussian integral. The price of a European call is:

$$C = S_0 N(d_1) - K e^{-rT} N(d_2), \quad N(x) = \int_{-\infty}^x \frac{e^{-t^2/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\,dt$$

Myron Scholes and Robert Merton received the [Nobel Prize in Economics 1997](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/1997/summary/) for this formula. Fischer Black died before the award. The Gaussian integral you learned here is literally the backbone of the model.

### Calculating interest with continuous flow

A Brazilian company invests in a project generating continuous cash flow $C(t) = 50,000 + 8,000t$ reais per year ($t$ in years), at discount rate $r = 12\%$ per year. The present value on $[0, 5]$:

$$\text{PV} = \int_0^5 C(t)\,e^{-0.12t}\,dt = \int_0^5 (50,000 + 8,000t)\,e^{-0.12t}\,dt$$

Solved by parts: $\int t e^{-rt}\,dt = -\frac{t}{r}e^{-rt} - \frac{1}{r^2}e^{-rt} + C$.

### Volume of a cylindrical-conical silo

A Brazilian silo has cross-section defined by $y = \sqrt{x}$ on $[0, 4]$ m, rotated about the $y$-axis. Volume by shell method:

$$V = 2\pi \int_0^4 x \cdot \sqrt{x}\,dx = 2\pi \int_0^4 x^{3/2}\,dx = 2\pi \left[\frac{2}{5}x^{5/2}\right]_0^4 = 2\pi \cdot \frac{2}{5} \cdot 32 = \frac{128\pi}{5} \approx 80.4 \text{ m}^3$$

### Common errors — checklist before submitting

1. Forgot $+C$ in indefinite integral.
2. Did not swap limits when using sub in definite.
3. Confused LIATE order in parts (Log, Inverse trig, Alg, Trig, Exp).
4. Did not divide $P/Q$ when $\deg P \geq \deg Q$ before partial fractions.
5. Sign flipped in trig sub when going from $\theta$ back to $x$.
6. Forgot the $2\pi$ factor in volume by shells.
7. Did not sketch the region before computing area.

## Worked examples

<Exemplo titulo="FTC2 applied with substitution in limits (application)" numero="1">

**Problem:** Compute $\displaystyle\int_0^1 2x e^{x^2}\,dx$.

**Strategy:** The integrand has the form $f(g(x)) \cdot g'(x)$ with $g(x) = x^2$ and $g'(x) = 2x$. Apply substitution $u = x^2$ and swap integration limits directly in $u$.

**Solution:**

Let $u = x^2$, then $du = 2x\,dx$.

Limits: $x = 0 \Rightarrow u = 0$; $x = 1 \Rightarrow u = 1$.

$$\int_0^1 2x e^{x^2}\,dx = \int_0^1 e^u\,du = \bigl[e^u\bigr]_0^1 = e^1 - e^0 = e - 1$$

**Verification:** The antiderivative of $2x e^{x^2}$ is $e^{x^2}$ (differentiating: $(e^{x^2})' = e^{x^2} \cdot 2x$ ✓). Evaluating: $e^{1^2} - e^{0^2} = e - 1 \approx 1.718$.

**Source.** [Active Calculus §5.3, Activity 5.3.2](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-5-3-substitution.html) — CC-BY-NC-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Iterated integration by parts — xe^x (intermediate)" numero="2">

**Problem:** Compute $\displaystyle\int x^2 e^x\,dx$.

**Strategy:** Product of polynomial by exponential, no chain relation between them. Use integration by parts with LIATE: $u = x^2$ (algebraic) and $dv = e^x\,dx$ (exponential). Will need to apply parts twice.

**Solution:**

**1st application:** $u = x^2$, $du = 2x\,dx$; $dv = e^x\,dx$, $v = e^x$.

$$\int x^2 e^x\,dx = x^2 e^x - \int 2x e^x\,dx$$

**2nd application** (for $\int 2x e^x\,dx$): $u = 2x$, $du = 2\,dx$; $dv = e^x\,dx$, $v = e^x$.

$$\int 2x e^x\,dx = 2x e^x - \int 2 e^x\,dx = 2x e^x - 2e^x$$

**Final result:**

$$\int x^2 e^x\,dx = x^2 e^x - (2x e^x - 2e^x) + C = (x^2 - 2x + 2)e^x + C$$

**Verification:** $\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[(x^2 - 2x + 2)e^x\bigr] = (2x - 2)e^x + (x^2 - 2x + 2)e^x = x^2 e^x$ ✓.

**Source.** [APEX Calculus §8.1, Example 8.1.4](https://www.apexcalculus.com/) — CC-BY-NC.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Partial fractions — denominator with two linear factors (intermediate)" numero="3">

**Problem:** Compute $\displaystyle\int \frac{1}{x^2 - 1}\,dx$.

**Strategy:** The integrand is rational with $\deg(\text{num}) < \deg(\text{den})$. Factor the denominator: $x^2 - 1 = (x-1)(x+1)$. Decompose into partial fractions.

**Solution:**

$$\frac{1}{(x-1)(x+1)} = \frac{A}{x-1} + \frac{B}{x+1}$$

Multiplying both sides by $(x-1)(x+1)$: $1 = A(x+1) + B(x-1)$.

$x = 1$: $1 = 2A \Rightarrow A = 1/2$.
$x = -1$: $1 = -2B \Rightarrow B = -1/2$.

$$\int \frac{1}{x^2 - 1}\,dx = \int \frac{1/2}{x-1}\,dx + \int \frac{-1/2}{x+1}\,dx = \frac{1}{2}\ln\lvert x - 1\rvert - \frac{1}{2}\ln\lvert x + 1\rvert + C$$

$$= \frac{1}{2}\ln\left\lvert\frac{x-1}{x+1}\right\rvert + C$$

**Verification:** Differentiating: $\frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{(x+1) - (x-1)}{(x-1)(x+1)} = \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{2}{x^2-1} = \frac{1}{x^2-1}$ ✓.

**Source.** [OpenStax Calculus Vol. 2 §3.4, Example 3.4.1](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-2/pages/3-4-partial-fractions) — CC-BY-NC-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Area between parabola and line (application)" numero="4">

**Problem:** Compute the area of the region bounded by $f(x) = x^2$ and $g(x) = x + 2$.

**Strategy:** Find the intersections (points where $f = g$), determine which curve is above in each subinterval, and integrate the difference.

**Solution:**

**Intersections:** $x^2 = x + 2 \Rightarrow x^2 - x - 2 = 0 \Rightarrow (x-2)(x+1) = 0 \Rightarrow x = -1$ or $x = 2$.

**Which is "on top"?** For $x \in (-1, 2)$: $g(0) = 2 > f(0) = 0$, so $g \geq f$.

$$A = \int_{-1}^2 [g(x) - f(x)]\,dx = \int_{-1}^2 (x + 2 - x^2)\,dx$$

$$= \left[\frac{x^2}{2} + 2x - \frac{x^3}{3}\right]_{-1}^2 = \left(2 + 4 - \frac{8}{3}\right) - \left(\frac{1}{2} - 2 + \frac{1}{3}\right) = \frac{10}{3} - \left(-\frac{7}{6}\right) = \frac{10}{3} + \frac{7}{6} = \frac{27}{6} = \frac{9}{2}$$

**Verification:** $9/2 = 4.5$ square units. The parabola and line form a tear-drop-shaped region — reasonable result for an interval of length 3.

**Source.** [Active Calculus §6.1, Activity 6.1.2](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-6-1-area.html) — CC-BY-NC-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Volume of revolution by shell method (advanced)" numero="5">

**Problem:** Compute the volume of the solid generated by rotating the region bounded by $y = \ln x$, $y = 0$ and $x = e$ about the $y$-axis.

**Strategy:** The region is to the right of the $y$-axis. Rotation about the $y$-axis with integration in $x$ suggests the cylindrical shell method: $V = 2\pi \int_a^b x f(x)\,dx$.

**Solution:**

The solid goes from $x = 1$ (since $\ln 1 = 0$) to $x = e$.

$$V = 2\pi \int_1^e x \ln x\,dx$$

Apply integration by parts: $u = \ln x$, $dv = x\,dx$; $du = dx/x$, $v = x^2/2$.

$$\int x \ln x\,dx = \frac{x^2}{2}\ln x - \int \frac{x^2}{2} \cdot \frac{1}{x}\,dx = \frac{x^2}{2}\ln x - \frac{x^2}{4} + C$$

Evaluating:

$$\int_1^e x \ln x\,dx = \left[\frac{x^2}{2}\ln x - \frac{x^2}{4}\right]_1^e = \left(\frac{e^2}{2} - \frac{e^2}{4}\right) - \left(0 - \frac{1}{4}\right) = \frac{e^2}{4} + \frac{1}{4} = \frac{e^2 + 1}{4}$$

$$V = 2\pi \cdot \frac{e^2 + 1}{4} = \frac{\pi(e^2 + 1)}{2} \approx \frac{\pi \cdot 8.389}{2} \approx 13.18$$

**Verification:** For $x \in [1, e]$, the shell has radius $x$ and height $\ln x$. The result has correct dimension (unit³). $\approx 13.18$ is reasonable for this region.

**Source.** [OpenStax Calculus Vol. 2 §2.3, Example 2.3.4](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-2/pages/2-3-volumes-of-revolution-cylindrical-shells) — CC-BY-NC-SA.

</Exemplo>
