### Antiderivative and indefinite integral

<Definicao titulo="Antiderivative">

Let $f: I \to \mathbb{R}$ defined on interval $I$. A function $F: I \to \mathbb{R}$ is called an **antiderivative** (or primitive) of $f$ on $I$ if $F'(x) = f(x)$ for all $x \in I$.

The **indefinite integral** of $f$ is the family of all antiderivatives, and is written:

$$\int f(x)\, dx = F(x) + C, \quad C \in \mathbb{R}$$

</Definicao>

> "If $F$ is an antiderivative of $f$ on an interval $I$, then the most general antiderivative of $f$ on $I$ is $F(x) + C$, where $C$ is an arbitrary constant." — [OpenStax Calculus Vol. 1, §4.10](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/4-10-antiderivatives)

<Definicao titulo="Why the constant C?">

If $F' = f$, then $(F + C)' = F' + 0 = f$ for any $C \in \mathbb{R}$. Thus every vertical translation of $F$ is also an antiderivative.

**Corollary (consequence of MVT):** on interval $I$, if $F$ and $G$ are both antiderivatives of $f$, then $F - G$ is constant on $I$. Therefore the antiderivative is unique **up to a constant**.

</Definicao>

### Linearity of antidifferentiation

<Teorema titulo="Linearity of the indefinite integral">

For functions $f$, $g$ with antiderivatives on interval $I$, and scalars $\alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{R}$:

$$\int (\alpha f(x) + \beta g(x))\, dx = \alpha \int f(x)\, dx + \beta \int g(x)\, dx$$

</Teorema>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

You know how to **add** and how to **subtract**? Subtraction undoes addition: if you gained 3 candies and then lost 3, you're back where you started.

**Derivative** is like counting candies you gain per minute. **Antiderivative** goes the other way: if I tell you how many you gained per minute, you find out how many you had.

But there's a catch: I don't know if you started with 0 candies or 100. That's why we write "+ C" — C keeps that secret.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

The car's speedometer tells you the **speed** at each moment. The **antiderivative** goes backwards: given the speed, find the **position**.

If a car goes at $v(t) = 2t$ meters per second, where is it after $t$ seconds? The antiderivative of $2t$ is $t^2$. So position $= t^2 + C$, where $C$ is the initial position you need to know separately.

**Trick for polynomials**: $\int x^n$ becomes $x^{n+1}/(n+1)$. Add 1 to the exponent and divide by the new exponent.

Examples: $\int x^2\, dx = x^3/3$. $\int x^5\, dx = x^6/6$. Always add $+ C$ at the end.

</Porta>
