### Definition and justification via Riemann

<Definicao titulo="Area between two curves">

Let $f$ and $g$ be continuous on $[a, b]$ with $f(x) \geq g(x)$ for all $x \in [a, b]$. The **area of the region** $R = \{(x, y) : a \leq x \leq b,\ g(x) \leq y \leq f(x)\}$ is

<Equation explicacao="Fundamental formula for area between curves — integration of upper minus lower." latex="A(R) = \int_a^b [f(x) - g(x)]\, dx." />

**Justification:** for each $x_i^*$ in a subinterval of width $\Delta x$, a rectangle of height $[f(x_i^*) - g(x_i^*)]$ and width $\Delta x$ approximates the local area. The Riemann sum $\sum_i [f(x_i^*) - g(x_i^*)]\Delta x$ converges to the integral as $n \to \infty$.

</Definicao>

> "The area of the region between the graphs of $f$ and $g$ is found by integrating the difference $f - g$ over the interval, provided $f \geq g$ throughout. If the graphs cross, break the interval at the crossing points." — [Active Calculus §6.1](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-6-1-area.html)

### Integration with respect to $y$

<Definicao titulo="Area with integration in y">

If the region is bounded by curves $x = h(y)$ (right) and $x = k(y)$ (left), with $h(y) \geq k(y)$ for $y \in [c, d]$:

<Equation explicacao="Formula for area when curves are more naturally expressed as x in terms of y." latex="A = \int_c^d [h(y) - k(y)]\, dy." />

Use integration with respect to $y$ when: the functions $x(y)$ are simpler than $y(x)$, or the region has boundaries requiring multiple subdivisions in $x$ but only one in $y$.

</Definicao>

### General procedure

<Definicao titulo="Algorithm for calculating area between curves">

1. **Sketch** the curves. Without a sketch, error is nearly certain.
2. **Determine intersection points**: solve $f(x) = g(x)$.
3. **Identify which curve is above** in each subinterval (by checking a test point).
4. **Divide the interval** at crossing points if the order changes.
5. **Integrate** $|f - g|$ in each subinterval and sum.
6. The area is **always positive** — if $\int(f-g)$ comes out negative in any section, you swapped $f$ and $g$.

</Definicao>

> "Finding the area of a region between two curves requires careful attention to the sign of the integrand. Always determine which function is greater on the interval of integration." — [APEX Calculus §7.1](https://www.apexcalculus.com/apex-calculus-v5.pdf)

<Insight>

**Connection to the FTC:** $A = \int_a^b(f - g)\, dx = F(b) - G(b) - F(a) + G(a)$ where $F' = f$, $G' = g$. The area between curves is the difference of the accumulated values of $f$ and $g$ over the interval. In economics: area between marginal revenue and marginal cost = accumulated net profit.

</Insight>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

Imagine you paint a sandwich between two pieces of bread — one on top, one on bottom. The "thickness" of the sandwich changes along its length.

The painted area is the sum of all the thicknesses — from one side to the other.

Integrating is adding up all those thin slices. Area between curves is the total thickness of the sandwich.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

The integral $\int_a^b f$ gives you the area between the curve $f$ and the $x$-axis.

If you want the area between **two curves**, just subtract one from the other: $\int_a^b (f - g)$ with $f$ on top.

But there's a catch: if the curves **cross**, the one on top changes. You need to divide the interval at the crossing points and calculate separately in each part.

And sometimes it's easier to integrate with respect to $y$ — instead of vertical slices, you use horizontal slices.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old">

The formula is $A = \int_a^b (f - g)\, dx$ with $f \geq g$ on $[a, b]$.

### Recipe in 5 steps

1. **Sketch** the graph. Without it you don't know who is on top.
2. **Find intersections**: solve $f(x) = g(x)$ — these are your integration limits.
3. **Check the order**: $f$ upper, $g$ lower. If it switches, divide the interval.
4. **Integrate** $f - g$ in each subinterval.
5. **Add** the partial areas.

### Complete example

Area between $y = \sin x$ and $y = \cos x$ on $[0, \pi/2]$.

Intersection: $\sin x = \cos x \Rightarrow x = \pi/4$.

On $[0, \pi/4]$: $\cos x \geq \sin x$. On $[\pi/4, \pi/2]$: $\sin x \geq \cos x$.

$A = \int_0^{\pi/4}(\cos x - \sin x)\, dx + \int_{\pi/4}^{\pi/2}(\sin x - \cos x)\, dx$

$= [\sin x + \cos x]_0^{\pi/4} + [-\cos x - \sin x]_{\pi/4}^{\pi/2} = (\sqrt{2} - 1) + (\sqrt{2} - 1) = 2\sqrt{2} - 2$.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

Area between curves appears in **economics**, **thermodynamics**, **statistics** and **control**.

### Engineering applications

**Consumer surplus:** $CS = \int_0^{Q^*}[D(q) - p^*]\, dq$ — area between demand curve and equilibrium price. Measures consumer gain by paying less than they would be willing to.

**Producer surplus:** $PS = \int_0^{Q^*}[p^* - S(q)]\, dq$ — area between price and supply curve.

**Work in a thermodynamic cycle:** in a $P$-$V$ diagram, net work per cycle = area enclosed by the cycle. Carnot, Otto, Diesel: all calculated via area between curves.

**Total variation between distributions:** $TV(f, g) = \frac{1}{2}\int|f - g|\, dx$ — total variation distance between two probability densities.

### Choice of integration axis

Prefer **integration with respect to $y$** when:
- Curves express themselves more naturally as $x(y)$.
- The region has multiple subdivisions in $x$ but only one in $y$ (e.g., horizontal parabola).
- The number of intersections in $y$ is smaller.

### Numerical

```python
from scipy.integrate import quad
import numpy as np

def area_between(f, g, a, b):
    return quad(lambda x: abs(f(x) - g(x)), a, b)[0]
```

### Generalization: area in $\mathbb{R}^2$ via double integrals and Green

The area of a planar region $R$ is $A(R) = \iint_R 1\, dA$. Via Fubini, this reduces to iterated integrals — which motivates the area-between-curves formula as a special case.

By Green: $A = \frac{1}{2}\oint_{\partial R}(x\, dy - y\, dx)$. For parametric curves $\gamma(t) = (x(t), y(t))$, this gives $A = \frac{1}{2}\int_\alpha^\beta (x\dot{y} - y\dot{x})\, dt$ — the generalized shoelace formula.

### Integral geometry and isoperimetry

The isoperimetric problem: among all closed curves of perimeter $L$, which maximizes area? Answer: the circle. Isoperimetric inequality: $A \leq L^2/(4\pi)$. Proved via Fourier analysis of curves (Hurwitz, 1901) — Fourier coefficients bound the area in terms of perimeter.

### Calculus of variations

The area of a surface with given boundary is a functional $A[\gamma] = \iint 1\, dA$. Minimizers are **minimal surfaces** (Laplace equation for mean curvature). Plateau's problem: given a wire frame, which minimal surface does it span?

### Polygons — shoelace

For a polygon with vertices $(x_1, y_1), \ldots, (x_n, y_n)$:

$$A = \frac{1}{2}\left\lvert\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i y_{i+1} - x_{i+1} y_i)\right\rvert.$$

(Indices mod $n$.) Direct application of Green — no integral needed.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="pratica" titulo="Practical application">

**Foolproof recipe:**

1. Draw ALWAYS — without a sketch error is guaranteed.
2. Find intersections by solving $f = g$.
3. Identify upper/lower in each section.
4. Compute $\int(\text{sup} - \text{inf})$ per section.
5. Add — the area is positive.

### Consumer surplus — example with actual data

Demand curve $D(q) = 100 - q$ (dollars per unit), equilibrium price $p^* = 60$ at $Q^* = 40$ units:

$$CS = \int_0^{40}[(100 - q) - 60]\, dq = \int_0^{40}(40 - q)\, dq = \left[40q - \frac{q^2}{2}\right]_0^{40} = 1600 - 800 = 800.$$

The consumer "gained" the equivalent of 800 currency units in surplus.

### Accumulated profit

Marginal revenue $R'(t) = 100$ $/day, marginal cost $C'(t) = 50 + 5t$ $/day. Net profit over the first $T$ days:

$$L(T) = \int_0^T [R'(t) - C'(t)]\, dt = \int_0^T (50 - 5t)\, dt.$$

Crosses zero at $t = 10$: from then on, cost exceeds revenue. Area on $[0, 10]$ = maximum accumulated profit = 250 dollars.

### Common mistakes

- Not sketching and integrating in the wrong order (negative result).
- Forgetting to break where curves cross.
- Computing $\int(f - g)$ without absolute value when $g > f$ in a section.

</Porta>

## Worked examples

<Exemplo titulo="Classic area x² and x (basic)" numero="1">

**Problem.** Compute the area between $y = x$ and $y = x^2$ on the interval $[0, 1]$.

**Strategy.** Find intersection, check which is above, integrate the difference.

**Solution.**

Intersection: $x = x^2 \Rightarrow x(x-1) = 0 \Rightarrow x = 0$ or $x = 1$.

On $(0, 1)$: $x > x^2$ (check: $0.5 > 0.25$). So $f = x$, $g = x^2$.

$$A = \int_0^1 (x - x^2)\, dx = \left[\frac{x^2}{2} - \frac{x^3}{3}\right]_0^1 = \frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{3} = \frac{1}{6}.$$

**Verification.** $1/6 \approx 0.167$. The sketch shows the region covering roughly $17\%$ of the square $[0,1]^2$. Plausible.

**Source.** [Active Calculus §6.1, Activity 6.1.1](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-6-1-area.html) — CC-BY-NC-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Curves with crossing — sin and cos (intermediate)" numero="2">

**Problem.** Compute the area between $y = \sin x$ and $y = \cos x$ on $[0, \pi/2]$.

**Strategy.** The curves cross at $x = \pi/4$. Divide and integrate each section separately.

**Solution.**

Crossing: $\sin x = \cos x \Rightarrow \tan x = 1 \Rightarrow x = \pi/4$.

On $[0, \pi/4]$: $\cos x \geq \sin x$. On $[\pi/4, \pi/2]$: $\sin x \geq \cos x$.

$A = \int_0^{\pi/4}(\cos x - \sin x)\, dx + \int_{\pi/4}^{\pi/2}(\sin x - \cos x)\, dx$.

$= [\sin x + \cos x]_0^{\pi/4} + [-\cos x - \sin x]_{\pi/4}^{\pi/2}$

$= (\sqrt{2} - 1) + ((-0 - 1) - (-\sqrt{2}/2 - \sqrt{2}/2))$

$= (\sqrt{2} - 1) + (-1 + \sqrt{2}) = 2\sqrt{2} - 2 \approx 0.83$.

**Verification.** By symmetry of the problem, the two parts are equal: each is $\sqrt{2} - 1$. Sum $= 2(\sqrt{2} - 1)$. Consistent.

**Source.** [APEX Calculus §7.1, Example 7.1.3](https://www.apexcalculus.com/apex-calculus-v5.pdf) — CC-BY-NC.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Integration in y — horizontal parabola (intermediate)" numero="3">

**Problem.** Compute the area of the region bounded by $x = y^2$ and $x = y + 2$.

**Strategy.** The curves are more natural in $y$. Find intersection in $y$, integrate $h(y) - k(y)$.

**Solution.**

Intersection: $y^2 = y + 2 \Rightarrow y^2 - y - 2 = 0 \Rightarrow (y-2)(y+1) = 0 \Rightarrow y = -1$ or $y = 2$.

On $[-1, 2]$: $y + 2 \geq y^2$ (check at $y = 0$: $2 \geq 0$). So $h = y + 2$, $k = y^2$.

$$A = \int_{-1}^2 [(y + 2) - y^2]\, dy = \left[\frac{y^2}{2} + 2y - \frac{y^3}{3}\right]_{-1}^2.$$

At $y = 2$: $2 + 4 - 8/3 = 6 - 8/3 = 10/3$.

At $y = -1$: $1/2 - 2 + 1/3 = -7/6$.

$A = 10/3 - (-7/6) = 20/6 + 7/6 = 27/6 = 9/2$.

**Verification.** Integration in $x$ would require two parts (the parabola $x = y^2$ has two "branches" in $x$) — more tedious. Integration in $y$ was more efficient.

**Source.** [OpenStax Calculus Vol. 2, §2.1, Example 2.5](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-2/pages/2-1-areas-between-curves) — CC-BY-NC-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Consumer surplus (modeling)" numero="4">

**Problem.** Demand curve $D(q) = 100 - q$, equilibrium price $p^* = 60$, quantity $Q^* = 40$. Compute consumer surplus.

**Strategy.** $CS = \int_0^{Q^*} [D(q) - p^*]\, dq$ — area between demand curve and horizontal price line.

**Solution.**

$$CS = \int_0^{40} [(100 - q) - 60]\, dq = \int_0^{40}(40 - q)\, dq = \left[40q - \frac{q^2}{2}\right]_0^{40} = 1600 - 800 = 800.$$

**Verification.** Geometrically, the region is a triangle of base $40$ and height $D(0) - p^* = 40$. Area = $(40 \cdot 40)/2 = 800$. Confirms.

**Source.** [Active Calculus §6.1, Exercise 6.1.5](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-6-1-area.html) — CC-BY-NC-SA.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Area with subdivision — cubic polynomial (challenge)" numero="5">

**Problem.** Compute the total area of the region between $y = x^3 - 4x$ and the $x$-axis on $[-2, 2]$.

**Strategy.** Identify where $x^3 - 4x$ changes sign; divide the interval; use $|f|$ in each part.

**Solution.**

Zeros: $x^3 - 4x = x(x^2 - 4) = 0 \Rightarrow x = -2, 0, 2$.

On $[-2, 0]$: $f(x) = x^3 - 4x \geq 0$ (check $f(-1) = -1 + 4 = 3 > 0$).

On $[0, 2]$: $f(x) \leq 0$ (check $f(1) = 1 - 4 = -3 < 0$).

$A = \int_{-2}^0 (x^3 - 4x)\, dx + \int_0^2 [0 - (x^3 - 4x)]\, dx$.

For the 1st part: $\left[\frac{x^4}{4} - 2x^2\right]_{-2}^0 = 0 - (4 - 8) = 4$.

For the 2nd part: $\left[-\frac{x^4}{4} + 2x^2\right]_0^2 = (-4 + 8) - 0 = 4$.

$A = 4 + 4 = 8$.

**Verification.** By odd symmetry of $f$, the two areas are equal. Total $= 8$.

**Source.** [APEX Calculus §7.1, Example 7.1.4](https://www.apexcalculus.com/apex-calculus-v5.pdf) — CC-BY-NC.

</Exemplo>
