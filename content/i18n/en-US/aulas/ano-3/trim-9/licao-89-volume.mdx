### Cavalieri's principle and slicing

<Definicao titulo="Volume by cross-sections">

Let $S$ be a solid such that, for each $x \in [a, b]$, the area of the cross-section perpendicular to the $x$-axis is $A(x)$, with $A$ integrable on $[a, b]$. The **volume** of $S$ is:

<Equation explicacao="Volume by slicing: continuous sum of slices of thickness dx and area A(x)." latex="V = \int_a^b A(x)\, dx" />

**Justification:** Partition $a = x_0 < x_1 < \cdots < x_n = b$. Each slice $[x_{i-1}, x_i]$ has approximate volume $A(x_i^*) \Delta x_i$. The Riemann sum converges to the integral as $\|\mathcal{P}\| \to 0$.

</Definicao>

> "If two solids have the same height and equal cross-sections at each level, then the two solids have the same volume." — Cavalieri's Principle (17th century), formalized in [Active Calculus §6.2](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-6-2-volume.html)

### Disk method

<Definicao titulo="Disks — revolution around the x-axis">

Let $f$ be continuous and non-negative on $[a, b]$. The solid generated by revolving the region below $y = f(x)$ around the $x$-axis has volume:

<Equation explicacao="Each cross-section is a disk of radius f(x); area = pi r^2." latex="V = \pi \int_a^b [f(x)]^2\, dx" />

</Definicao>

### Washer method

<Definicao titulo="Washers — revolution of region between two curves">

Let $f, g$ be continuous on $[a, b]$ with $f(x) \geq g(x) \geq 0$. The solid generated by revolving the region between $y = f(x)$ and $y = g(x)$ around the $x$-axis has:

<Equation explicacao="Washer with outer radius R = f(x) and inner radius r = g(x). Washer area = pi(R^2 - r^2)." latex="V = \pi \int_a^b \bigl([f(x)]^2 - [g(x)]^2\bigr)\, dx" />

</Definicao>

### Cylindrical shell method

<Definicao titulo="Cylindrical shells — revolution around the y-axis">

Let $f$ be continuous and non-negative on $[a, b]$, with $0 \leq a < b$. The solid generated by revolving around the **y-axis** has:

<Equation explicacao="Each vertical strip of width dx generates a cylindrical shell of radius x, height f(x), thickness dx. Volume of shell = 2 pi x f(x) dx." latex="V = 2\pi \int_a^b x\, f(x)\, dx" />

</Definicao>

> "The shell method can be thought of as integrating along the axis parallel to the axis of rotation." — [APEX Calculus §7.3](https://www.apexcalculus.com/)

### Shifted axis of revolution

For revolution around $y = c$ (instead of the $x$-axis): substitute $f(x) \mapsto f(x) - c$ (or $|f(x) - c|$). For revolution around $x = c$ with shells: substitute $x \mapsto |x - c|$ in the radius role.

<Teorema titulo="Pappus's Theorem (volume)">

If a planar region of area $A$ is rotated around an external axis (in the same plane), the volume generated is:

<Equation explicacao="d is the distance from the centroid of the region to the axis of revolution." latex="V = 2\pi\,\bar{d}\, A" />

where $\bar{d}$ is the distance from the region's centroid to the axis.

</Teorema>

<Insight>

**Disk as a special case of washer.** When $g(x) = 0$ (no hole), the washer formula reduces to the disk formula: $\pi(R^2 - 0^2) = \pi R^2$. The three methods are instances of the general principle $V = \int A(x)\,dx$ — only the expression for $A(x)$ changes.

</Insight>

<Porta nivel="5" titulo="For a 5-year-old">

Imagine an orange. You cut it into **thin slices**. Each slice is a round circle. To find the volume of the whole orange, you **measure the area of each slice** and add them all up.

If the slices are very thin — super thin — the sum becomes an integral.

That's exactly what we do: we cut the solid into infinitely many thin slices and add up the areas. The result is the volume.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="10" titulo="For a 10-year-old">

A **solid of revolution** is what you get when you spin a curve around an axis — like a woodworking lathe that transforms a piece of wood into a round piece.

To calculate the volume, imagine cutting the solid into **thin slices**, each with thickness $\Delta x$.

- Each slice is a **disk** of radius $f(x)$.
- Area of disk: $\pi r^2 = \pi [f(x)]^2$.
- Volume of slice: area × thickness = $\pi [f(x)]^2 \, \Delta x$.

When $\Delta x$ goes to zero, the sum becomes an integral: $V = \pi \int_a^b [f(x)]^2\, dx$.

If the region has a **hole** in the middle (between two curves), each slice is a **washer** — subtract the area of the hole.

And when the axis of revolution is vertical, we use **cylindrical shells**: imagine peeling an onion in layers. Each layer is a thin cylinder that you "unroll" into a rectangle. Add up all the layers.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="15" titulo="For a 15-year-old teenager">

### Disk method

Revolution of $y = f(x) \geq 0$ around the $x$-axis, for $x \in [a, b]$:

$$V = \pi \int_a^b [f(x)]^2\, dx$$

**Quick example:** $y = \sqrt{x}$ on $[0, 4]$ rotated around the $x$-axis.

$$V = \pi \int_0^4 x\, dx = \pi \left[\frac{x^2}{2}\right]_0^4 = \pi \cdot 8 = 8\pi \approx 25.1 \text{ u}^3$$

### Washer method

Two radii, outer radius $R(x)$ and inner radius $r(x)$:

$$V = \pi \int_a^b \bigl([R(x)]^2 - [r(x)]^2\bigr)\, dx$$

**Quick example:** Region between $y = x$ and $y = x^2$ on $[0, 1]$, rotated around the $x$-axis.

$R(x) = x$, $r(x) = x^2$:

$$V = \pi \int_0^1 (x^2 - x^4)\, dx = \pi \left[\frac{x^3}{3} - \frac{x^5}{5}\right]_0^1 = \pi\!\left(\frac{1}{3} - \frac{1}{5}\right) = \frac{2\pi}{15}$$

### Cylindrical shells

Revolution around the **y-axis**, with integration in $x$:

$$V = 2\pi \int_a^b x\, f(x)\, dx$$

**Quick example:** $y = x^2$ on $[0, 2]$, rotated around the $y$-axis.

$$V = 2\pi \int_0^2 x \cdot x^2\, dx = 2\pi \int_0^2 x^3\, dx = 2\pi \cdot 4 = 8\pi$$

### Golden rule

Look at the **axis of revolution**:
- Parallel to the integration axis → **disk/washer**.
- Perpendicular to the integration axis → **shell**.

**Always sketch** the region before setting up the integral.

</Porta>

<Porta nivel="25" titulo="For an engineering student">

### General formulation

All methods are instances of $V = \int_a^b A(x)\,dx$. What varies is how $A(x)$ is determined by the geometry:

| Method | $A(x)$ | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| Disk | $\pi [f(x)]^2$ | Revolution around parallel axis, no hole |
| Washer | $\pi([R(x)]^2 - [r(x)]^2)$ | Revolution between two curves, parallel axis |
| Shell | $2\pi x f(x)$ (lateral area element) | Revolution around perpendicular axis |

### Shifted axis

For revolution around $y = c$: outer radius = $f(x) - c$, inner radius = $g(x) - c$ (assuming $f > g > c$). For axis $x = c$ with shells: radius = $|x - c|$.

### Pappus (quick verification)

$V = 2\pi \bar{d} A$. Useful for checking: compute the centroid $(\bar x, \bar y)$ of the planar region and the distance to the axis. For a torus with outer radius $R$ and tube $r$: $A = \pi r^2$, $\bar d = R$ → $V = 2\pi^2 R r^2$.

### Forms derived from slicing

- **Pyramid** with base $A_0$ and height $h$: $V = \int_0^h A_0(1 - y/h)^2\, dy = A_0 h/3$.
- **Solid with square cross-sections**: $V = \int_a^b [s(x)]^2\, dx$.
- **Sphere** of radius $r$: $V = \pi \int_{-r}^r (r^2 - x^2)\, dx = 4\pi r^3/3$.

### Connection to mechanics

- **Mass**: $m = \int \rho\, dV$ (volumetric density $\rho$).
- **Center of mass**: $\bar z = \frac{1}{m}\int z\,\rho\,dV$.
- **Moment of inertia** around the axis: $I = \int r_\perp^2\,\rho\,dV$.
- **Pumping work**: $W = \int_0^H \rho g A(y)(H - y)\,dy$ for tank with cross-section $A(y)$ up to height $H$.

### Numerical

```python
from scipy.integrate import quad
import numpy as np

def volume_disk(f, a, b):
    V, _ = quad(lambda x: np.pi * f(x)**2, a, b)
    return V

# Unit sphere: verification
V_sphere = volume_disk(lambda x: np.sqrt(1 - x**2), -1, 1)
# V_sphere ≈ 4.189 ≈ 4π/3 $\checkmark$
```

### Measure and abstract volume

In $\mathbb{R}^n$, the volume of $K$ is the Lebesgue measure $\lambda^n(K) = \int_K 1\,d\mathbf{x}$. Slicing is **Fubini's Theorem**: under measurability hypotheses, $\int_{\mathbb{R}^n} f\,d\lambda^n = \int_{\mathbb{R}} \left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}} f(x, y)\,d\lambda^{n-1}(y)\right) d\lambda(x)$.

### Cavalieri as an application of Fubini

Cavalieri's Principle is immediate: if $A(x) = \lambda^{n-1}(K_x)$ (measure of the slice) is the same for two solids $K$ and $K'$, then $\lambda^n(K) = \int A(x)\,dx = \lambda^n(K')$.

### Curvilinear coordinates

Change of variables with Jacobian $|J|$:
- Cylindrical $(r, \theta, z)$: $dV = r\,dr\,d\theta\,dz$.
- Spherical $(\rho, \phi, \theta)$: $dV = \rho^2 \sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta$.

Cylindrical shells are the integration in $r$ of the cylindrical coordinate marginalizing over $\theta$ and $z$.

### Differential forms and orientation

The volume of a solid $\Omega$ with smooth boundary $\partial\Omega$ can be expressed as $V = \frac{1}{3}\oint_{\partial\Omega} \mathbf{r} \cdot d\mathbf{S}$ (Divergence Theorem with $\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{r}/3$, $\operatorname{div}\mathbf{F} = 1$). In polyhedron volume computation (CAD, FEM), this is more efficient than volumetric integration.

### Generalized Pappus

In differential geometry, Pappus's Theorem generalizes to fibrations $\pi: E \to B$: the volume of $E$ is the integral over $B$ of the volumes of the fibers $\pi^{-1}(b)$ (coarea formula). Applications in sub-Riemannian geometry and tomography.

### Volumes in high dimension and Monte Carlo

For large $n$, $\lambda^n(B^n) = \pi^{n/2}/\Gamma(n/2 + 1) \to 0$ (the unit sphere in high dimension is almost empty). Volumes of domains in $\mathbb{R}^n$ computed by Monte Carlo: $V \approx V_{\text{box}} \cdot (\text{points inside})/(\text{total})$. Applications in quantitative finance (integrals of option prices over asset baskets, high-dimensional integration).

### Engineering applications

**Storage tank.** A horizontal cylindrical tank of radius $R$ and length $L$ has liquid volume up to height $h$:
$$V(h) = L \int_{-R}^{h-R} 2\sqrt{R^2 - y^2}\, dy$$
The integral becomes a circular sector area — useful for fuel level gauges in truck tanks.

**Engine piston.** The displacement (volume swept by piston) is $V_{\text{cyl}} = \pi R^2 \cdot \text{stroke}$, confirmed via disk with $f(x) = R$ constant.

**Torus (tire, donut, inner tube).** Ring with circular cross-section of radius $r$ centered at distance $R$ from the axis. Pappus: $V = 2\pi R \cdot \pi r^2 = 2\pi^2 R r^2$.

### Common errors

- Confusing **outer** with **inner** radius in the washer method (subtract, not add).
- Using disk when the axis is perpendicular (the integral goes in $y$, not $x$).
- Forgetting the factor $2\pi$ in cylindrical shell.
- Shifted axis: the radius is $|f(x) - c|$, not $f(x)$.

### Usage recipe

1. **Sketch** the 2D region with care — mark intersections.
2. Identify the **axis of revolution**.
3. Choose method: parallel → disk/washer; perpendicular → shell.
4. Express **radii** (or radius + height) as a function of the integration variable.
5. **Integrate** and verify with Pappus or a known formula (sphere, cone, cylinder).

### Pocket formulas

| Solid | Formula |
|---|---|
| Sphere radius $r$ | $4\pi r^3 / 3$ |
| Cone radius $R$, height $h$ | $\pi R^2 h / 3$ |
| Cylinder radius $R$, height $h$ | $\pi R^2 h$ |
| Torus radii $R, r$ | $2\pi^2 R r^2$ |

## Worked examples

<Exemplo titulo="Volume of sphere by disks (application)" numero="1">

**Problem:** Derive the volume formula of a sphere of radius $r$ using the disk method.

**Strategy:** A sphere of radius $r$ is generated by revolving the semicircle $y = \sqrt{r^2 - x^2}$, for $x \in [-r, r]$, around the $x$-axis. Each cross-section is a disk of radius $\sqrt{r^2 - x^2}$.

**Solution:**

Applying the disk formula:

$$V = \pi \int_{-r}^{r} \bigl(\sqrt{r^2 - x^2}\bigr)^2\, dx = \pi \int_{-r}^{r} (r^2 - x^2)\, dx$$

$$= \pi \left[r^2 x - \frac{x^3}{3}\right]_{-r}^{r} = \pi \left[\left(r^3 - \frac{r^3}{3}\right) - \left(-r^3 + \frac{r^3}{3}\right)\right]$$

$$= \pi \cdot 2\left(r^3 - \frac{r^3}{3}\right) = \pi \cdot 2 \cdot \frac{2r^3}{3} = \frac{4\pi r^3}{3}$$

**Verification:** For $r = 1$: $V = 4\pi/3 \approx 4.19$ u³. Classical result confirmed.

**Source.** [OpenStax Calculus Volume 2 §2.2, Example 2.5](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-2/pages/2-2-determining-volumes-by-slicing) — CC-BY-NC-SA license.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Volume by washers — region between two parabolas (intermediate)" numero="2">

**Problem:** Calculate the volume of the solid generated by revolving, around the $x$-axis, the region bounded by $y = x$ and $y = x^2$, with $x \in [0, 1]$.

**Strategy:** The two curves intersect at $x = 0$ and $x = 1$. On $[0, 1]$, we have $x \geq x^2$, so $R(x) = x$ (outer radius) and $r(x) = x^2$ (inner radius). Washer method.

**Solution:**

$$V = \pi \int_0^1 \bigl([x]^2 - [x^2]^2\bigr)\, dx = \pi \int_0^1 (x^2 - x^4)\, dx$$

$$= \pi \left[\frac{x^3}{3} - \frac{x^5}{5}\right]_0^1 = \pi\!\left(\frac{1}{3} - \frac{1}{5}\right) = \pi \cdot \frac{2}{15} = \frac{2\pi}{15}$$

**Verification:** The volume should be less than the cone of radius 1 and height 1 ($V_{\text{cone}} = \pi/3 \approx 1.047$). Indeed, $2\pi/15 \approx 0.419 < \pi/3$. Plausible — the region between $y=x$ and $y=x^2$ is smaller than the complete triangle.

**Source.** [Active Calculus §6.2, Activity 6.2.2](https://activecalculus.org/single/sec-6-2-volume.html) — CC-BY-NC-SA license.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Volume by shells — comparison with washers (advanced)" numero="3">

**Problem:** Use cylindrical shells to calculate the volume generated by revolving the region between $y = x^2$ and $y = x$, on $[0, 1]$, around the **y-axis**. Compare with the washer result if you integrated in $y$.

**Strategy:** The axis of revolution is $y$ (vertical) and the natural variable is $x$: shell method. Each vertical strip of width $dx$ generates a shell of radius $x$, height $f(x) - g(x) = x - x^2$.

**Solution:**

$$V = 2\pi \int_0^1 x(x - x^2)\, dx = 2\pi \int_0^1 (x^2 - x^3)\, dx$$

$$= 2\pi \left[\frac{x^3}{3} - \frac{x^4}{4}\right]_0^1 = 2\pi\!\left(\frac{1}{3} - \frac{1}{4}\right) = 2\pi \cdot \frac{1}{12} = \frac{\pi}{6}$$

**Cross-check by washers in $y$:** The curves in $y$: from $y = x^2$ we have $x = \sqrt{y}$; from $y = x$ we have $x = y$. Outer radius $R(y) = \sqrt{y}$, inner radius $r(y) = y$:

$$V = \pi \int_0^1 (y - y^2)\, dy = \pi\!\left[\frac{y^2}{2} - \frac{y^3}{3}\right]_0^1 = \pi\!\left(\frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{3}\right) = \frac{\pi}{6} \checkmark$$

**Source.** [APEX Calculus §7.3, Example 7.3.2](https://www.apexcalculus.com/) — CC-BY-NC license.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Volume of cone by disks — formula derivation (demonstration)" numero="4">

**Problem:** Derive the formula $V = \pi R^2 h/3$ of a right circular cone of radius $R$ and height $h$.

**Strategy:** The cone is generated by revolving the line $y = (R/h)x$ around the $x$-axis, for $x \in [0, h]$. Each cross-section is a disk of radius $(R/h)x$.

**Solution:**

$$V = \pi \int_0^h \left(\frac{R}{h} x\right)^2 dx = \pi \frac{R^2}{h^2} \int_0^h x^2\, dx = \pi \frac{R^2}{h^2} \cdot \frac{h^3}{3} = \frac{\pi R^2 h}{3}$$

**Verification:** For $R = h = 1$: $V = \pi/3 \approx 1.047$ u³. Pappus's Theorem confirms: the centroid of the right triangle with base $R$ and height $h$ is at $\bar{x} = h/3$ from the vertex. Volume of the shell: $2\pi \cdot (R/2) \cdot (Rh/2) = \pi R^2 h / 2 \neq \pi R^2 h/3$ — here Pappus applies to the solid, not the triangle. Direct derivation by disks is cleanest.

**Source.** [OpenStax Calculus Volume 2 §2.2, Example 2.4](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-2/pages/2-2-determining-volumes-by-slicing) — CC-BY-NC-SA license.

</Exemplo>

<Exemplo titulo="Pumping work — spherical tank (modeling)" numero="5">

**Problem:** A spherical tank of radius 3 m is full of water (density $\rho = 1000$ kg/m³, $g = 9.8$ m/s²). Calculate the work required to pump all the water to the top of the tank.

**Strategy:** Place the sphere's center at the origin. The top is at $y = 3$. A slice of thickness $dy$ at height $y$ must be lifted $(3 - y)$ meters. The circular cross-section at height $y$ has radius $\sqrt{9 - y^2}$, hence area $\pi(9 - y^2)$.

**Solution:**

$$W = \int_{-3}^{3} \rho g \cdot \pi(9 - y^2) \cdot (3 - y)\, dy$$

$$= 1000 \cdot 9.8 \cdot \pi \int_{-3}^{3} (9 - y^2)(3 - y)\, dy$$

Expanding the product:

$$(9 - y^2)(3 - y) = 27 - 9y - 3y^2 + y^3$$

$$\int_{-3}^{3} (27 - 9y - 3y^2 + y^3)\, dy = \left[27y - \frac{9y^2}{2} - y^3 + \frac{y^4}{4}\right]_{-3}^{3}$$

The odd terms ($-9y$ and $y^3$) cancel by symmetry. Even terms:

$$= 2\left(27 \cdot 3 - 3^3\right) = 2(81 - 27) = 108$$

$$W = 9800\pi \cdot 108 \approx 3.33 \times 10^6 \text{ J} = 3.33 \text{ MJ}$$

**Verification:** Order of magnitude: moving $\approx 113$ t of water $\approx 1.5$ m (centroid 1.5 m from the top) gives $113,000 \times 9.8 \times 1.5 \approx 1.66$ MJ. Factor 2 expected because the actual centroid (by symmetry, at $y = 0$, distance 3 from the top) gives 3.32 MJ. Confirms.

**Source.** [OpenStax Calculus Volume 2 §6.5, Example 6.24](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-2/pages/6-5-physical-applications) — CC-BY-NC-SA license.

</Exemplo>
